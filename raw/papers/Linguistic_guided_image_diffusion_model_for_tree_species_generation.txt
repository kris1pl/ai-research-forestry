A B S T R A C T
A key challenge in applying AI to forest monitoring is tree species identification, hindered by complex visual 
variations and limited data of tree canopies. Moreover, AI-assisted recognition frameworks that effectively 
integrate large language models with human knowledge bases in forestry have not yet been fully established. 
Against this background, this study introduces linguistic guided image diffusion model (LGINet), a framework 
integrating textually guided image generation with tree species detection to enhance forest applications through 
technological synergy. The framework comprises three key methodological components: (1) A module designed 
to leverage detailed textual descriptions specific to forest environments, incorporating elements such as species 
traits, spatial layout, and phenological features. It generates text embeddings that are semantically aligned with 
forest knowledge. (2) An innovative diffusion-based framework integrates an improved U-Net architecture with 
Markov chain theory and textual semantic embeddings, fusing noisy images with forest-specific linguistic se­
mantics to generate highly realistic aerial tree images. (3) An optimized detection pipeline utilizing the generated 
images, based on an enhanced YOLOv11 architecture with a context-aware feature extractor and adaptive anchor 
scaling, enabling efficient, large-scale tree crown detection and species identification. Experimental evaluations 
validate the efficacy of the proposed framework, with the image generation module achieving a Structural 
Similarity Index (SSIM) of 0.94 and a Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) score of 6.42, demonstrating exceptional 
fidelity in synthetic output quality. Furthermore, the detection pipeline attained a mean Average Precision 
(mAP50) of 0.868 in species identification tasks, consistently outperforming all baseline models across evalua­
tion metrics. The integrated system improves capabilities of forest species detection by leveraging linguistic- 
driven synthesis to generate high-fidelity forest imagery and tree species identification.
1. Introduction
Forest ecosystems are vital for global biodiversity, carbon seques­
tration, and climate regulation, yet they face escalating threats from 
deforestation, climate change, invasive species, and habitat fragmenta­
tion [1]. Precise monitoring and species identification are essential for 
advancing ecological conservation and sustainable forest management. 
The complexity and diversity of forest ecosystems present significant 
challenges for deep learning applications in forest, particularly due to 
the scarcity of training samples. At present, multi-modal data fusion, 
which integrates diverse data sources such as imagery, text semantics, 
and ecological metadata to provide a more comprehensive representa­
tion of specialized forest questions, is emerging as a key technical trend 
and strategy for enhancing the accuracy and robustness of forest 
monitoring systems.
Although multimodal remote sensing approaches are increasingly 
explored for forest monitoring, most current methods for tree species 
identification remain reliant on discriminative features from RGB or

[3], Mask R-CNN [4], and Segment Anything [5] have been tailored to 
address ecological complexity. However, their effectiveness is con­
strained by a heavy dependence on labor-intensive annotations and 
performance degradation under challenging conditions like illumination 
changes, phenological shifts, and environmental variability. While some 
studies have attempted to enhance robustness through multimodal 
fusion (e.g., combining hyperspectral with aerial imagery [6] or inte­
grating multispectral and aerial data [7]) , spectral-based approaches 
still face inherent limitations. These include the "different canopy, same 
spectrum" and "same canopy, different spectrum" phenomena caused by 
inter-species similarity and intra-species variation, which complicate 
accurate identification. A more fundamental limitation is the underuti­
lization of linguistic information for auxiliary recognition, which re­
mains largely unaddressed.
Nevertheless, existing methods have yet to fully leverage large-scale 
semantic repositories or exploit the rich descriptive capabilities inherent 
in human language. Large Language Models (LLMs) [8] as core models 
in natural language processing, are inherently capable of understanding 
and generating natural language text by learning from vast text corpora. 
By modeling probability distributions over word sequences, LLMs cap­
ture linguistic patterns, structures, and semantic information, excelling 
in a wide range of language tasks. Their strength lies in analyzing un­
structured textual data, a capability that proves particularly valuable in 
contexts requiring the distillation of actionable insights from diverse 
linguistic sources. Pre-trained transformer architectures like BERT [9] 
and its derivatives (e.g., SciBERT [10] for scientific literature, mBERT 
[11] for multilingual tasks) represent foundational advancements in 
natural language processing. Originally developed for general-purpose 
language understanding, these models demonstrate remarkable capac­
ity to process textual data through self-supervised learning on massive 
corpora. Although LLMs have been widely applied across various in­
dustries, their application in forest is still in its nascent stages. For 
instance, recent works have begun exploring LLMs for forest applica­
tions. ForestryBERT [12], a tailored BERT variant, addresses the dy­
namic nature of forestry knowledge by pre-training on 204,636 Chinese 
texts using a Dynamic Knowledge Absorption Mechanis. Despite their 
transformative potential, LLMs face critical limitations in specialized 
applications. 
First,

general-purpose models like BERT underperform in capturing special­
ized forest knowledge due to their training on broad, non-specialized 
corpora. Second, LLMs often exhibit poor interpretability [13], making 
it challenging to trace the reasoning behind their outputs, which is 
critical for reliable decision-making in forest. Third, deep reasoning in 
forest-specific contexts is difficult: the reliance on general-purpose 
corpora dilutes specialized, long-tail knowledge [14], and the lack of 
modeling experience with domain-specific formats leads to distortions 
or omissions of critical details when addressing complex forest 
problems.
On the other side, the scarcity of labeled training data in deep 
learning applications has driven the adoption of generative models, 
which have revolutionized data synthesis across various domains and 
now offer transformative potential for forest and agriculture. These 
models can be broadly classified into three main categories: Generative 
Adversarial Networks (GANs) [15], Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) 
[16], and Diffusion Models [17], each bringing distinct mechanisms and 
applications to these fields. GANs, comprising a generator and 
discriminator trained in an adversarial setting, remain pivotal for 
dataset augmentation. In agriculture, GANs such as DCGAN [18] have 
generated synthetic images of crop diseases, enabling robust training of 
diagnostic models with limited real-world data. In forest, GANs have 
been utilized to generate synthetic forest imagery, aiding in tasks such as 
habitat mapping and species identification. However, GANs are prone to 
mode collapse and training instability—issues that become particularly 
pronounced in ecologically complex settings such as forests. The high 
structural heterogeneity, occlusions, and varying illumination condi­
tions challenge both the generator and discriminator: the generator may 
fail to produce sufficiently diverse and realistic tree features, while the 
discriminator can become overly sensitive to subtle textures and lighting 
variations, disrupting the adversarial balance. This often leads to re­
petitive or unrealistic synthetic outputs that inadequately represent the 
true variability of forest environments. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) 
encode data into probabilistic latent spaces, enabling applications in 
agriculture and forest through data-driven synthesis and cross-modal 
generation [19]. In forest, they have been applied to generate syn­
thetic tree crown images conditioned on annotated species labels, 
thereby augmenting datasets for training deep learning classifiers [20]. 
While advanced variants like β-VAE [21] and Conditional VAEs (CVAEs) 
[22] offer potential for disentangling latent factors (e.g., seasonal vari­
ations or light conditions), their direct application to simulating forest 
growth dynamics or time-series canopy maps remains exploratory and 
dependent on integration with ecological models or multimodal sensing 
data. A key limitation of VAEs is their tendency toward 
sharpness-fidelity trade-offs, often producing smoothed or blurry out­
puts due to the variational approximation in the latent space. In forest 
environments, this smoothing effect impairs the model’s ability to pre­
serve fine-grained textural and morphological features that are critical 
for species identification. For example, when distinguishing between 
visually similar species such as Pinus sylvestris and Picea abies—which 
differ subtly in crown shape, branching pattern, and bark textur­
e—VAE-generated images may lack the high-frequency details necessary 
to capture these discriminative characteristics, thereby reducing the 
reliability of downstream classification tasks. Diffusion Models, partic­
ularly Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) [23] , have 
recently excelled in high-fidelity forest scene generation. Unlike GANs, 
which suffer from mode collapse and training instability, DDPMs itera­
tively refine noise into structured botanical details, producing images 
with precise leaf venation patterns and bark textures. In forest, DDPMs 
simulate rare ecological events by conditioning generation on climato­
logical variables (e.g., temperature anomalies, precipitation shifts). 
Emerging methods that integrate linguistic and generative models, such 
as Rsdiff [24], and multimodal frameworks like Versatile Diffusion [25], 
demonstrate significant potential for efficient and semantically city 
building detection and rich forest scene, though their application in this 
domain faces further challenges and development space.
To address these interconnected challenges of species diversity and 
phenology, we propose a linguistic guided image diffusion network 
(LGINet). This holistic framework synergistically integrates linguistic 
semantics with diffusion models to generate realistic tree samples that 
capture species-specific traits and seasonal phenological shifts, thereby 
enhancing tree species identification in aerial imagery. LGINet achieves 
three key objectives: (1) Developing a specialized semantic generation 
library for forest text, leveraging optimized neural architectures and 
integrating domain-specific corpora to construct a tailored forest lan­
guage model. (2) A comprehensive review of the theoretical foundations 
of diffusion probabilistic models is conducted, followed by the presen­
tation of an enhanced forest tree species diffusion model. This model 
integrates an improved U-Net-similar architecture and incorporates 
guidance from a text-semantic network. (3) Leveraging these synthetic 
images as training samples for effective tree crown detection and species 
identification, powered by a customized YOLOv11 architecture opti­
mized for large-scale forest monitoring.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data acquisition
In this study, we employed a diverse set of image datasets to ensure 
broad geographic, ecological, and phenological coverage for multimodal 
forest analysis. The selected datasets include international and domestic 
sources, supplemented by aerial imagery collected from publicly avail­
able internet repositories. International datasets form the foundation of 
our study. The Prue Forest Dataset [26] offers temperate urban forest 
imagery with representative canopy structures, while the Quebec Trees 
Dataset [27] provides high-resolution multi-seasonal imagery from 
northern mixed forests, capturing pronounced phenological variations 
across spring, summer, and fall conditions. Additionally, specialized 
single-species datasets—Kolovai-Trees (coconut palms) and Larch_Ca­
sebearer (larch) [28]—enhance the representation of specific tree 
morphologies. For subtropical forest contexts, we incorporated two 
Chinese datasets. The Zhongshan Scenic Area Canopy Inventory delivers 
high-resolution drone imagery of a managed memorial landscape, while 
the 
Nanjing 
Forestry 
University 
Arboretum 
dataset 
provides 
multi-temporal observations of diverse woody species from 2018 to 
2022. Furthermore, our training corpus was augmented with aerial 
imagery of multiple tree species sourced from publicly available internet 
databases, expanding the diversity of canopy architectures and envi­
ronmental conditions. Table 1 summarizes key characteristics of the four 
primary datasets, including species diversity, sample counts, class dis­
tributions, spatial resolution, phenological diversity, and dominant tree 
species.
The linguistic dataset consists of 150 academic articles and forestry 
reports (totaling approximately 1.2 million words), sourced from mul­
tiple databases: Google Scholar (80 articles), Web of Science (50 peer- 
reviewed studies on ecology and forestry), and China National Knowl­
edge Infrastructure (20 reports focusing on subtropical forestry data in 
China). Text data were retrieved through a combination of manual 
searches and automated tools, including web scraping with Python li­
braries such as BeautifulSoup and database API interactions using re­
quests. Keywords included terms like "forest ecology," "tree species 
classification," and "urban forestry." Results were filtered to publications 
from 2015 to 2022 to ensure relevance and timeliness. The raw text data 
underwent rigorous preprocessing to transform them into an analysis- 
ready structured format. This included text cleaning with regular ex­
pressions to remove HTML tags, special characters, and non-text ele­
ments; tokenization into words and sentences using the NLTK library; 
lowercasing and lemmatization with NLTK’s WordNetLemmatizer to 
standardize terminology; and domain-specific processing to extract 
forest-related terms, such as species names and phenological terminol­
ogy, using a predefined vocabulary. The final text dataset was then 
embedded using pre-trained language models, improving the inter­
pretability of the diffusion model in the forest-specific context when 
combined with image data.
To construct a robust multimodal dataset, individual tree crown 
images were first extracted from all source datasets and uniformly 
resized to 32 × 32 × 3 pixels, a patch resolution chosen to strike a 
balance between computational efficiency and the preservation of crit­
ical details such as leaf texture, while remaining consistent with estab­
lished benchmarks like DDPM on CIFAR-10. Each image subsequently 
underwent a series of preprocessing operations to improve invariance 
and generalizability. These included affine transformations (rotation, 
horizontal flip) and radiometric adjustments (brightness and contrast 
variation within ±30 %). Each processed image was paired with a 
descriptive text annotation detailing acquisition angle, species classifi­
cation, lighting condition, and phenological stage. The resulting multi­
modal dataset encompasses a diverse set of tree species and was 
partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets at a 7:2:1 ratio, 
facilitating reproducible evaluation of model performance. Fig. 1 illus­
trates approximately 30 representative species examples alongside their 
textual descriptions. This integrated dataset supports the joint training 
of a 2.2.2. Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training framework (CLIP)- 
based vision–language encoder and a diffusion-based generator, 
enabling effective cross-modal alignment between visual features and 
ecological semantics for enhanced species detection in complex forest 
environments.
2.2. Textual semantic guided image generation via diffusion modeling in 
LGINet
This section describes the textual semantic-guided image generation 
framework of LGINet, which integrates three core components: a 
denoising diffusion process for learning image representations, a 
contrastive language-image pre-training module inspired by the 
Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) framework [29] for 
cross-modal alignment, and an enhanced U-Net-based [30] noise pre­
diction network conditioned on textual inputs. From random noise, the 
model iteratively refines images using textual prompts to synthesize 
photorealistic forest scenes with detailed botanical features, ensuring 
ecological plausibility.
2.2.1. Theoretical foundations of Markov chains for our diffusion model
This subsection outlines the theoretical foundation of our proposed 
framework as depicted in Fig. 2. The forward process involves pro­
gressively injecting Gaussian noise into the input image Ii(x0) (where i 
indexes the image in the batch) via a Markov chain mechanism. Over a 
predefined number of timesteps T, this transforms the original data 
distribution into an approximate standard normal distribution. 
Conversely, the reverse process utilizes a learned neural network θ to 
iteratively refine the noisy intermediate states. Starting from the ter­
minal noise distribution, the network gradually reconstructs a high- 
fidelity image by predicting and removing the noise. The trained 
model parameters θ and the current timestep are used to generate a 
clearer image at each previous step. a) Forward Diffusion Processes

inal image Ii(x0) into standard normal distribution noise over T time­
steps by adding Gaussian noise incrementally. Governed by the

2.2.2. Contrastive language-image pre-training framework (CLIP)
In this study, we adopt the CLIP framework, which leverages 
contrastive learning to train a model capable of aligning textual and 
visual representations in a shared embedding space. As illustrated in 
Fig. 3(a), the model is trained on a large-scale preprocessed dataset 
comprising aligned image-text pairs (Ii ↔Si) , where each image is 
associated with a corresponding textual description. These pairs are 
organized in batches of size b and passed through dual encoders—a text 
encoder and an image encoder. a) ViT-Based Image Encoder
The image encoder, highlighted in the blue module of Fig. 3(a), 
employs the Vision Transformer (ViT) [32], chosen for its lower 
computational requirements and ease of integration. The input image 
Ii(i = 1, 2, ..., b) is divided into 4 image patches, each of size (c, p, p) , 
where pis set to 16. These patches, combined with positional encodings, 
are fed into the Transformer [33] architecture to generate image em­
beddings EIi. The dimension of EIi is [b,e], with e set to 512 to align with 
the text embeddings generated in section b). b) Text Encoder with 
Word2Vec and Transformer

For the generation pipeline depicted in Fig. 3(c), the inference pro­

encoded into contextual embeddings via a pretrained text encoder. 
Corresponding Gaussian noise samples Ii(xT) ∼N(0, 1) and temporal

final image reconstructions that semantically align with input text

For training the improved U-Net, we employed the AdamW opti­
mizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 104, a linear warmup schedule 
over 1000 steps to stabilize early training, and Exponential Moving 
Average (EMA) with a decay rate of 0.999 to improve generalization by 
averaging model weights across iterations. Training was conducted 
70,000 steps with a batch size of 25 on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. During 
training, the text encoder is frozen to leverage domain-specific knowl­
edge without overfitting, while the U-Net diffusion backbone is fully 
trained. Shared components include the variance scheduler βt and noise 
sampler, which are fixed across forward and reverse processes for 
consistency.
2.3. Forest canopy detection via diffusion-generated outputs in LGINet
To leverage synthetically generated tree canopy images for practical 
forest monitoring , the 32 × 32-pixel generated patches were first 
assembled into composite 640 × 640-pixel images to simulate realistic 
forest scenes. This approach allows for the scalable creation of larger 
scenes and can be extended to real-world forest coverage through iter­
ative tiling or a sliding window strategy, thereby reducing the atten­
tional complexity inherent in processing high-resolution aerial data. 
Based on this, we developed an enhanced YOLOv11-based detection 
pipeline. This pipeline incorporates two key architectural improve­
ments: (1) We implemented a Feature Pyramid Network FPN enhanced 
with bidirectional(BiFPN) cross-scale connections, enabling richer 
multi-scale feature fusion. This improvement is critical for detecting tree 
crowns of varying sizes within a single composite image, ensuring robust 
performance across diverse forest scenes. (2) We integrated globally 
pooled attention-augmented fully connected layers into the classifica­
tion branches. These layers leverage semantic context derived from the 
entire image to modulate classification scores, improving species dif­
ferentiation by combining local features with broader contextual cues. 
The detailed network structure is illustrated in Fig. a2 of Appendix.
The improved YOLOv11 was pre-trained on our dataset and fine- 
tuned using our multimodal forest dataset. The training followed a 
curriculum learning approach, beginning with detection on isolated tree 
crowns and progressively introducing composite images with increasing 
numbers of overlapping tree crowns. Data augmentation techniques, 
including photometric distortions, synthetic occlusion patterns 
mimicking canopy gaps, and spatial transformations preserving 
ecological validity, were applied to enhance robustness. The loss func­
tion combines a generalized intersection-over-union (GIoU) term for 
bounding box regression with a hierarchical softmax classification loss, 
weighted by species frequency to address class imbalance.

The ablation study confirms that each component contributes 
significantly to the overall performance of the model. We first identified 
an optimal text prompt weight of 1.0, which effectively balances con­
ditional fidelity and generative diversity. To qualitatively evaluate this 
trade-off, Fig. 5(d) presents 16 sample images generated from 8 distinct 
semantic prompts across different weighting configurations. The results 
indicate that unconditional generation (weight=0) yields coherent but 
semantically unaligned samples. A weight of 0.5 provides limited se­
mantic fidelity (FID=6.94, SSIM=0.90), while the optimal weight of 1.0 
achieves a superior balance (FID=6.42, SSIM=0.94). Although a weight 
of 1.5 offers marginal metric gains (FID=6.37, SSIM=0.96), it in­
troduces subtle artifacts, such as oversaturated foliage. Beyond the text 
prompt weight, the noise scheduler ensures sufficient granularity for 
high-quality denoising, positional encodings provide crucial temporal 
information, textual semantics align generated images with prompts, 
self-attention enhances spatial and semantic coherence, and the optimal 
batch size ensures efficient learning. Fig. 6(a) demonstrates the loss 
variations across epochs under different parameter settings, highlighting 
the stability and convergence benefits of these optimized components. 
To further evaluate the effectiveness of different text encoder variants in 
LGINet, we conducted an additional ablation study, with the results 
presented in Fig. 6(b). The comparison of No-Text (IS=5.32, PSNR=25),

that progressively sophisticated text guidance leads to significant im­

4. Discussion
This study was motivated by the hypothesis that structured ecolog­
ical knowledge, encoded in linguistic descriptions, can guide probabi­
listic image generation to overcome challenges [46] like interspecies 
tree similarities, environmental disturbances and phenological varia­
tions [47]. To operationalize this, we proposed LGINet, a novel multi­
modal framework that leverages domain-specific text from forestry 
corpora to steer a diffusion-based synthesis process. At its core, LGINet 
employs a stabilized diffusion architecture founded on a Markovian 
chain of iterative denoising, which refines synthetic images under se­
mantic constraints derived from text. This approach integrates a 
large-scale language model to parse complex and often ambiguous 
botanical descriptions into dense ecological embeddings, effectively 
bridging the gap between linguistic knowledge and visual generation. 
Our results demonstrate that LGINet generates highly realistic tree im­
agery with exceptional fidelity (SSIM: 0.94; FID: 6.42). The downstream 
detection module attained a mAP50 of 0.868, outperforming baseline 
models. These results highlight the potential of our approach, which 
stands in contrast to the limitations of prevailing data augmentation 
paradigms.
Previous research in this domain has commonly employed fusion 
strategies integrating GANs with CNNs or VAEs with transformer-based 
architectures. For instance, GAN-based methods leveraging StyleGAN or 
BigGAN have been fused with detection frameworks like Faster R-CNN 
to augment training datasets and enhance species classification accuracy 
[48]. Similarly, VAEs have been combined with CNNs to model forest 
structural complexity while supporting detection tasks [49]. However, 
these fusion approaches exhibit notable deficiencies. For instance, GANs 
suffer from mode collapse and training instability, leading to synthetic 
images with blurred textures and semantic inconsistencies that impair 
downstream detection performance [50]. VAEs, on the other hand, often 
produce outputs with compromised sharpness due to inherent fidelity 
trade-offs, resulting in semantic drift that misaligns generated images 
with ecological contexts. Furthermore, the lack of a cohesive optimiza­
tion strategy in these fusion frameworks limits their effectiveness in 
handling dense forest canopies and inter-tree occlusions, where detec­
tion performance frequently degrades [51].
Although LGINet outperforms GAN and VAE baselines in image fi­
delity (e.g., IS 9.89 vs. StyleGAN’s 9.74), it introduces certain limitations 
requiring attention. The iterative denoising process inherent to diffusion 
models incurs notable computational overhead. As summarized in 
Table 2, LGINet requires 5.2 s, 4.5 GFLOPs, and 324 MB of memory per 
image on an A100 GPU, compared to StyleGAN’s 1.8 s, 5.0 GFLOPs, and 
280 MB—resulting in a 2.9 × increase in latency. This computational 
profile currently impedes the deployment of optimized versions on 
embedded drone chips for real-time monitoring at the plot or regional 
scale. Second, although CLIP improves ecological relevance, subtle se­
mantic gaps persist when translating complex ecological descriptions 
into visual outputs, particularly for heterogeneous forest scenes world­
wide. Third, the framework lacks iterative linguistic interaction, 
limiting the ability to adjust ecological parameters during scene gener­
ation. Furthermore, our tree species detection method primarily relies 
on discerning foliar characteristics such as crown shape and leaf texture. 
Consequently, the model’s performance is fundamentally limited when 
dealing with leafless specimens, including withered trees or those in the 
leaf-off season. Therefore, solving complex dead wood detection and 
species classification tasks—such as segmenting overlapping fallen logs 
[52] or isolating adjacent standing dead trees [53,54] —will require 
both a larger set of corresponding training samples and further model 
development. Our future work will therefore explore several directions 
to address these limitations: developing lightweight variants through 
model compression techniques like knowledge distillation [55]; inte­
grating taxonomic-ecological knowledge graphs to enhance semantic 
grounding [56]; designing human-language multi-turn interactive 
communication networks [57] to enable iterative refinement of complex 
ecological descriptions, thereby closing the subtle semantic gaps in the 
translation to visual outputs; and extending the model to various forest 
scenarios under nature disturbances by generating and training on 
hybrid feature sets with curated datasets, thereby enhancing general­
izability and applicability across diverse forest monitoring tasks. 
Furthermore, we will incorporate self-supervised learning strategies 
such as masked autoencoding and contrastive text-image alignment to 
improve ecological coherence while reducing reliance on labeled data.
5. Conclusion
This study presents an innovative framework that integrates a forest- 
specific language model with a text-guided diffusion model and a 
detection network, enabling semantically conditioned forest image 
synthesis and accurate tree species detection. The forest language model 
encodes ecological knowledge to guide the diffusion process, generating 
realistic forest scenes which are then used to train an improved 
YOLOv11-based detector. By aligning image and text embeddings under 
the CLIP paradigm, the approach ensures high semantic consistency 
between generated visuals and input descriptions. An enhanced U-Net 
progressively refines output images, achieving a PSNR of 30 dB, while 
the detection model attains a mAP of 0.868. Evaluations demonstrate 
the framework’s strong performance across complex canopy structures 
and species-rich environments, supported by a multi-source training 
corpus that promotes robustness and generalizability. Future work will 
focus on extending the model’s adaptability to diverse geographic and 
ecological settings, with the potential to incorporate large language 
models for global-scale tree sample generation.
