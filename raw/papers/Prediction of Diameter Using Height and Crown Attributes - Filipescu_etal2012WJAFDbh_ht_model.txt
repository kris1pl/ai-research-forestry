Prediction of Diameter Using Height and Crown
Attributes: A Case Study
Cosmin N. Filipescu, Arthur Groot, Dan A. MacIsaac, Mike G. Cruickshank, and
James D. Stewart
Recent advances in remote sensing provide increasingly detailed forest information in a timely and cost-effective manner. Individual tree stem diameter, an
important variable for operational forest inventory, cannot be determined directly from remotely sensed data; stem diameter must be estimated from ancillary
measures of tree crown, tree height, and/or measures related to stand structure. In this study, we developed predictive models of diameter as a function of
height and crown attributes using a nonlinear mixed-effects approach. Long-term silvicultural experiments provided data for several species: black spruce,
Douglas-ﬁr, and lodgepole pine. Addition of crown area and crown area of larger trees to diameter-height models signiﬁcantly improved predictive performance,
resulting in low root mean squared error values between 0.9 and 1.8 cm (10–11% of mean diameter). Our models indicated the need to include additional
explanatory variables at wider levels of spacing and thinning. This case study highlights several practical implications for developing and reﬁning individual
tree diameter models based on variables that can be remotely sensed.
KEYWORDS: diameter-height models, crown, nonlinear mixed-effects
D
iameter and height constitute fundamental information
for the development of reliable growth and yield tools (Ass-
mann 1970). Forest management relies on diameter and
height to predict tree and stand volume, biomass, site index, and
other important attributes that support the decisionmaking process.
Traditionally, ﬁeld measurements of diameter at breast height (dbh)
have been relatively easy, inexpensive, and more accurate compared
with measurements of height; thus dbh is usually measured for all
sample trees, whereas height is measured only for a subsample, fol-
lowed by ﬁtting of graphical or statistical models between height and
dbh (Calama and Montero 2004). Although forest biometricians
have modeled height as a function of dbh for decades (e.g., Meyer
1940), relationships between height and dbh for various species
have only recently been explored using nonlinear mixed-effects
models for better prediction and local calibration (e.g., Lynch et al.
2005, Crecente-Campo et al. 2010).
Recent developments in remote sensing have enabled collection
of forest structure and terrain attributes using light detection and
ranging (LiDAR) applications (e.g., Hudak et al. 2009, Sullivan et
al. 2009). LiDAR-retrieved information has been used to estimate
stand-level structural parameters (Lefsky et al. 2007, Næsset and
Gobakken 2008, Goerndt et al. 2010), as well as attributes of indi-
vidual trees (Popescu et al. 2003, Lefsky and McHale 2008). The
estimation of stem diameter for individual trees has received rela-
tively less attention, largely because of costs associated with acquir-
ing high-density LiDAR information. However, some of these lim-
itations may be overcome by combining LiDAR data with aerial
photographs and high-resolution multispectral imagery to delineate
the height and crown attributes of individual trees (Leckie et al.
2003, Sua´rez et al. 2005). Simple linear relationships have been
developed to predict dbh, typically using height and crown area or
crown diameter (Zagalikis et al. 2005, Popescu 2007, Brandeis et al.
2009). Although linear equations may appear suitable, the question
remains whether these models would be generally applicable to a
broader range of conditions and stand structures.
Forest inventories require acquisition of increasingly detailed infor-
mation for large areas in a timely and cost-effective manner. Forest
inventories are currently facing several challenges to increase the level of
detail, lower costs, and include information on the quality of ﬁber and
value of products (Temesgen et al. 2007a, Pitt and Pineau 2009). The
objective of our study was to evaluate the feasibility of predicting stem
diameter using height and crown characteristics. In our case study, we
used ground-based measurements; this allowed for efﬁcient testing of
model forms, suitability of independent variables, and assessment of
potential accuracy. Suitable models identiﬁed in this study can then be
ﬁtted in subsequent work using ground-measured diameter and re-
motely sensed independent variables.

Analysis of ﬁxed-effects models of diameter as a function of
height, crown area, crown area of larger trees, and treatment (where
applicable) was expanded by including random parameters to cap-
ture plot and site effects (e.g., Sharma and Parton 2007, Vargas-
Larreta et al. 2009). A mixed-effects approach was used because of
the hierarchical structure of data with multiple sample units (plots
and locations); therefore, it is reasonable to expect both among-unit
variability and correlation of measurements within a unit (Hall and
Bailey 2001).
Model parameters were estimated in R (R Development Core
Team 2011). Validity of model assumptions was based on analysis
of residuals for equality of variance and normality. Despite the ab-
sence of independent data sets for validation, data were not split into
estimation and cross-validation subsets. Several authors have argued
that cross-validation provides little if any additional information
compared with models developed using all of the available data, and
there are clear disadvantages to ﬁtting models with split data sets
(Hirsch 1991, Kozak and Kozak 2003, Yang et al. 2004).
Results
Relevant ﬁt statistics for diameter prediction models based on
height only; crown area only; and height combined with crown area,
crown area of larger trees, and treatment are listed in Table 4. All
goodness criteria (R2, RMSE, MAD, and AIC) showed similar
trends of goodness-of-ﬁt with different combinations of predictor
variables. For models of diameter as a function of height, RMSE
ranged between 1.65 and 3.43 cm, whereas for models of diameter
as a function of crown area, RMSE ranged between 1.76 and 4.62
cm. Height alone was a better predictor of diameter for black spruce
and lodgepole pine, whereas crown area alone was a better predictor
for Douglas-ﬁr compared with height alone. A considerable amount
of unexplained variation (R2 as low as 0.619) remained with models
predicting diameter from height alone (Table 4). Combining height
with crown area resulted in models with more accurate predictions,
whereas the addition of crown area of larger trees further improved
predictions. Inclusion of treatment rendered signiﬁcant parameters
for wide spacing levels (3 and 4 m) for black spruce; for lodgepole
pine, treatment parameters were not signiﬁcant for fertilization, and
only the high level of thinning was signiﬁcant. Treatment parame-
ters provided a relatively small but signiﬁcant improvement of
model ﬁt (Table 4).
The preferred ﬁxed-effects models were Equation 9 for black
spruce (height, crown area, and spacing), Equation 8 for Douglas-
ﬁr, and Equation 10 for lodgepole pine (height, crown area, CAL,
and thinning). These models were explored further by adding ran-
dom parameters for plots (black spruce) and for plots and locations
(Douglas-ﬁr and lodgepole pine). Best results (lowest AIC) were
obtained with only one random parameter, as models with two or
more random parameters failed to converge or provided inferior
predictions (larger AIC).

Discussion
Models developed in this study provide accurate predictions of
stem diameter based on height and crown attributes for several
species. Previous studies on predicting height from diameter have
indicated a clear need for a nonlinear mixed effects approach
(Sharma and Parton 2007, Temesgen et al. 2008). Our results sug-
gest that nonlinear models are also needed for relationships predict-
ing diameter from height, in agreement with other similar studies
(e.g., Hall et al. 2001, Parker and Evans 2004). Moreover, recent
ﬁndings indicate that mixed-effects models provide the best good-
ness-of-ﬁt for predicting dbh from LiDAR-derived variables (Salas
et al. 2010).
Several base models of diameter and height were evaluated and
tested in our study. Somewhat surprisingly, power functions seemed
to perform as well as or better than sigmoidal-shaped equations. The
latter models (e.g., Chapman-Richards) have been reported to ap-
proach the asymptote too quickly when there is a weak relationship
between the dependent and independent variables (Huang et al.
1992); in addition, Temesgen et al. (2007b) have suggested that
height-diameter models are more strongly asymptotic for individual
stands than for regional models. This indicates that further valida-
tion of these models may be needed for application at wide geo-
graphical scales.
Our results show that diameter models based solely on height
performed better than models based solely on crown for black
spruce and lodgepole pine, whereas crown area alone was a stronger
predictor of diameter for Douglas-ﬁr. Our ﬁndings contradict those
reported by Hall et al. (1989), who suggested that crown area is a
better predictor of diameter than height for lodgepole pine in Al-
berta. However, it is unclear whether these differences can be further

The addition of crown area and crown area of larger trees to base
models of diameter and height signiﬁcantly improved predictive
performance. Crown area of larger trees likely reﬂects the competi-
tive status (i.e., social status) and access to resources of the subject
tree (Purves et al. 2007). In turn, resource availability during the
growing season affects stem diameter to a larger extent than tree
height, accounting for the need to include additional explanatory
variables. The improvement in performance obtained by adding
measures of competitive status corresponds to the improvement in
height-diameter model performance when stand density and basal
area are included as predictors (Sharma and Parton 2007). More-
over, the improvement in model ﬁt for wider spacing and thinning
levels may indicate further the importance of resource access. Trees
growing in a more open environment are able to access more light
relative to their height and crown dimensions compared with trees
of the same size in more closed stands, leading to larger stem diam-
eter. The relationship between height and diameter, as expressed by
the slenderness coefﬁcient, is related to crown length, stand density
and site index for boreal conifers (Wang et al. 1998). For lodgepole
pine, both stand-level variables (e.g., crown closure, Rudnicki et al.
2004) and tree-level variables (e.g., crown radius and length, Fish et
al. 2006) can inﬂuence height-diameter relationships. Depending
on data available, diameter equations could include stand-level or
tree-level variables to improve prediction of base models.
Our best models provided low RMSE values between 0.99 and
1.79 cm, which is between 10.1 and 11.3% of average diameter
(Table 5). By comparison, other studies have reported RMSE be-
tween 2.4 and 3.6 cm for nonlinear models for conifer species in
Canada (Hall et al. 2001), 4.9 cm (18% of average) for linear models
for pine stands in the southeastern United States (Popescu 2007),
3.8 cm (10%) for spruce and pine in Sweden (Persson et al. 2002),
and 4.6 to 5.9 cm (11.9 to 15.2%) for several conifers and broad-
leaves in Germany (Heurich 2008), although the last study also
reported prediction bias (i.e., over- or underprediction) for broad-
leaves in certain diameter classes.
Results presented in this case study are encouraging for the ap-
plication of dbh models to a wider range of stands and conditions.
However, further development and reﬁnement of diameter models
is needed to determine the inﬂuence of regional variation, site, and
climate by using data sets covering large areas. Moreover, remotely
sensed proxies for crown area and height could include errors that
may require further evaluation of diameter models.
Conclusions
Our study indicates that nonlinear mixed-effects prediction of
individual stem diameter based on ground measurements of height,
crown area, and crown area of larger trees provides reliable, unbiased
models with low prediction error. For operational predictions of
stem diameter, these models should be ﬁtted with ground-measured
diameter, and tree height and crown dimensions determined by
operational remote sensing methods currently under testing and
development. Increasing application and operational use of remote
sensing will help validate and reﬁne these models.
Developing models that relate diameter to height and crown
variables is an important step forward for forest inventories. Infor-
mation on piece size distribution and product value will allow for
better planning and optimization of decisionmaking in forest man-
agement. Our results indicate the potential of these predictive mod-
els and will help to inform further testing with remotely sensed
height and crown data.
