A B S T R A C T
The use of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), particularly with high-density point clouds obtained through UAV 
laser scanning (ULS) and UAV structure from motion (UAV-SfM) techniques, offer cost-effective alternatives for 
forest inventory. However, the literature lacks comprehensive assessments of their limitations across diverse 
ranges of age classes and site conditions. This study addressed this gap by evaluating the estimation accuracy of 
crucial tree attributes, diameter at breast height (DBH) and tree height, in a range of age classes within forest 
plantations. In addition, this study thoroughly evaluated the performance of ULS and UAV-SfM in diverse site 
conditions using point clouds obtained from Pinus radiata D. Don plantations, a widely planted commercial 
timber species worldwide. To achieve this, UAV and field data were gathered from twelve sites, including 
multitemporal data for four sites. By employing an automated data processing pipeline, individual trees were 
segmented and structural metrics extracted from tree segments to estimate DBH and tree height at an individual 
tree level. Results indicated that UAV-SfM and ULS performed comparably in estimating DBH over the entire 
dataset, with R2 values of 0.67 and 0.74 and RMSE values of 2.05 cm (11 %) and 2.13 cm (11 %) respectively. 
However, ULS generally outperformed UAV-SfM at the site level, achieving higher R2 values (0.46–0.90 vs 
0.21–0.85) and RMSE values (0.33–7.24 cm at 7–24 % vs. 0.35–6.15 cm at 8–17 %). ULS also consistently 
outperformed UAV-SfM in tree height measurements across sites, with an average per site RMSE of 0.68 m (5.4 
%) compared with 1.21 m (11.59 %), demonstrating its robustness in diverse conditions. Site-specific factors 
such as stand maturity and logging debris affected measurement reliability in both datasets, with accuracy 
improving for younger sites, sites with a more open canopy and more favourable site conditions (less logging 
debris and weed cover). The study also indicated a moderate relationship between ground sampling distance 
(GSD) of the imagery and UAV-SfM accuracy. The findings highlight the significance of considering site-specific 
variables when choosing UAV technologies for conducting forest inventory, ensuring informed decisions in UAV- 
based forest inventory practices. Consequently, the insights gained from this research hold significant importance 
for practical forestry applications.
1. Introduction
Pinus radiata D Don (P. radiata) is a key coniferous species widely 
planted globally for timber production (Lavery and Mead, 1998), 
particularly in the Southern Hemisphere (Yan et al., 2006). Monitoring 
its growth is essential for optimising productivity and determining the 
appropriate timing for management interventions like thinning and 
pruning.
In forest monitoring, different inventories demand specific mea­
surements: height is generally used to plan silvicultural interventions 
(Maclaren, 2000), while diameter at breast height (DBH) is crucial for 
stand valuations, such as pre-harvest inventories. Other tree attributes, 
like stem straightness and branch characteristics, are also recorded at 
various stages. Among these, tree height and DBH are the most

commonly used tree attributes in forest stand assessments (Maclaren, 
2000; Popescu, 2007). Historically, DBH has been preferred for its ease 
of measurement, serving as a growth proxy and facilitating calculations 
of volume, biomass and carbon (Clark et al., 2000). Together, DBH and 
tree height provide quantitative assessments of timber production, and 
when paired with branching patterns and stem straightness, offer a 
comprehensive appraisal of timber value in forest stands.
Traditionally, DBH is measured with callipers or a diameter tape, 
while tree height is measured using a height pole or a hypsometer, 
depending on tree age (McGlade et al., 2022). These methods are 
time-consuming, costly and prone to bias, especially for height mea­
surements (Hartley et al., 2020; Hyypp¨a et al., 2020; Larjavaara and 
Muller-Landau, 2013; Wang et al., 2019). Moreover, ground-based 
measurements require trained technicians to navigate through the for­
est, raising health and safety concerns, especially in steep terrain (Bayne 
and Parker, 2012). This reliance on skilled labour can also result in 
delays and data integrity issues, particularly during labour shortages.
To overcome these limitations, remote and proximal sensing tech­
niques provide more efficient ways for measuring tree structural attri­
butes, 
predominantly by deriving 
measurements from three- 
dimensional (3D) point clouds. Below-canopy laser scanning tech­
niques, such as terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) and personal mobile laser 
scanning (PLS), can accurately measure DBH by reconstructing tree 
stems within the 3D point clouds. TLS is generally superior due to its 
advanced technology and precision (Levick et al., 2021; Liang et al., 
2018, 2019). However, both methods still require technicians to navi­
gate through the forest (Dalla Corte et al., 2020), and face challenges 
with height measurements due to occlusion from dense canopy foliage 
(Cabo et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2018). Additionally, the 
resource-intensive nature of these methods and the need for forest 
accessibility limit their scalability in larger forest stands.
Airborne laser scanning (ALS), on the other hand, allows 3D struc­
tural data to be captured from forest stands at large scales. ALS is highly 
accurate for measuring tree heights, and prior research has demon­
strated its ability to derive additional tree metrics, such as crown di­
mensions and shape from individual tree point clouds (Erikson and 
Olofsson, 2005; Zhen et al., 2016a). The strong correlation observed 
between crown dimensions and DBH (Balenovi´c et al., 2015; Popescu, 
2007) highlights that ALS could also be used to remotely assess crucial 
tree measurements, such as DBH and stem volume, using point 
cloud-derived tree metrics as proxies. Although ALS provides a scalable 
solution for measuring individual tree attributes, its accuracy can vary 
significantly depending on point cloud specifications, such as point 
density and the laser footprint size (Jakubowski et al., 2013; Liu et al., 
2018; Manuri et al., 2017). Moreover, ALS can be expensive for 
small-scale stands due to costs associated with the aircraft and other 
operational expenses (Bruggisser et al., 2019).
An emerging technology in the forestry industry is the use of un­
manned aerial vehicles (UAVs), which offer affordability, flexibility and 
resource efficiency (Dainelli et al., 2021; Torresan et al., 2016). Ad­
vances in UAV technology have significantly improved the 
cost-effectiveness and efficiency of airborne data collection. UAVs pro­
vide greater flexibility in both data acquisition timing and the types of 
data captured, making them an increasingly valuable tool for foresters. 
(Hartley, 2017; Heaphy et al., 2017). One notable advancement is UAV 
laser scanning (ULS), which generates high-density point clouds by 
flying at lower altitudes and speeds compared to traditional ALS 
methods. ULS combines higher capture speed with increased viewpoint 
angles and thus effectively addresses occlusion issues. Further, ULS is 
not constrained by the mobility issues faced by terrestrial mobile plat­
forms like PLS (Liang et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the costs associated 
with deploying ULS systems and the specialised expertise required for 
data processing have posed barriers to their widespread adoption by 
forest managers. An alternative and cost-effective approach to ULS is the 
use of high-resolution Red-Green-Blue (RGB) imagery captured by 
UAV-mounted camera sensors, which can be processed into point clouds 
using photogrammetric techniques like structure from motion (Dandois 
and Ellis, 2013).
Previous studies have extensively explored the use of ULS and UAV- 
SfM point clouds in various forestry applications, including inventory 
(Jayathunga et al., 2019; Puliti et al., 2017), and specifically for pre­
dicting tree height (Hartley et al., 2020; Rodríguez-Puerta et al., 2021). 
Some studies also attempted to directly measure DBH from UAV point 
clouds, attaining relatively good results with relative root mean square 
error (RMSE) values ranging from 19 % to 36 % (Kuˇzelka et al., 2020; 
Puliti et al., 2020). The latter study also proposing a novel method to 
develop models from the DBH and crown measurement of the most 
accurately characterised individual tree point clouds, which can then be 
applied to the tree point clouds with fewer stem returns. However, both 
of these methods rely on having stem returns, making them unsuitable 
for young trees with heavy branching and foliar occlusion of the stem, 
forest environments with heavy understory, tree species with retained 
branches on the lower stems, or UAV-SfM data, all of which are unlikely 
to yield tree point clouds with satisfactory coverage of stem returns. 
Machine learning methods have been used to model forestry metrics 
from UAV-SfM point cloud-derived measurements, achieving strong 
results for predicting stand density, basal area and above ground 
biomass (R2 = 0.91, 0.79 and 0.92, respectively; (Alonzo et al., 2018)). 
In a previous study, machine learning methods were applied to crown 
metrics derived from ULS to model DBH effectively in a recently thin­
ned, mid-rotation stand of P. radiata, with a relative RMSE of ~7 % 
(Watt et al., 2024).
Despite numerous studies examining the accuracy of DBH and height 
measurement from UAVs, a comprehensive assessment of the limitations 
of these technologies across different age classes and site conditions is 
still lacking. Notably, there is limited understanding of how factors such 
as terrain conditions, the prevalence of weeds, and the presence of non- 
vegetation obstacles (e.g., logging debris, thinned trees, pruning res­
idue) affect the performance of point cloud-based measurements when 
estimating tree attributes at various stages of the plantation lifecycle 
using UAVs. For instance, a previous study by the authors evaluated the 
accuracy of height measurements for young P. radiata trees (aged five 
months to three years) using ULS and SfM techniques (Hartley et al., 
2020). While strong correlations between UAV-SfM-derived and 
field-measured tree heights were observed, the study also noted that site 
conditions, such as harvest residue and weeds, affected the performance 
of UAV point clouds, though this was not thoroughly explored.
Since these site conditions are often typical of real-world forestry 
plantations and cannot always be mitigated through proper planning or 
improving data processing methods, understanding their impact on the 
accuracy of key tree attribute estimations is crucial for forest managers 
to make informed decisions. Therefore, further research is needed to 
address this gap and establish a more comprehensive understanding of 
the capabilities and limitations of UAV point clouds in real-world 
forestry settings.
Using point clouds captured across a range of P. radiata plantations, 
this study explores the utility of ULS and UAV-SfM point clouds by (i) 
comparing the accuracy of each sensor type for predicting DBH and 
measuring tree height in stands with diverse age classes and site con­
ditions, and (ii) identifying the optimal site conditions in which each 
technology can be used.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Study site
To conduct a comprehensive evaluation of ULS and UAV-SfM tech­
nologies in characterising P. radiata, this study aggregated UAV point 
cloud datasets collected by Scion over a number of years across various 
different projects and studies (Fig. 1, Table 1).
Management regime and site conditions, including slope (Saremi 
et al., 2014a, 2014b), amount of logging debris (Ballard and Will, 1981;

growth (Richardson et al., 1996), can significantly impact on the growth 
and form of trees in plantation forests. These factors, combined with age, 
and consequently tree size, also affect the accuracy of remotely sensed

All sites constituted forestry trials (breeding or genetic trials), except 
Kinleith Schnapper Road and Kaki Road, which consisted of plots within 
production forest stands. In addition, three sites– Rangipo, Kaingaroa

mising growth rates of P. radiata in plantation forestry (Smaill et al., 
2023). The sites represented a range of age classes including early 
establishment (<4 years), pre-thinning (age 4–6), recently thinned and 
mid-rotation (age 7–10) and late rotation (age 17+) stands.
2.2. Field data
Ground truth for remote sensing measurements was captured in the 
field using traditional tools. The DBH of all the trees were measured 
directly utilising a diameter tape with a precision of 1 mm. Heights were 
measured using two methods: for smaller trials, trees were generally 
measured with a height pole with a precision of 1 cm; for taller trees 
(over 5 m tall), a Vertex 4 hypsometer (Hagl¨of, Langsele, Sweden) with a 
precision of 0.1 m was utilised. With the exception of Block 16, all Scion 
sites were measured using a height pole with a precision of 10 cm. De­
tails of tree size for each site can be found in Table 2. As all of the sites 
studied were either forestry or breeding trials, the trees were laid out in 
systematic grids, with corner pegs to facilitate identification of plots in 
the field, and numbers painted onto the trees for regular re- 
measurement (Fig. 2). The regular layout and pegs demarcating plot 
boundaries also enabled effective individual tree identification within 
the remotely sensed data (See Section 2.3 and Fig. 2).

difficult task in forest remote sensing studies due to the unreliable per­

dense forest canopies (Murgaˇs et al., 2018). To precisely match field and 
remotely sensed measurements in this study, the trial layouts were 
digitised and georeferenced, and the tree numbers were accurately 
assigned to individual tree point clouds. This was carried out using a 
pipeline developed using the R programming language (R Core Team, 
2022). The pipeline generated four spatial layouts for each trial: a trial 
boundary polygon layer, a grid/block polygon layout, peg location 
points and individual tree location points. The process involved the 
following steps: (i) Input the basic trial layout specifications as per the 
trial establishment reports, such as the number of rows of grids, number 
of columns of grids, number of tree rows per grid, number of trees per 
row, planting spacing, numbering origin point (e.g., bottom right, bot­
tom left, top right, top left), and numbering orientation (e.g., left to 
right, right to left, top to bottom, bottom to top); (ii) Generate trial 
layouts in Cartesian coordinates utilising the user inputs and basic 
geometric principles: create a digitised layout of the trial and label its 
components, including grids, trees, and pegs; (iii) Georeference the 
spatial layouts, allowing them to be combined with remotely sensed data 
and used to label individual tree point clouds (see section 2.6). A point 
vector file or a spreadsheet was input, containing a minimum of four peg 
locations with their IDs and XY coordinates in the desired coordinate 
system (in this case New Zealand Geodatum, 2000; NZGD, 2000 and 
New Zealand Transverse Mercator–NZTM; EPSG 2193). These peg lo­
cations served as ground control points (GCPs) to geo-reference the 
created layouts, i.e., converting the local Cartesian coordinates of the 
created layouts to the same coordinate system as the point cloud data. 
GCP peg locations can be identified and extracted from the point cloud 
data, since the pegs were topped with highly reflective material prior to 
data capture (Fig. 2).
2.3. UAV data acquisition
UAV data was collected from each site using two different sensors: a 
ULS sensor and either an RGB or multispectral camera. Although site set- 
up methodology varied slightly at each site, the first step involved 
establishing ground control. A minimum of five GCPs were established 
at each site utilising high contrast targets in accordance with the best 
practice guidelines outlined by Pix4D (2020). GCPs were surveyed using 
a handheld Trimble Geo7X global navigation satellite system (GNSS) 
unit (Trimble Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA), combined with a Trimble 
Zephyr Model 2 external aerial. The accuracy of GCP measurements, as 
measured by the RMSE, ranged from 0.01 to 0.15 m (Table 3). At 
recently captured sites, where highly reflective high-contrast targets 
were deployed (Hartley et al., 2022), GCP target centres were extracted 
from the ULS point cloud data. This approach aimed to increase the 
accuracy of co-registration between UAV-SfM and ULS datasets. Rec­
ognising the inherent difficulty in aligning ground-based and remotely 
sensed measurements in mature forest stands, a methodology referred to 
as CloudReg was developed to align tree-level field and remote sensing 
measurements at the two mature Kinleith Forest sites and the Scion 
Block 16 trial (Hartley et al., 2022, 2024). CloudReg uses highly 
reflective ground control targets that are established in the field prior to 
remotely sensed captures to enable alignment between ULS and 
sub-canopy mobile laser scanning (MLS). This enables more accurate 
location of the areas of interest (AOI, e.g., stand or plot) and stem maps 
that can be used to precisely align field and remotely sensed measure­
ments, particularly in forests that are not planted on a regular grid like 
breeding trials, and where plot limits are not demarcated with pegs. 
These targets, located at the centre and boundary of the AOI at two or 
more cardinal points, are constructed from 1 m2 coreflute plastic panels 
coated in retroreflective material to give them a uniquely high intensity 
return in the lidar point clouds to facilitate point filtering. This system 
enables the effective identification of the AOIs in dense forest point 
clouds, the co-registration of the MLS data, which can often only be

2.4. Raw point cloud generation
2.4.1. UAV-SfM photogrammetric point cloud generation
Generating SfM point clouds from RGB UAV imagery involved 
following standard procedures in the Pix4DMapper software package 
(Pix4D, Lausanne, Switzerland). For each site, the imagery was im­
ported, and tie points were detected, leading to key feature extraction 
and the generation of a sparse point cloud. Subsequently, point cloud 
densification was carried out to produce a dense point cloud with 
detailed and precise reconstruction of objects within the 3D space. The 
optimal processing parameters were determined for each site through a 
trial-and-error approach that the Scion Autonomous Systems team has 
refined over the years. An example of the processing steps can be found 
in Hartley et al. (2020). The point cloud was georeferenced to the NZGD 
2000 New Zealand Transverse Mercator (NZTM) coordinate system 
(EPSG 2193) using the spatial coordinates of the GCPs collected in the 
field. The resulting point cloud that had XYZ and RGB attributes 
attached to each point was exported as a single file in compressed LAS 
(LAZ) file format.

the standard processing steps recommended by the manufacturer. For 
more information please refer to Hartley et al. (2020) and Watt et al. 
(2024). Subsequently, the processed data was exported as a single LAZ

2.5. Point cloud pre-processing
Pre-processing of ULS and UAV-SfM point clouds followed the same 
steps to ensure consistency, enabling meaningful comparison between 
the two data sources. Due to the differences in sensor configurations and 
flight plan settings, there were noticeable disparities in point density 
among the datasets. Consequently, the standardisation of point density 
was necessary to facilitate meaningful comparisons between different 
datasets, thereby preventing differences in accuracy solely attributable 
to variations in point densities. In addition, it was essential to normalise 
variations in point density within individual datasets, including elimi­
nating areas with exceptionally high point densities caused by over­
lapping flight lines. This standardisation approach is vital for individual- 
tree-level studies, ensuring that variations in point density do not 
disproportionately influence metrics derived from individual tree point 
clouds.
To address these tasks, a homogenisation process was applied to all 
point clouds to ensure uniform point densities within and across data­
sets. Initially, a density raster with a resolution of 1 m was generated 
from each dataset to understand the spatial distribution of point density 
across the site. From this, the average and standard deviation of point 
density per dataset were computed. Following this analysis, a density of 
~400 ppm2 was determined as appropriate for most sites, except for 
small sites with very young trees (e.g., Scion sites). Consequently, all 
point clouds were homogenised using the "sample_homogenise" function 
within the lidR library (Roussel et al., 2020) in the R programming 
language. This function employs an algorithm to create a grid with a 
defined resolution and randomly selects points to be retained within 
each grid cell. The proportion of retained points within each grid cell is 
determined by comparing the local density to the desired density; if the 
desired density exceeds the local density, the points remain unchanged.
Following homogenisation, the point cloud underwent standard pre- 
processing steps, which included denoising, ground point classification, 
digital terrain model (DTM) generation, and ground normalisation. 
Generating an accurate high-resolution DTM from UAV-SfM point 
clouds is often challenging in forest stands, particularly where SfM 
techniques failed to detect the ground due to occlusion caused by tree 
canopy. Therefore, in this study, DTMs derived from ULS and UAV-SfM 
point clouds were compared, revealing that the UAV-SfM-derived DTM 
becomes highly unreliable once canopy closure occurs. As a result, for all 
sites, UAV-SfM point clouds were ground normalised using ULS-derived 
DTMs. The pre-processing steps were conducted by implementing an 
automated pipeline formulated using the LASTools software package 
(Isenburg, 2019).
2.6. Individual tree segmentation
A canopy height model (CHM) with a resolution of 25 cm was 
generated by applying the pit-free algorithm developed by Khosravipour 
et al. (2014) on the ground-normalised point cloud. Subsequently, the 
CHM underwent smoothing using a 3 x 3 pixel moving window. Indi­
vidual tree peaks were then detected using a local maxima algorithm 
with a fixed window size corresponding to the initial planting spacing of 
the site. To align point cloud measurements with field measurements, 
individual trees were labelled according to the tree maps created in the 
trial digitisation process (see section 2.2). To do this, tree peaks detected 
on both the CHM and point clouds, along with the digitised and geore­
ferenced tree map were matched. The matching process involved 
calculating the Euclidean distance and orientation from each detected 
tree peak to the corresponding tree location on the digitised tree location 
layout. Any unmatched tree peaks were discarded, while those having

2.7. Metric extraction
A comprehensive list of individual tree metrics, encompassing 
various aspects of tree structure, was compiled based on existing liter­
ature (Coops et al., 2007; du Toit et al., 2022; du Toit et al., 2023; du Toit 
et al., 2020; Plowright and Roussel, 2021; Popescu and Wynne, 2004). 
This compilation included metrics representing both the vertical and 
horizontal variations of individual tree foliage, as well as metrics 
capturing the two-dimensional (2D) and 3D structural characteristics of 
trees. The selected metrics were computed for each individual tree point 
cloud segment using packages within R.
Point-based metrics, such as standard height, intensity, and crown 
density, were calculated utilising the "stdmetric" function within the lidR 
package. A convex hull was fitted to each individual tree segment using 
the cxhull package (Barber and Laurent, 2023), estimating both the 2D 
area of the individual crown convex hull and the 3D surface area and 
volume of the individual tree convex hull. Please refer to Watt et al. 
(2024) for the comprehensive list of metrics investigated in this study. 
The metrics included can be categorised into the following four broad 
groups: (i) standard height, (ii) standard intensity–only for lidar metrics, 
(iii) standard crown density, and (iv) 2D and 3D convex hull measure­
ments. The metric zmax (maximum height within each convex hull) was 
used for comparison with field-based heights.

2.8. Prediction of DBH
Within each trial dataset, the following observations as reported in 
the field data were filtered out prior to modelling: trees reported to be 
dead or dying, suppressed, malformed, leaning or split trees, trees with 
one leader dead or broken, trees affected by windthrow, trees with 
forked stems (codominant leaders), and observations that had no DBH 
measurement. In addition, any observation that had no corresponding 
lidar point cloud metrics associated with them was removed. For each 
trial dataset, DBH was modelled using all non-intensity related point 
cloud metric variables as predictors, that had <20 % missing values.
The following three statistical methods were tested for predicting 
DBH, chosen to encompass a representative range of statistical methods 
with different strengths and weaknesses: polynomial ordinary least 
squares (OLS) regression with backwards variable selection, partial least 
squares (PLS), and random forest (RF). OLS linear regression is the 
simplest method and performs best with uncorrelated variables that 
have a linear relationship to the response. Backward variable selection 
starts with a model where all available independent variables are 
included and sequentially drops variables that do not improve the fit and 
predictive ability of the model until an optimal subset of explanatory 
variables is found. PLS is also a linear regression technique, but instead 
of using the original predictor variables in the model, it reorganises the 
data into orthogonal components that are linear combinations of orig­
inal predictors and finds the optimal number of components. This 
method is suited to highly correlated data and the presence of linear 
relationships with the response variable. RF is an ensemble method that 
uses regression trees and bootstrap aggregating to predict the response 
variable. It is suited to complex data with non-linear relationships.
Each regression method was implemented using the caret package in 
R (Kuhn, 2008). Hyperparameter tuning was performed using 10-fold 
cross-validation with five repeats on a training set that included 80 % 
of the data, through selecting optimal parameters that minimised RMSE. 
The fitted model was used to test model performance on the remaining 
20 % of the data. The RMSE and R2 were calculated in the training and 
testing process using the following formulae:

2.9. Statistical analysis of model performance
To assess the effect of sample size on model performance for DBH 
predictions, an increasing number of observations were randomly 
sampled to include in the training dataset, up to 80 % of the full dataset. 
The model was fitted every time and RMSE and R2 metrics were 
extracted from the cross-validation process.
To assess the effect of trial characteristics and capture type on model 
performance a linear regression was fitted using R2 as the response 
variable and three predictor variables: stand age at the time of UAV data 
capture (continuous variable), data type (two categories: ULS and UAV- 
SfM), and amount of weed cover on the ground. The weed cover cate­
gories in Table 1 were grouped into two categories: “none to little” 
(merging “regularly mowed” and “minor”) and “moderate to heavy” 
(merging “moderate” and “heavy”). The interaction between stand age 
at the time of UAV data capture and amount of weed cover was also 
included. Variable selection was performed using a custom assessment 
of model estimates and Akaike information criteria (AIC).

3. Results
3.1. DBH estimation results
Despite their differences, the three statistical methods chosen to 
model DBH yielded remarkably similar model performance measured by 
R2 and RMSE (Fig. 6). Therefore, all subsequent results and analyses of 
model performance were assessed using RF models.
When averaged over all sites, the RF model was able to predict DBH 
with a moderately strong correlation between UAV predictions and field 
measurements of DBH, with a mean R2 of 0.74 for ULS and 0.67 for UAV- 
SfM (Table 4). However, the correlation across sites varied considerably, 
with a range of R2 = 0.46–0.90 for ULS and R2 = 0.21–0.85 for UAV-SfM 
(Table 4). Similarly, the mean accuracy of RMSE values were moder­
ately high, with a mean RMSE of 2.13 cm (11 %) for ULS and 2.05 (11 %) 
for UAV-SfM, and a range of 0.33–7.24 cm (7–24 %) for ULS, and 
0.35–6.15 cm (8–17 %) for UAV-SfM (Table 4). Predictions were least 
accurate at Tarawera (R2 = 0.46 for ULS, 0.21 for UAV-SfM, RMSE =
4.79 cm (10 %) for ULS, 6.15 cm (12 %) for UAV-SfM; Table 4). These 
results demonstrate similar performance of predictions from both data 
types (Table 4, Fig. 6). There was also a general trend for the sites with 
the lowest predictive accuracy to be more mature, whilst the sites with 
highest predictive accuracy were generally young (<7 years old) with no 
or little weed cover.
Table 4 shows crown metrics with the highest variable importance 
from the RF estimation process for each dataset. Crown area 
(cnx3D_area, cvx2D_vol) and crown volume (cnx3D_vol) metrics as well 
as the total number of points within each individual tree point cloud 
consistently appeared as the most important predictor variables. It is 
notable that 3D crown area was a more important variable for the 
models from UAV-SfM data at younger sites, such as Scion South, Scion 
North, and Rangipo, whereas 2D crown metrics were more important for 
the mature sites, Schnapper Road, Kaki Road and Tarawera (Table 4).
Through examination of the influence of site characteristics on the 
model performance, several distinct relationships can be identified. 
Model performance generally declined with the age of the trees being 
captured, with strong correlations of R2 = 0.71 and 0.69 for UAV-SfM 
and ULS datasets, respectively (Fig. 7a). Modest correlations were also 
observed between model performance and canopy gap area (R2 = 0.28 
for UAV-SfM and R2 = 0.26 for ULS; Fig. 7b). Site conditions (logging 
debris and weed cover), initially assessed subjectively (Table 1), were 
objectively characterised using point height distributions analysed 
through histograms. The skewness of these histograms served as a 
suitable proxy for site conditions, showing a moderate relationship with 
subjective assessments (R2 = 0.50). This proxy also demonstrated 
modest to moderate correlations with model performance (R2 = 0.45 
and R2 = 0.32 for UAV-SfM and ULS respectively; Fig. 7c). It is also 
notable that ULS was less impacted by deteriorating site conditions than 
SfM (Fig. 7c.) and to a lesser extent stand age (Fig. 7a) and canopy 
openness (Fig. 7b).
The number of observations used in the DBH prediction training 
process did not have a strong effect on model performance (Fig. 8). The 
model performance measures R2 and RMSE did not change when fewer 
than the total available number of trees were used in the training

4. Discussion
4.1. DBH estimation
Overall, the results of this study demonstrated that DBH can be 
derived from UAV point clouds with a moderately strong precision 
(mean R2 = 0.71) and accuracy (mean RMSE = 2.09 cm (11 %)) 
compared to field-measured DBH (Fig. 6 and Table 4). The consistent 
performance in R2 and RMSE by the model when using as few as 200 
observations indicates that the model is robust to variations in the 
number of trees used for training and does not require the whole dataset 
to achieve a high level of prediction accuracy for most sites (Fig. 8). The 
results of this study align with the general findings in existing literature, 
which reported a range of R2 from 0.45 to 0.91 (Dalla Corte et al., 2020; 
Guerra-Hern´andez et al., 2019; Guerra-Hern´andez et al., 2017; Hayashi 
et al., 2014; Iizuka et al., 2017; Jakubowski et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2018; 
Persson et al., 2002; Popescu, 2007; Yao et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2011) and 
RMSE% values from 5 to 20 % (Cosenza et al., 2018; Dalla Corte et al., 
2020; Guerra-Hern´andez et al., 2019; Guerra-Hern´andez et al., 2017; Liu 
et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2023; Persson et al., 2002; Popescu, 2007; 
Vauhkonen et al., 2010; Yao et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2011). In addition, it 
is notable that the results of this study were not consistent across 
different sites, with some falling within the upper range of results re­
ported in the literature and others falling short of the lower end (R2

ULS consistently outperformed UAV-SfM, on an ideal site such as Scion 
North or Scion South, characterised by an open canopy and the absence 
of tall objects that could introduce individual tree segmentation errors, 
both UAV-SfM and ULS generally performed at a comparable and ac­
curate level at most sites.
Clear trends were observed between model performance at each site 
and the age of the trees (Fig. 7a), as well as the extent of canopy closure 
and site conditions (weed cover and logging debris) at each site (Fig. 7b 
and 7c respectively). This aligns with the previous findings, where the 
lowest reported RMSE% (5 %; (Guerra-Hern´andez et al., 2017)) was 
observed in a study with the smallest trees (mean height per group of 
9.16–10.04 m), and the highest RMSE% values reported by studies with 
mean tree heights of 16.53 m (RMSE% = 20.35 %; (Yu et al., 2011)), and 
19.72 m (RMSE% = 18 %; (Popescu, 2007)). To the best of the authors’ 
knowledge, no other study has attempted to assess the performance of 
DBH predictions across such a varied range of tree sizes at this scale 
before, therefore, the findings of this study provide valuable insights for 
foresters interested in applying these techniques across their forests at 
different age classes. The novel technique of using point height histo­
grams to predict site conditions could also be valuable for those wanting 
to get an idea of how accurate DBH predictions could be for a given site. 
Future research should further explore this method as it could have 
additional uses for predicting weed cover, inferring site nutrient levels 
based on weed cover, or measuring the amount of harvest residues.
It was also notable that ULS was less impacted than UAV-SfM by site 
conditions and stand age (Fig. 7). This could be due to the tree canopies 
not being accurately resolved by the imagery, which has been attributed 
to shadow causing issues in the 3D reconstruction of the SfM process 
(Wallace et al., 2016). Unlike ULS, SfM is a passive technology and so 
the reconstruction of tree canopies would be less detailed in more 
complex forest conditions, such as closed canopies. This theory is sup­
ported by our results which showed that key metrics for predicting DBH, 
3D crown volume and area (Table 4), were more important in younger 
stands, where the canopy would be more open and the reconstruction of 
the overall tree structures would be more complete (Fig. 5), compared to 
older stands where the canopy closure was greater and 2D crown area 
became more important.
When assessing the performance of the two sensor types, the average 
results across all sites for each sensor produced comparable accuracies 
(R2 = 0.67/RMSE = 2.05 cm (11 %) and R2 = 0.74/RMSE = 2.13 cm (11 
%) for UAV-SfM and ULS, respectively). However, at each site, ULS 
consistently provided the best results. It was interesting to note that, for 
both sensor types, the best results were seen in sites that had little to no 
weed cover (e.g., Rangipo, Scion North and Scion South), or had recently 
been thinned, i.e. the canopy has been opened (e.g., Kaingaroa BC55_2). 
The worst results for both sensor types were seen in mature sites (e.g., 
Scion Block 16, Tarawera and Kinleith Schnapper Road). A clear trend 
emerged in which the model performed better on younger sites with 
minimal weed cover or logging debris, or in more mature sites where the 
canopy was relatively open (Kaingaroa BC55_2). Model performance 
was superior at young sites with higher levels of logging debris and weed 
cover, than on mature sites, implying that age class had a stronger in­
fluence on results (Fig. 7a and c). Canopy closure is noted as a major 
challenge for individual tree detection in forests (Zhen et al., 2016b), 
which could explain these results. In this study, canopy openness was 
demonstrated to have a moderate level of correlation with model per­
formance (Fig. 7b), implying that a clear view of the extent of each tree 
crown was crucial to accurate crown delineation. This was supported by 
the predictor variables of importance included in the RF models, which

The results of this study indicated that the model accuracy was 
reduced and bias (MBE) increased through the inclusion of forked trees 
in the dataset (Fig. 9). This was likely due to the irregular and larger 
crown size of a forked tree falsely inflating the predicted value of the 
DBH for those trees. This would be a greater mismatch for trees that 
forked below 1.4 m, where the field-measured DBH for one of the forked 
stems would typically be smaller than the DBH measured on a tree of a 
similar size that forked above 1.4 m. It is possible that the irregularity of 
these tree crowns could be exploited to develop a model capable of 
predicting the presence of codominant leaders, something the authors 
believe has not previously been attempted using remote sensing. Aside 
from the potential for increasing the accuracy of DBH predictions for a 
given site, this method could also be useful for the digitalisation and 
mechanisation of forestry operations (Stewart and Hartley, 2022). The 
pre-selection of trees for removal can reduce the possibility of errors in 
the field, and laser technologies have been highlighted as a means of 
expediting the process (Gomide et al., 2014). Developing a model to 
predict codominant leaders would facilitate a remotely-sensed pre-­
thinning assessment, enabling the automation of thinning operations, 
and removing the requirement for real-time, in-situ computations (da 
Silva et al., 2023). Future research should further explore methods for 
predicting codominant leader trees to improve detection capabilities.
Despite the encouraging results observed in this study, it is worth 
considering whether remote and proximal sensing tools will still be used 
in the future to estimate DBH when more holistic and representative 
metrics, such as crown volume, are so accessible (Åkerblom and Kaita­
niemi, 2021). Although useful for standardising measurement, DBH was 
a practical measurement in the past when it was not possible to measure 
whole tree crowns or conduct 100 % samples during inventory, how­
ever, UAV technologies have now enabled these measurements. Future 
research should be focused on how these new UAV-derived metrics 
perform in predicting the metrics that foresters are truly interested, such 
as stem volume and carbon content.
4.2. Height measurement
The height measurements derived from both ULS and UAV-SfM 
methodologies align with previous studies employing these technolo­
gies for tree height measurement across diverse forest environments and 
age classes, including P. radiata plantations. Previous research has 
indicated that ULS outperforms UAV-SfM in measuring tree heights in 
young plantations (Hartley et al., 2020). The results of the current study 
support that study, extending its findings to mature plantations, as it was 
observed that, a model developed from the combined UAV-SfM dataset 
had lower accuracy (RMSE = 1.23 m (10.93 %); Table 6), than ULS 
(RMSE = 0.55 m (7.42 %); Table 6). This trend was even more pro­
nounced for predictions averaged across all sites (R2 = 0.66, RMSE =
1.21 m (11.59 %); and R2 = 0.84, RMSE = 0.68 m (5.4 %) for UAV-SfM 
and ULS, respectively; Table 6). This aligns with the literature and the 
reported range in the coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.85–0.99; 
(Dalla Corte et al., 2020; Gyawali et al., 2022; Hartley et al., 2020; Hu 
et al., 2020; Jaakkola et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018)) and accuracy (RMSE 
% = 2.83–7.97 %; (Dalla Corte et al., 2020; Dash et al., 2019a; Hartley 
et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2020; Jaakkola et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018; 
Rodríguez-Puerta et al., 2021; Wallace et al., 2014)) for heights 
measured by ULS, compared to the coefficient of determination and 
accuracy of UAV-SfM (R2 = 0.21–0.99 and RMSE% = 1.89–19.4 %; 
(Birdal et al., 2017; Brieger et al., 2019; Dandois and Ellis, 2013; Dan­
dois et al., 2015; Fankhauser et al., 2018; Fawcett et al., 2019; Guer­
ra-Hern´andez et al., 2017; Gyawali et al., 2022; Hentz et al., 2018; 
Huang et al., 2019; Iizuka et al., 2017; Karpina et al., 2016; Krause et al., 
2019; Lin et al., 2018; Lisein et al., 2014; Lisein et al., 2013; Moe et al., 
2020; Ni et al., 2015; Ota et al., 2017; Puliti et al., 2015; Shin et al., 
2018; Surový et al., 2018; Yurtseven et al., 2019; Zainuddin et al., 
2016)).
When assessing the sensor types individually, ULS generally fell 
within the range for accuracy and coefficient of determination reported 
in the literature, aside from the lower values observed at the four mature 
sites (Table 6). As observed with the DBH prediction results, age and site 
conditions can impact on the quality of results, with mature sites 
exhibiting higher RMSE and lower R2 values. An increase in absolute 
height error with height categories was observed to be contrary to the 
decrease in percentage height error with height categories (Fig. 11). This 
relationship was observed in previous studies of the same species 
(Hartley et al., 2020; Rodríguez-Puerta et al., 2021), especially the 
decrease of relative RMSE with age, although this is the first time that 
this trend has been observed over the entire rotation age of a forestry 
species, such as P. radiata. While there could be several reasons for this 
observation, the most plausible explanation is the dense understory of 
ferns present at these mature sites, which could contribute to the 
elevated RMSE values through errors within the DTM. Another study 
also noted a correlation between increasing flight altitude and RMSE 
(Chen et al., 2022), which could be another explanation for this lower 
performance as the mature sites in this study were captured at approx­
imately double the altitude of the other sites (Table 3). The apex of trees 
of this form (i.e., conical and apically dominant), is often very slender 
too, depending on the growth status of the current year’s flush at the 
time of capture. This small target would likely contribute to height er­
rors because objects smaller than the beam footprint of the lidar lead to 
inaccuracies in the location of the return (Wilkes et al., 2021), poten­
tially explaining why the difference between shorter and taller height 
categories was more pronounced for the absolute than the percentage 
error.
Trends for the precision of UAV-SfM data are more pronounced, 
however, there is a modest correlation (R2 = 0.38) between the absolute 
RMSE of height measurements and the GSD of the imagery of the UAV- 
SfM data. Previous research has found a strong correlation (R2 = 0.97) 
between point cloud density and altitude (Dandois et al., 2015), which 
has a positive relationship with point cloud completeness (Frey et al., 
2018). This potential for incompleteness in the upper canopy of the 
UAV-SfM point clouds could explain the discrepancy between field- and 
UAV-SfM-measured heights. The two lowest observed absolute RMSE 
values for height (Tarawera and Kinleith Schnapper Road, 2021) were 
also both for the mature sites, which as with other sites, both used a ULS 
DTM, therefore it is highly likely that any error impacting on the ULS 
heights caused by understory, would be translated to the UAV-SfM 
heights through the use of the same DTM.
5. Conclusions
This study evaluated the effectiveness of two UAV technologies, ULS 
and UAV-SfM, for inventory of a major global plantation forestry spe­
cies, Pinus radiata. ULS consistently outperformed UAV-SfM in tree 
height measurements, suggesting its robustness in diverse conditions 
and age classes. Both methods were more accurate for predicting DBH in 
younger stands and stands with a higher percentage of canopy gaps; 
however, UAV-SfM was more sensitive to age and site conditions. Stand 
maturity influenced measurement reliability in both data sources, with 
measurement error noticeably improving over time as trees matured and 
crown structures evolved. These findings underscore the importance of 
accounting for site-specific variables when selecting UAV technologies 
for forest inventory and making forest management decisions based on 
UAV-derived forest inventory data.
By employing a fully automated data processing pipeline with min­
imal manual input, this study demonstrated that UAV-based forest in­
ventory is nearing operational readiness for large-scale use by foresters. 
Its strengths are most apparent in early rotation stages that are crucial 
for silvicultural operations, site productivity assessments, and yield 
forecasting. As the cost of UAV sensors continues to decline, foresters 
can better assess the trade-offs between measurement accuracy and

sensor cost.
It is also important to consider the ability of UAVs to operate at scale 
and provide wall-to-wall forest information. Although UAV-derived 
DBH had a lower accuracy than field measurements, UAV remote 
sensing facilitated tree-level data capture across entire stands, capturing 
variability within the stand, in contrast to traditional field measure­
ments, which are typically limited to plot-level sampling.
In conclusion, UAV-based forest inventory is reaching a robust stage 
where foresters should consider its benefits for accurate, large-scale, 
individual tree measurement and it is hoped that this study, through 
its in-depth evaluation of accuracies and limitations, will encourage 
more adoption of these tools in operational forestry.
