Diameter, height and volume increment single tree models
for improved Sitka spruce in Great Britain
Rubén Manso1,*, Rachael Davidson1 and J. Paul McLean1,2
1Forest Research, Forest Resources and Product Development Group, Northern Research Station, Roslin, Midlothian EH25 9SY, UK
2Norwegian Institute of Bioeconomy Research, Division of Forest and Forest Resources, Høgskoleveien 8, 1433 Ås, Norway
*Corresponding author. Tel: +44 (0)300 067 5979; E-mail: Ruben.Manso@ForestResearch.gov.uk, rmgforestal@hotmail.com

The British forestry sector lacks reliable dynamic growth models for stands of improved Sitka spruce, the most
important commercial forest type in Great Britain. The aim of this study is to ﬁll this gap by trialling a new
modelling framework and to lay the foundations of a future dynamic growth simulator for that forest type. First,
we present single tree diameter and height increment models that are climate sensitive and include explicit
competition effects. The predictions from the increment models are pooled to project diameter and height at a
given age. These projections are then used as inputs to an integrated taper model from which stochastic tree
volume predictions are obtained. Retrospective data from over 1400 trees collected in two extensive genetic
trials in Scotland and Wales were used for the purposes of this study. Diameter increment and height increment
predictions were highly accurate and diameter and height projections proved consistent. The predicted volume
at the time of harvesting also exhibited a high degree of accuracy, which shows the robustness of our approach.
Further data will be needed in the future to recalibrate the present models and extend their range of validity to
the whole of Great Britain.
Introduction
Sitka spruce (Picea sitchensis (Bong.) Carr.) is the most important
commercial species in Great Britain, amounting to 50 per cent
of the total conifer area in the country and supplying 60 per
cent of the national timber production (Forestry Commission,
2020). Much of the research around forest biometrics and timber
properties carried out in the last decade in Great Britain has
focused on Sitka spruce (Achim et al., 2006, Auty et al., 2012,
Butler et al., 2010, Fonweban et al., 2011, Hale et al., 2009, Manso
et al., 2020, Minunno et al., 2010).
Sitka spruce has also been a focus of breeding programmes
conducted by the Forestry Commission, the British forest service,
since the 1950s. As a result, signiﬁcant genetic gains in terms of
growth have already been achieved for the species in the earlier
cycle of tree breeding (Hubert and Lee, 2005), and further gains
are expected as the improved material obtained from controlled
crosses (i.e. both parents are known and deliberately paired, as
opposed to the less selective seed orchard material) is being
increasingly used. These gains have been so substantial that
there is concern that the old yield models developed by the
Forestry Commission (Edwards and Christie, 1981, Matthews et
al., 2016) might not accurately model improved stands. This
could pose a considerable challenge for forecasting timber pro-
duction, as most of the Sitka spruce forests planted in the past
20 years originate from improved material.
One solution would have been to build dedicated growth
models for improved Sitka spruce with repeated measurements
data from permanent sample plots (PSP). However, this is not
yet possible because the network of PSP monitored by Forest
Research does not yet contain enough repeated measurements
of improved Sitka spruce. An alternative to obtain more data
in the short term whilst a strategy is set to extend the PSP
would be to use a retrospective analysis of existing trees, such as
measuring ring widths and inter-whorl distances to infer diam-
eter increments and height increments, respectively. These are
common techniques in the ﬁeld of forest ecology (e.g. Calama
and Montero, 2005), timber properties (Bergsten et al., 2001) and,
obviously, dendrochronology (e.g. Martín-Benito et al., 2010).
Although this kind of analysis has limitations, particularly in the
absence of mortality records, the data generated could prove
useful to set the basis of a modelling framework for improved
Sitka spruce, which could be revisited and updated when and if
more complete data are provided. Further, the timing of silvicul-
tural thinnings to promote stand stability is not dependent on
mortality, but rather on individual tree size. Models constructed
in this way can immediately be used to guide mid-rotation for-
est management and to examine volume production from any
operations.
Another advantage of using retrospective analysis is that
diameter and height increments are systematically obtained on
a yearly basis. Besides the obvious gain in precision, this can help

to efﬁciently capture climatic signals, as dendrochronological

In 2017, a large scale project was launched, which aims to
develop genomic prediction techniques for further improvements
of British Sitka spruce (Tumas et al., 2021). The project extensively
sampled two large genetic trials established in 1996 by Forest
Research. These trials were originally aimed at selecting the most
promising genotypes resulting from controlled crosses of the
British breeding population of elite trees. These trials included a
range of genotypes that are characteristic of the improved mate-
rial that has been widely planted. During that project, approxi-
mately 1400 trees were felled and phenotyped for both volume
yield and wood property traits. An additional opportunity was
taken to conduct retrospective analysis on the stems, which pro-
vided tens of thousands of diameter and height growth records.
The main objective of the present study was to develop single
tree diameter and height increment models for improved Sitka
spruce in Great Britain using the aforementioned data to inform
and test a new modelling framework for improved stands that
can be implemented in growth simulators. Because simulators
are ultimately aimed to project volume into the future, the diam-
eter and height increment models were used to dynamically
predict diameter and height over time. These predictions were
subsequently used to estimate volume statically through an inte-
grated stem taper model. Secondarily, we used this opportunity
to strengthen the notion of uncertainty in the British forestry
sector by making the models fully stochastic and to explore
the role of climate in Sitka spruce growth by including climatic
predictors in the models.
Materials and methods
Data
The data were obtained from two Sitka spruce full sibling progeny
trials established in 1996 by Forest Research. The sites were
located at Kintyre (Scotland; Lat: 55.36◦, Lon: −5.64◦; 60 m
above sea level) and Brecon (Wales; Lat: 51.89◦, Lon: −3.33◦;
375 m above sea level). Both sites are representative of typical
Atlantic climatic conditions such as an absence of severe summer
droughts and mild temperatures across the year. Despite
this pattern, the Brecon site tends to exhibit more extreme
temperature values than the Kintyre site as a consequence
of its greater altitude and continentality (Pyatt et al., 2001).
Additionally, the trees at Brecon were relatively more exposed
to wind than those from Kintyre according to the detailed aspect
method of scoring (DAMS, Quine and White, 1994). DAMS can
be accessed at http://www.forestdss.org.uk/geoforestdss/. A
summary of several climatic variables for both sites is shown in
Table 1. These climatic data were gathered through the software
ClimateEU v4.63 (Wang and Hamann, 2012). The soils types
and soil cultivation methods were, respectively, mineral gley
and shallow double-mouldboard ploughing in Kintyre; and forest
brown earth and scariﬁcation in Brecon.
A total of 50 full siblings obtained from controlled crosses
from selected Sitka spruce trees were trialled across both sites.
We will refer to each of these full siblings as ‘families’ hereafter.
An additional non-improved specimen was included as a control
family, which was excluded from the analysis for the purposes
of this study. These 51 families (i.e. 50 full siblings plus control)
were replicated at least 60 times at each site by means of 60
square adjoining blocks. Each block consisted of a 64 tree array
that included all the families at least once. The individuals were
planted following a completely randomized scheme within each
block. The initial square-spacing was 1.8 m in Kintyre and 2 m in
Brecon.
All trees were harvested in August 2017 in Brecon and in
September 2017 in Kintyre. A random sample of 14 trees from
each family was initially selected for analysis, which amounted
to a total of 700 trees per site, although a few individuals were
lost due to mistakes at harvesting (20 in Kintyre and 17 in Brecon).
Stem height, stump height and diameter at breast height (DBH,
1.30 m) were measured for all the selected and successfully
retrieved trees with a measuring tape. Height increments were
also obtained by tape-measuring the inter-whorl distances, from
top to bottom. Additionally, two perpendicular diameter mea-
surements were taken along the stem at 1 m intervals with a
caliper. The taper of the trees was assessed as the series of the
mean diameter at each of these cross-sections.
A 1 m log that included the breast height cross-section was
chainsawed from each of the selected trees and taken to the
laboratory for further analysis. A 15 cm thick disc at breast
height (i.e. upper side 1.30 m off the ground) was then extracted
from each log and then processed into 2 cm slices. The internal
structure of these slices was captured through X-ray computed
tomography (CT) scanning at 0.1 cm resolution, using the equip-
ment and services of Scotland’s Rural College. These images were
analysed with the software WinDENDROTM v.2012b to obtain ring
widths.
Height increment chronologies were straightforwardly gener-
ated, as the year of the last height increment (2017) was known
and could be used as the reference. Trees with missing tops were
discarded from the analysis (9 and 36 trees in Kintyre and Brecon,
respectively). In addition, height increments at the base of all
trees (i.e. below breast height) were also disregarded as they
were not fully reliable. This is because the scars left by the whorls
are less evident as the scar itself ages. We do not expect that
omitting early height information will have any signiﬁcant impact
in practice, as growth models are typically initialized with data
from trees with non-zero merchantable volume (i.e. breast height
and have been reached and DBH is larger than some predeﬁned
value).
Ring-width chronologies posed more issues because (1) the
outermost rings from many trees were not distinguishable in
the CT images, and (2) there was some uncertainty about the
actual age at which trees reached 1.30 m in height (‘DBH age’
hereafter), which impedes the ability to reliably assign a year to
the innermost ring. This uncertainty is due to the lack of accuracy
of the early height increments mentioned above. Consequently,
we lacked a clear reference to correctly date the rings in many
cases.

In order to generate reliable ring-width chronologies, we ﬁrst
selected all complete ring series (i.e. the outermost rings were
distinguishable) to create a master series for each site. Each
incomplete series was then cross-dated against its respective
master series. To do this, the Spearman’s rank correlation coef-
ﬁcient (ρ) was calculated for each pair incomplete series-master
series. This was done considering 0, −1, 1, 2 and 3 years lags,
i.e. we assumed that the DBH age of the incomplete series could
be the same, 2 years less, or 1–3 years more than that of the
master series, respectively. The lags corresponding to the two
larger ρ were retained and their chronosequences were visually
checked. As a ﬁnal check, the series were compared against our
own assessment of DBH age based on the aforementioned height
increment records and against an early tree height assessment
carried out in 1999, where available. Trees with very poor CT
imaging were discarded from the analysis.
The cross-dated ring-width chronologies and the height
increment chronologies were then compiled to create DBH
increment and height increment datasets with 1-year non-
overlapping growth intervals. The last growth interval was
removed from the Brecon site, given that the growing season was
not complete. The ﬁnal dataset included more than 20 000 DBH
and height increments from 1292 and 1249 trees, respectively,
excluding the control family. At the time of harvesting, the mean
and standard deviation of the DBH and height of the selected
trees were, respectively, 17.75 (3.60) cm and 17.51 (1.67) m in
Kintyre, and 20.76 (4.21) cm and 14.06 (1.40) m in Brecon. Further
details can be found in Table 2. The DBH increment dataset is
annexed to this paper as Supplementary material.
Modelling developments
Modelling approach
We chose to model DBH and height increments on individual
trees using difference growth equations, which are derived
from static growth equations in integral form. Those difference
equations can be arranged so that the current growth rate or
increment be a function of the current age and the current size
of a given organism (Zeide, 1993, Zeide, 1993) also found that
most of the resulting equations are particular cases of either of
these two general equations:
y = α1yβ1tγ1
(1)
y = α2yβ2eγ2t
(2)
where y is current increment, y is current size, t is current total
age, and α1,2, β1,2 and γ1,2 are generic estimable parameters
that result from the rearrangement of the parameters from the
original functions. Note that β > 0 and γ < 0.
These forms are convenient as growth is decomposed into
an expansion component, which is a power function of current
size (i.e. yβ1), and a decline component, which is either a power
function of age (i.e. tγ1) or an exponential function of age (i.e.
eγ2t).
We used these equations for the purposes of this study.
DBH increment models. Model speciﬁcation
After some preliminary tests, we concluded that Equation 1
represented DBH increment better than Equation 2. This basic
form could not, however, capture all the variability in DBH incre-
ment, with competition and climatic effects being likely over-
looked. These effects can be considered by expanding some of
the parameters of Equation 1. Because early growth is mostly
controlled by genetic and site characteristics in planted forests
and competition and climate act as limiting factors, it makes
sense to expand parameter γ1, in the decline component, to take
these limiting factors into account.

Given the fact that our trees were selected at random and
attention was not paid to all the neighbouring individuals, vari-
ables such as basal area or the basal area of trees larger than
the current tree, which are typically used to take competition into
account, make little sense here. We used the quadratic mean
diameter (dg) of all the studied trees (included the control) as
a proxy instead. One additional advantage of dg is that it can be
interpreted in the context of the self-thinning theory. Concerning
climate, we tested a range of annual variables, as the time step
in our data was 1 year. We focused on those covariates that
could be used as proxies for known limiting factors in Sitka spruce
DBH growth such as summer drought and late frost (Cannell and
Smith, 1984, Mason et al., 2012, Meason and Mason, 2014, Mur-
ray et al., 1994). The following covariates were speciﬁcally trialled:
mean maximum temperature in summer and the standardized
precipitation evapotranspiration index (SPEI; Vicente-Serrano et
al., 2010) (drought), mean minimum temperature in spring (late
frost), and other variables informing more general characteristics
of the growing year, like the mean annual temperature or total
annual precipitation. The variables from each group of proxies
were sequentially tested.
Additionally, we took into consideration the effect of the lag to
reach DBH age with respect to the earliest DBH age in both sites (3
years). This basically tests whether a delayed early growth has an
effect on late growth. We assumed that this effect could affect
any of the parameters in Equation 1.
We would ideally have ﬁtted a single DBH increment model
for both sites, with some site speciﬁc parameters in it. However,
the DBH growth pattern of the Brecon site exhibited an unusual
behaviour until 2004 (t = 8, see Figure 1), with a very slow and
constant growth rate apparently not dominated by age. After this
time, growth adopts the pattern that would have been expected
from the beginning. This behaviour is likely due to the type of
soil cultivation in this site and could not be accommodated by
the functional forms of the selected models, where increments
steadily increase to a maximum in the ﬁrst few years. As a
consequence, we ﬁtted independent models for each site. We
used a form of segmented regression for the Brecon site to
accommodate the pattern described earlier. Before deﬁning the
preliminary formulation of our models, let i, j and k be the indexes
for the tree, the family, and the time interval, respectively. Let
also d be the index for ‘DBH’ and h for ‘height’. To distinguish
between sites, an apostrophe is added to all estimable terms for
the Brecon site.

Height increment models. Model speciﬁcation
The functional form in Equation 2 was better suited to model
height increments than that of Equation 1. Again, we ﬁtted inde-
pendent height increment models for the Kintyre and Brecon sites
despite the fact that height increment did follow a signiﬁcantly
similar pattern across sites. This was only made for practical
reasons, such as the computation of joint simulations with the
DBH increment models. In this case, competition effects were
discarded after some preliminary tests and only climatic effects
were considered in the age-related limiting component of the
models. The same group of covariates as in the DBH increment
models were used here.

Model calibration and autocorrelation issues
The previous preliminary models were ﬁtted sequentially by ﬁrst
testing the lag effect and the structure of the segmented regres-
sion where appropriate. Next, the competition effect was con-
sidered. Last, the climatic effects were added. At each iteration,
the normalized residuals were visually checked against the ﬁtted
values and the different covariates in use or to be tested. Het-
eroscedasticy issues were also checked in this step. The variables
were kept according to their improvement in the Akaike Informa-
tion Criterion (AIC).
Once a satisfactory parameterization was reached, we
addressed the issue of autocorrelation. Given the hierarchical
structure of our data, it could reasonably be expected that the
assumption of independence between errors does not hold. This
is speciﬁcally the case for the repeated measurements taken
within a given tree. Correlation could also be expected at the
family level. Because trees were selected at random across
the experiment, correlation at the block level should not be an
issue. In order to relax the assumption of independence, we
ﬁrst implemented a continuous-time autoregressive correlation
(CAR) structure in the variance covariance matrices of the errors
at the tree level. This structure is deﬁned such that  = σ 2
ϵ C,
where σ 2
ϵ is a generic term for the residual variance and C is
a correlation diagonal block matrix with non-zero elements
equal to θ t, with θ an estimable parameter and t the time
interval between observations within the same tree. We also
included random effects at the family level associated with
different model parameters. Although the number of families is
theoretically ﬁnite, the potential number of controlled crosses
is large enough as to treat the family as a random effect.
Random effects were assumed to be normally distributed such
as b ∼N(0, ), where b is a generic vector of random effects
and  is the variance covariance matrix of the random effects,
with diagonal elements σ 2

relevance of using these techniques was evaluated by comparing
the AICs of the models with and without the CAR structure
and random effects. The normalized (Cholesky) residuals and
the signiﬁcance of all variables were checked again once a
satisfactory correlation structure was in place.
All parameters were estimated through the maximization of
their likelihood function. All models were ﬁtted with package
nlme (Pinheiro et al., 2019) in R (R Core Team, 2020).

by data from large geographical areas and better age range
coverage, include a mortality module and take competition into
account (e.g. Fortin and Langevin, 2010, Hasenauer et al., 2006,
Pretzsch et al., 2002, Salminen and Hynynen, 2001, Soares and
Tomé, 2003, amongst many others). All these problems need
to be addressed to upscale, complete and update the current

From a functional standpoint, the developments presented
here can be seen as a simulator consisting of a set of two dynamic
submodels that predict DBH and height increments from an initial
list of trees plus a static submodel that takes DBH and height
projections to predict tree volume. Ideally, the DBH and height
increment submodels should have been ﬁtted simultaneously
to better capture the uncertainty of the two processes, as the
errors of these models are likely to be correlated. This could have
been done through Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR Gal-
lant, 1987). A requirement of this technique is that the datasets
containing each of the responses have the same number of
records. Due to the relatively large number of non-overlapping
missing data in both datasets, using SUR would have meant
discarding a lot of valuable information to ﬁt the models (some
20 000 records), and therefore we chose the non-simultaneous
approach developed in this paper. The SUR approach is not to be
overlooked in the future though should enough data be available.
We developed our dynamic models through difference equa-
tions, i.e. we modelled discrete increments in DBH or height. This
approach has been widely used in growth models within forestry,
and it produces dynamic outcomes by deﬁnition (e.g. Fortin et al.,
2008). A different line of research has alternatively focused on
creating dynamic models by integrating differential growth equa-
tions (García, 1983, Ochi and Cao, 2003). These models directly
predict the variable of interest (and not its increment) from
an given observation. Similar functional forms can be achieved
through the so-called generalized algebraic differential approach
(GADA, Cieszewski and Bailey, 2000). Regardless of the approach,
projecting a variable into the future implies that a prediction error
is generated at each time step. These errors need to be propa-
gated if an accurate notion of the uncertainty of the predictions
is to be obtained. Working out analytical expressions for the prop-
agated errors at the end of the simulation for these integrated
dynamic models is not a simple task. García, (1983) and Rennolls,
(1995) deducted such error term after arduous calculations and
only for a very basic version of their stand-level models. To the
best of our knowledge, no GADA modelling exercise has ever
taken error propagation into account. In contrast, our approach
allowed us to efﬁciently propagate errors from all sources (resid-
uals, random effects, parameter estimates) through the whole
projection of the variables of interest by means of stochastic
simulations. The expected uncertainty only departed from the
observed uncertainty in the case of the DBH projection in Kintyre.
This could be due to the occurrence of very high DBH increments
in the early stages, which the model may not have been able
to capture, leading to a slight underestimation of the actual
conﬁdence intervals.
An additional advantage of our modelling approach is that
the functional form of the models could easily be rearranged
in terms of growth expansion and growth decline components
(Crecente-Campo et al., 2010, Nunes et al., 2011, Schröder et al.,
2002, Zeide, 1993), which allows making ecologically sensible
choices when expanding the model parameters to include dif-
ferent effects. This is in contrast to other more empirical incre-
ment models where competition and climatic variables enter the
models in a less meaningful way (Girard et al., 2014, Manso et al.,
2015).
Focusing on competition, we used the quadratic mean diam-
eter of the selected trees, dg, as a proxy. This is because it was
not possible to calculate a true competition index, as the trees
had been selected at random. The closer we could have got
to a true competition index was to use the mean dg of the
selected trees in each block, which is a common approach when
data come from typical forest inventory plots (e.g. Calama and

Montero, 2005). However, our blocks were adjacent and we felt
they could not be seen as plots in that classical sense (e.g. the
quadratic mean diameters of two contiguous blocks could be
equally representative for a tree in the limit of the two blocks).
The speciﬁc functional form for the competition term is such
that the growth decline component of the model becomes less
than one—i.e. growth is less than the maximum potential for
a given size and age—when some dg is reached given a set of
climatic conditions. If we take the example of Kintyre and use
the mean value of each of the climatic variables involved, it is
possible to see that growth decrease would roughly take place
when dg ∼12 −13 cm. This corresponds to an age of 12 or
13 years. Our data clearly shows that canopy closure must have
occurred at age 11–12 at that site, which suggests that this event
can successfully be approximated by our model. Another way to
think of this is that the self-thinning line was being approached at
these ages as a result of a fast initial DBH increment in a context
of low mortality.
In terms of climate, we used proxies for summer drought
(minimum SPEI) and late frost (mean minimum temperature in
spring), as these are factors known to negatively affect Sitka
spruce in Great Britain (Cannell and Smith, 1984, Mason et al.,
2012, Meason and Mason, 2014, Murray et al., 1994). These
factors can be seen as age-dependent and limiting for growth,
hence they were included in the decline component of the model.
Our ﬁndings using the above-mentioned proxies suggest that
these factors have a negative effect on growth and are consistent
across sites and the studied response. The mean annual tem-
perature only has a signiﬁcant effect in DBH increment. Inter-
estingly, warmer years hindered DBH growth in Kintyre whilst
they favoured it in Brecon. A possible explanation could be that
Kintyre is a less extreme location than Brecon. We interpret that
warm years may imply a certain strain on water resources in
Kintyre, whereas they may reduce the negative effect of altitude
in Brecon. It is important to note though that our time series are
relatively short when considering our limited geographical scope
and therefore caution needs to be observed when interpreting
these results.
Site effects were evident when looking at the data and the
model predictions but could not be taken into account in the
model directly. Technically, it would have been possible to include
any site effects by expanding the constants αd and αh but the
inference would have not been valid as we only had two sites.
For example, the delayed DBH growth observed in Brecon with
respect to Kintyre was potentially related to soil cultivation where
the less intrusive scariﬁcation may have led the root system of
trees in Brecon to take longer to develop than in Kintyre. However,
a dummy variable ‘soil cultivation’ would have amount to ‘site’.
Another example is the effect of wind in the taper of trees. It
is well known that trees exposed to frequent gusts above the
average wind background tend to be more tapered than more
sheltered trees (Bonnesoeur et al., 2016). In this respect, the
Kintyre site was relatively less exposed than the Brecon site and
their trees were also slenderer. Although the wind hypothesis
makes sense, our ﬁndings cannot be conclusive as other site
factors such as the slightly different spacing between sites may
have had a role in shaping the form of the trees.
Most studies do not consider tree effects other than the cur-
rent size in growth models. One legitimate reason for this is that
there are usually not many variables that are measured at the
tree level. In contrast, we had the genetic family as individual
tree information. Our aim in this study was to model the general
growth trend of controlled crosses improved individuals and not
differences between families, which will form part of a dedicated
study. However, the possible correlation caused by this unac-
counted effect had to be dealt with. We then considered the
family as a random effect in contrast to studies that focus on
genetic gains (e.g. Zas et al., 2004). This was done through the
expansion component of the models as the genetic origin is inde-
pendent of time. The low share of the total variance attributable
to the random effects at the family level showed that most of
this variability was accounted for by the current size of the tree,
which means that measuring DBH or height at a given age should
give already a good estimate of tree DBH or height in the future
regardless of the family.
This simpliﬁes the way the model can be used for the two dif-
ferent modalities of genetic improvement in British Sitka spruce,
which depend on the reproductive material used (Hubert and
Lee, 2005). On the one hand, seeds originating from clonal seed
orchards through open pollination (half siblings) lead to moder-
ate gains in volume and have widely been used as a result of
their relatively low production cost. On the other hand, individuals
originating from controlled crosses (full siblings), like those used
in this study, present the advantage that the top families can
be selected, which results in even greater genetic gains. This
approach is increasingly being used in new plantations. Given that
most of the family effect is implicit in the current size of the tree
(DBH or height) at a given age in our models, the information
about the genetic origin should be of lesser importance and the
models would be safely applicable with both genetic types.
Most growth models ultimately aim at predicting timber vol-
ume. Volume is often assessed statically in single tree models,
usually by feeding DBH predictions into volume tariffs (e.g. Fortin
and Langevin, 2010). We also resorted to a static approach when
we used our DBH and height predictions to estimate the taper
of the trees and then to estimate the volume by integrating the
taper, like in Fortin et al., (2013). This volume was slightly under-
estimated at both sites, although the bias was not signiﬁcant and
therefore this apparent consistency across sites may be spurious.
It seems nevertheless important to highlight that there was a
trend to overestimate small DBHs and to underestimate large
DBHs at both sites. Although these over and underestimations
nearly cancelled out to yield virtually unbiased DBH predictions
overall, the trend may have had some impact on volume predic-
tions, as positive errors in DBH would result in positive errors in
volume that may not be compensated by their negative coun-
terparts due to the non-linearity of the taper function (Fortin
and Langevin, 2012). This is an aspect that needs to be revisited
when more data are available and the models are used to project
volume over later ages.
A seeming drawback with our models from a practical point
of view is the difﬁculty to measure individual heights at any
given age to initialize the simulations. Manual height assessment
is expensive and the accuracy could be low, particularly at the
typical high stand densities for the species in Great Britain [over
2000 stems ha−1; (Matthews et al., 2016)]. Airborne LiDAR is a
precise and more affordable alternative, at least when scanning
relatively large areas. Although the identiﬁcation of individual

Conclusions
We set in this paper the methodological bases to accurately
model tree-level growth in improved British Sitka spruce. Given
the high commercial value of improved stands of the species in
Great Britain, these developments were most needed and are
likely to make an signiﬁcant impact on the British timber sector
once more data are available to upscale the model predictions.
The biological basis of our increment models, the aggregation
of these increments into volume through a sensible simulator
structure, the consideration of climatic effects and the use of ret-
rospective data makes the approach presented here speciﬁcally
suitable to predict, after calibration, tree growth in fast growth
planted forests of any species and under any conditions.

Data availability
Part of the data underlying this article (DBH increments) can be
found as Supplementary material. The rest of the data (height
increments) will be shared on request to the corresponding
author with permission of the partners of the Sitka spruced
project.
