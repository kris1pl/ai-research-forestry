A B S T R A C T
Accurate estimation of tree diameter at breast height (DBH) is essential for forest monitoring, biomass modeling, 
and carbon accounting. While DBH is traditionally measured in the field, this approach is labor-intensive and 
costly, especially at large scales. In contrast, tree height can now be efficiently obtained from remote sensing 
platforms such as airborne LiDAR and photogrammetry, creating opportunities to estimate DBH indirectly. To 
address this, we developed a species-specific nonlinear framework to predict DBH from tree height and stand- 
level relative density (RD) in the mixed temperate forests of New Brunswick, Canada. Our analysis used 1807 
trees from 653 permanent sample plots (1985–2014), representing six dominant species: Abies balsamea, Acer 
rubrum, Acer saccharum, Picea mariana, Picea rubens, and Picea glauca. Allometric (height-only) models explained 
part of DBH variation, with R² ranging from 0.15 to 0.35 (broadleaves) and 0.41–0.74 (conifers), but predictive 
accuracy was notably low for Acer rubrum and Acer saccharum. Incorporating RD as a competition index sub­
stantially improved model performance, with R² increasing to 0.85–0.89 (broadleaves) and 0.72–0.88 (conifers). 
Prediction errors (RMSE and MAE) consistently decreased, with broadleaves showing the greatest improvement 
compared to conifers, reflecting their stronger sensitivity to stand density. These findings demonstrate that 
combining tree height with RD provides reliable estimates of DBH across diverse species. The framework bridges 
ground-based inventory with remote sensing applications, offering a scalable approach for biomass estimation, 
stand density analysis, and sustainable forest management in temperate mixed-species forests.
1. Introduction
Measuring tree diameter at breast height (DBH) is a cornerstone of 
forest monitoring, growth modeling, and biomass estimation. DBH plays 
a pivotal role in forest management plans, carbon stock assessments, and 
wood inventory calculations. Although traditionally measured using 
simple field tools such as measuring tapes or calipers, DBH collection 
can be labor-intensive and challenging, particularly in remote or densely 
vegetated forests. In contrast, tree height is now widely accessible via 
remote sensing technologies, including airborne LiDAR, drone-based 
LiDAR, and photogrammetry, offering high accuracy over large 
forested landscapes (Hyypp¨a et al., 2008; White et al., 2013).
This difference in measurement accessibility has opened new ave­
nues for indirectly estimating DBH from tree height using statistical 
modeling frameworks.
The height–DBH relationship has been extensively studied in 
forestry. Historically, DBH has been the independent variable used to 
predict tree height, due to its ease of field measurement and strong 
correlation with height (Huang et al., 1992; Fang and Bailey, 1998). For 
example, Huang et al. (1992) and Peng et al. (2001) developed and 
validated nonlinear height–diameter models for major species in Alberta 
and Ontario, respectively. Temesgen et al. (2014)
analyzed 
height–diameter relationships for multi-species, multi-layered forests in 
Northeast China, while Fang and Bailey (1998) developed robust models 
for tropical forests. These studies have shown that DBH can explain over 
80 % of the variation in height for many forest types.
However, the reverse modeling approach—predicting DBH from tree 
height—has received significantly less attention. With the rise of remote 
sensing technologies, height has become a readily available and 
spatially continuous variable, offering a valuable opportunity to esti­
mate DBH in areas where field access is limited. Studies by White et al. 
(2013) and Hyypp¨a et al. (2008) demonstrated that LiDAR-derived

direct DBH measurements remain unattainable from above-canopy 
remote sensing, especially in closed-canopy and multi-layered forests. 
Consequently, reliable models that estimate DBH from height can serve 
as critical links between remote sensing and traditional forest inventory 
methods.
Several recent studies have begun to address this challenge. For 
instance, Zhang et al. (2016) modeled DBH from height in mixed conifer 
forests in the western United States, incorporating stand structure and 
species variation. Similarly, Chave et al. (2005) incorporated height and 
wood density into allometric models for DBH and aboveground biomass 
estimation in tropical forests. Despite these efforts, most models remain 
species-specific or ecosystem-limited, with limited application to cold, 
mixed-species forests like those found in New Brunswick, Canada. 
Moreover, many models have overlooked the influence of structural 
variables such as relative density (RD), which plays a significant role in 
modifying the height–DBH relationship.
Relative density, defined as the ratio of actual stand density to the 
maximum stand density for a given size class, reflects inter-tree 
competition for resources like light and water. In denser stands, trees 
often invest more in vertical growth to access light, leading to taller but 
thinner stems (i.e., lower DBH for a given height) (Zhang et al., 2005). 
Zhang et al. (2005) showed that incorporating RD in height–diameter 
models significantly improves predictive accuracy, particularly in 
complex, multi-layered forests.
New Brunswick’s forests, covering more than 85 % of the provincial 
landbase, contain a rich mix of economically and ecologically important 
species such as Balsam Fir (Abies balsamea), Red Maple (Acer rubrum), 
Sugar Maple (Acer saccharum), Black Spruce (Picea mariana), Red Spruce 
(Picea rubens), and White Spruce (Picea glauca) (Porter et al., 2001; NB 
Ecosystem Classification Working Group, 2003). These species differ in 
growth form, shade tolerance, and morphological responses to stand 
structure. As a result, general DBH–height models may be insufficient 
across these diverse forest types. Developing species-specific models 
based on measurable variables like height and RD is therefore crucial, 
particularly for integrating LiDAR data into operational forest 
management.
The objective of this study is to develop accurate, species-specific, 
and scalable nonlinear models for predicting DBH using tree height 
and relative density in the mixed-species forests of New Brunswick. The 
specific goals are to: 
1. Develop nonlinear DBH prediction models using height and RD as 
independent variables;
2. Evaluate model performance across six dominant tree species (both 
conifers and broadleaf);
3. Assess species-level differences in model accuracy and suitability for 
remote sensing applications.
The findings of this study are expected to facilitate the integration of 
remote sensing and field-based forest inventories, providing robust tools 
for forest structure analysis, carbon estimation, and climate adaptation 
strategies in boreal and temperate forests.

PSPs, more than 165,000 individual trees, and 580,000 tree measure­

It is important to note that the classical self-thinning slope (β ≈
–1.605) reported by Reineke (1933) was developed for even-aged, pure, 
and fully stocked stands with uniform crown structure. In contrast, the 
PSP stands used in this study are mixed-species and multi-cohort forests, 
where canopy stratification, uneven age structures, and interspecific 
competition lead to different density–size dynamics. As demonstrated in 
previous research, the self-thinning slope is not universal, and typically 
becomes less negative in structurally heterogeneous or mixed stands due 
to reduced or delayed density-driven mortality (Weiskittel et al., 2009; 
Charru et al., 2012; Pretzsch and Biber, 2016).
2.4. Model development
To predict tree diameter at breast height (DBH), a nonlinear 
regression model was developed and applied separately to each of the 
six selected species: Balsam Fir (Abies balsamea), Red Maple (Acer 
rubrum), Sugar Maple (Acer saccharum), Black Spruce (Picea mariana), 
Red Spruce (Picea rubens), and White Spruce (Picea glauca). The model 
was designed to examine the relationship between DBH and two 
explanatory variables: tree height and relative density (RD). Each spe­
cies was modeled individually to account for species-specific growth 
patterns and structural differences.
The general form of the nonlinear model used is as follows: 
DBH= (1+K0) × HEIGHTKh × RDKr                                                
(4)
Where:
- DBH = Diameter at breast height (cm)
- HEIGHT = Total tree height (m)
- RD = Relative density (unitless)
- K₀, Kₕ, Kᵣ = species-specific regression coefficients
This multiplicative power-law formulation was selected based on 
ecological and statistical considerations. In mixed-species and multi- 
cohort forest stands, such as those represented in the PSP data, trees 
of similar height may exhibit different DBH values due to variation in 
crown competition intensity and stand developmental stage. Under 
these conditions, parameter-varying extensions of the classical allome­
tric model (e.g., a(RD), b(RD)) often suffer from parameter non- 
identifiability and collinearity between HEIGHT and RD, leading to 
unstable coefficient estimation (Zhang et al., 2005; Temesgen et al., 
2014). In contrast, the chosen model maintains parameter stability 
while allowing HEIGHT to represent vertical growth allocation and RD 
to represent competition-driven suppression of diameter growth. This 
interpretation is consistent with self-thinning and crown competition 
theory (Reineke, 1933; Drew and Flewelling, 1979).
The model was fitted using nonlinear least squares (NLS) in R for 
each species. The goodness-of-fit was evaluated using R², root mean 
square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). Residual plots 
were also examined to assess model assumptions and potential bias. (all 
parameters (DBH, HEIGHT and RD values were derived directly from 
PSP data).
2.5. Model selection and statistical analysis
The nonlinear regression model selected for this study was chosen 
based on both scientific and practical considerations:
- Capturing Nonlinear Relationships
Tree growth involves complex, nonlinear interactions between 
structural variables such as tree height and relative density (RD). 
Traditional linear models fail to capture these curvilinear patterns, 
whereas nonlinear models provide greater flexibility to describe such 
natural relationships accurately.
- Species-Specific Flexibility
The model structure allows for species-specific parameterization, 
enabling independent coefficient estimation for each of the six dominant 
species. This design facilitates precise comparison of allometric patterns 
across different coniferous and broadleaf species in New Brunswick’s 
forests.
- High Predictive Accuracy
Due to its multiplicative-exponential structure, the model provides 
high accuracy in estimating DBH from measurable inputs like tree height 
and RD. This is particularly useful when analyzing heterogeneous 
datasets from mixed-species forests.
- Compatibility with PSP Data
The model leverages high-resolution, long-term data from New 
Brunswick’s Permanent Sample Plot (PSP) network. These datasets 
include detailed measurements on tree size and stand structure, 
providing a reliable basis for model calibration and validation.
2.6. Statistical methods and software
All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 
4.4.2; R Core Team, 2023). Parameter estimation was conducted using 
nonlinear least squares (NLS), and 95 % confidence intervals were 
calculated for each model coefficient. Data preprocessing, including 
relative density calculations and PSP data organization, was carried out 
in Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 (Microsoft Corporation, 2023).
The following statistical tests were applied to analyze interspecies 
differences among model coefficients and input variables:
- Shapiro–Wilk Test: Used to assess the normality of each variable. A 
p-value > 0.05 indicates normal distribution, while a p-value < 0.05 
suggests non-normality.
- ANOVA (Analysis of Variance): For variables that followed a normal 
distribution, one-way ANOVA was applied to compare means across 
species.
- Kruskal–Wallis Test: For non-normally distributed variables, the 
non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used to detect significant dif­
ferences between species groups.
Dunn’s Test with Bonferroni Correction: When either ANOVA or 
Kruskal–Wallis identified significant differences (p < 0.05), post-hoc 
pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test. Bonferroni 
adjustment was applied to control for Type I error due to multiple

3. Results
3.1. Descriptive structural characteristics of six dominant species
To provide a baseline understanding of interspecific differences, 
descriptive statistics were calculated for diameter at breast height 
(DBH), total tree height, stand density (N/ha), trees per plot, and rela­
tive density (RD) across the six dominant tree species in New Brunswick. 
A complete summary of these metrics is provided in Table A1 in Ap­
pendix A, while the distributions of DBH, height, and RD are visualized 
in Fig. 2. These metrics reflect the structural heterogeneity of forest 
stands and provide the empirical foundation for subsequent model 
development.
Among the selected species, Balsam Fir (FB) had the largest sample 
size (N = 514, across 193 plots; 2.66 trees/plot), highlighting its 
dominance in New Brunswick’s forest composition. In contrast, White 
Spruce (SW) had the smallest sample size (N = 105, across 41 plots; 2.56 
trees/plot), reflecting its relatively lower representation in the PSP 
dataset compared to other species.
In terms of tree diameter, White Spruce (SW) exhibited the largest 
mean DBH (28.36 cm ± 8.34), while Balsam Fir (FB) had the smallest 
(20.55 cm ± 6.33). Regarding tree height, Sugar Maple (MS) recorded 
the greatest mean tree height (19.02 m ± 3.74), whereas Balsam Fir 
(FB) showed the shortest average height (15.38 m ± 3.96), consistent 
with its known shade-tolerant and slow-growing characteristics.
Relative density (RD) varied notably among the six dominant tree 
species. Balsam Fir (FB) recorded the highest mean RD (0.62 ± 0.17), 
reflecting its tendency to grow under crowded conditions with stronger 
intraspecific competition. In contrast, Black Spruce (SB) showed the 
lowest mean RD (0.53 ± 0.16), indicating reduced competitive intensity 
and lower realized stand density. The other species exhibited interme­
diate values: Red Maple (MR: 0.59 ± 0.20), Sugar Maple (MS: 0.58 
± 0.23), Red Spruce (SR: 0.55 ± 0.19), and White Spruce (SW: 0.60 
± 0.22), highlighting differences in ecological strategies and stand oc­
cupancy. Note that RD is calculated at the plot level and is shared among 
all trees within the same plot. The RD values reported for each species 
represent the average RD of the plots in which that species occurred, and 
therefore do not reflect species-specific RD calculations. This approach 
ensures that RD captures the combined crowding pressure of both intra- 
and interspecific competition within mixed stands.
These interspecific variations underscore the structural complexity 
of New Brunswick’s mixed-species forests and justify the use of species- 
specific nonlinear models to better represent their growth dynamics. The 
distribution patterns shown in Fig. 2 reinforce these findings, with 
boxplots highlighting the broader spread in DBH for Sugar Maple (MS), 
the consistently smaller dimensions of Balsam Fir (FB), and the rela­
tively high density of FB compared to the lower density of Black Spruce 
(SB). In terms of height, Sugar Maple (MS) exhibited the tallest in­
dividuals, while Balsam Fir (FB) was the shortest, consistent with their 
contrasting growth strategies.
3.2. Estimated self-thinning parameters (α and β)
The estimated self-thinning parameters (α and β) exhibited clear 
interspecific variation (Table A2 in Appendix A). Among the six domi­
nant species, the highest α value was observed for Black Spruce (SB: 
99.45), indicating its relatively high maximum stand density potential, 
while the lowest α occurred in Red Maple (MR: 62.64). For the self- 
thinning coefficient (β), values ranged from –0.05 in SB, suggesting 
minimal self-thinning tendency, to –1.075 in Sugar Maple (MS), which 
was closest to the classical –3/2 self-thinning rule. Balsam Fir (FB), Red 
Spruce (SR), and White Spruce (SW) exhibited intermediate β values 
(–0.734 to –0.765), consistent with moderate density-dependent mor­
tality. These results highlight species-specific differences in density 
regulation and competitive dynamics.
3.3. Statistical analysis results
In this study, various statistical tests were used to analyze data 
related to DBH (Diameter at Breast Height), Height, and RD (Relative 
Density) across different species in the forests of New Brunswick. 
Initially, the normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk 
test. Then, to compare means between different species, the Krus­
kal–Wallis test and Dunn’s test (with Bonferroni method) were applied. 
Below are the detailed steps of the analysis.
3.3.1. Normality check with the Shapiro-Wilk test
The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data for 
DBH, Height and RD. The results indicated that none of the variables 
followed a normal distribution (all p < 0.05; Table A3 in Appendix A). 
Therefore, non-parametric tests were applied for subsequent group 
comparisons.

differences for all variables (p < 0.05; Table A4 in Appendix A). To 
further explore these differences, Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjust­
ment was conducted for pairwise comparisons.
3.3.3. Dunn’s Test with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons
After the Kruskal–Wallis test, Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjustment 
was applied to conduct detailed pairwise comparisons among species. 
For each comparison, the test statistic and adjusted p-value are reported 
(Tables A5–A7 in Appendix A). An asterisk (*) denotes statistical sig­
nificance at α = 0.05.
Significant interspecific differences in DBH were observed

other species. Red Maple (MR) differed from FB, MS, SR, and SW, but did 
not differ from Black Spruce (SB). Sugar Maple (MS) differed from FB, 
MR, and SB, but showed no significant difference compared with SR and 
SW. SB differed from FB, MS, SR, and SW, but not from MR. SR differed 
from FB, MR, and SB, but not from MS or SW. SW differed from FB, MR, 
and SB, but not from MS or SR. Overall, FB had the smallest DBH, MS 
and SW had the largest, while SB and SR occupied intermediate posi­
tions, with MR being similar to SB.
Tree height also varied significantly among species (Table A6). FB 
was the shortest species. MR differed from FB, MS, SR, and SW, but not 
from SB. MS was taller than FB, MR, and SB, but did not differ from SR or 
SW. SB differed from FB, MS, SR, and SW, but not from MR. SR differed 
from FB, MR, and SB, but not from MS or SW. SW was taller than FB, MR, 
and SB, but did not differ from MS or SR Overall, FB exhibited the lowest 
heights, MS the greatest, and MR, SB, SR, and SW showed intermediate 
or overlapping height distributions.
Differences in relative density (RD) were less pronounced (Table A7). 
FB had higher RD than SB, SR, and MS, but did not differ from MR or SW. 
MR showed no significant differences with any species. MS differed only 
from FB. SB and SR differed only from FB. SW showed no significant

cies, with the exception of Balsam Fir (FB), which tended to occur under 
higher stand densities compared to Black Spruce (SB) and Red Spruce 
(SR).3.4. Nonlinear Regression Analysis of Tree Diameter Across 
Species.
To model tree diameter at breast height (DBH) as a function of 
structural stand variables, species-specific nonlinear regression models 
were developed using total tree height and relative density (RD) as 
predictors. The model followed a multiplicative power form: 
DBH= (1+K0) × HEIGHTKh × RDKr                                                     
This formulation allows for nonlinear scaling of DBH with respect to 
both tree height and competitive stand conditions, accounting for 
interspecific allometric variation and density-dependent growth pat­
terns. Each species was modeled independently to capture species- 
specific structural dynamics.
Parameter estimates, standard errors, t-values, and p-values for each 
model are summarized in Table A8 in Appendix A, along with model 
performance metrics including the coefficient of determination (R²), 
root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE).The 
results demonstrate that the nonlinear regression model effectively 
captures species-specific variation in tree diameter through the inclu­
sion of height and RD as explanatory variables. The intercept parameter 
(K₀) was statistically significant across all species, serving as a funda­
mental scaling constant in the model. The height coefficient (Kₕ) showed 
strong and statistically significant positive effects in five of the six spe­
cies, confirming that tree height is a dominant structural predictor of 
DBH. In the case of White Spruce (Picea glauca), the Kₕ value was lower 
and only marginally significant, suggesting weaker coupling between 
height and diameter in this species, potentially due to greater structural 
variability or environmental heterogeneity.
The relative density coefficient (Kᵣ) was negative across all species 
and statistically significant in most cases. This pattern supports estab­
lished ecological theory, indicating that higher competition levels (i.e., 
greater RD) are associated with reduced diameter growth for a given 
height. This density effect was most pronounced in Sugar Maple (Acer 
saccharum) and White Spruce, where Kᵣ values indicated steeper diam­
eter reduction under denser stand conditions.
In terms of model fit, Sugar Maple and Red Spruce exhibited the 
highest performance, with R² values of 0.89 and 0.88, respectively, and 
low RMSE and MAE values. The model performed less effectively for 
White Spruce (R² = 0.72), suggesting higher structural heterogeneity or 
the influence of unmodeled factors such as site quality, microsite vari­
ation, or genetic diversity. Nonetheless, the models provided acceptable 
levels of predictive accuracy across all six species.
Overall, these findings highlight the importance of species-specific 
nonlinear modeling in forest structure analysis.
3.3.4. Comparative statistical analysis of parameters
The Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed that regression parameters (K₀, Kₕ, 
Kᵣ) followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05), supporting the use of 
parametric tests (ANOVA) for interspecific comparisons (Table A9 in 
Appendix A).
The ANOVA results revealed a statistically significant difference 
across species only for the height coefficient (Kₕ, p = 0.0098), while no 
significant interspecific differences were observed for the intercept (K₀, 
p = 0.4921) or the relative density coefficient (Kᵣ, p = 0.1382) 
(Table A10 in Appendix A).
Given the significant result for Kₕ, post-hoc pairwise comparisons 
were performed. Dunn’s test indicated significant differences among 
most species pairs, except between Black Spruce (SB) and Red Maple 
(MR) (p > 0.05), and between Sugar Maple (MS) and White Spruce (SW) 
(p = 0.0543), where no significant differences were observed 
(Table A11 in Appendix A).
3.3.5. Comparative performance of height-only vs. height+RD models in 
predicting DBH
The comparative evaluation of Height-only and Height+RD models 
demonstrated distinct species-specific patterns in DBH prediction. For 
Abies balsamea, the Height-only model explained 74 % of the variation in 
DBH (R² = 0.74), with relatively high errors (RMSE = 4.12 cm, MAE =
2.89 cm). Incorporating RD improved the model to R² = 0.86, while 
reducing RMSE to 2.39 cm and MAE to 1.73 cm. A similar improvement 
was observed for Acer rubrum, where the Height-only model exhibited 
poor performance (R² = 0.35, RMSE = 5.44 cm, MAE = 3.90 cm), but 
accuracy increased substantially when RD was included (R² = 0.85, 
RMSE = 2.88 cm, MAE = 2.11 cm).
The largest improvement occurred in Acer saccharum, where the 
Height-only model performed very poorly (R² = 0.15, RMSE = 6.27 cm, 
MAE = 4.69 cm), but the inclusion of RD raised model fit to R² = 0.89 
and reduced errors by more than half (RMSE = 3.15 cm, MAE =
2.05 cm). Picea mariana also benefited strongly, with R² increasing from 
0.54 to 0.82 and errors decreasing from RMSE = 5.14 cm to 2.61 cm and 
MAE from 3.74 cm to 1.96 cm. In Picea rubens, the Height-only model 
showed weak predictive capacity (R² = 0.41, RMSE = 6.15 cm, MAE =
4.48 cm), but performance improved markedly when RD was added (R² 
= 0.88, RMSE = 3.01 cm, MAE = 2.16 cm). By contrast, Picea glauca 
showed the 402 smallest gain: R² increased only from 0.58 to 0.72, with 
modest reductions in error (RMSE from 5.91 cm to 4.42 cm; MAE from 
4.20 cm to 2.92 cm). Overall, these results confirm that RD substantially 
enhances predictive accuracy for most species, particularly broadleaves 
such as Acer saccharum and Acer rubrum, while its effect is comparatively 
limited for Picea glauca (Fig. 3).

Building on the comparative analysis presented in Section 3.5, the

4. Discussion and conclusion
4.1. Integrated analysis of height and relative density in DBH prediction
The nonlinear regression model developed in this study demon­
strated strong predictive ability for estimating tree diameter at breast 
height (DBH) using tree height and relative density (RD) across six 
dominant tree species in New Brunswick. However, the magnitude of 
predictive accuracy varied notably among species, reflecting differences 
in morphological traits, competitive strategies, and ecological adapt­
ability. The overarching goal of the modeling framework was to derive 
reliable DBH estimates from tree height—an inversion of the traditional 
allometric approach in which DBH is used to predict height. Historically, 
forest biometric studies have often relied on DBH as the primary inde­
pendent variable due to its ease of field measurement, but in dense or 
inaccessible forests, measuring DBH can be difficult or impractical. With 
the advent of modern remote sensing technologies such as airborne and 
drone-based LiDAR, tree height has become widely and accurately 
measurable across large spatial scales (McKenney et al., 2011). This shift 
creates a pressing need to establish robust equations for predicting DBH 
from height, thereby bridging the gap between remotely sensed canopy 
information and ground-based inventory data.
The results of this study demonstrate that tree height alone is a sig­
nificant predictor of DBH, with positive and statistically significant co­
efficients (Kₕ) in five of the six species. For Balsam Fir (Abies balsamea), 
the model performed well (R² = 0.86; RMSE = 2.39 cm; MAE =
1.73 cm), confirming consistent allometric scaling. Similar results were 
found for Black Spruce (Picea mariana, R² = 0.82) and Red Spruce (Picea 
rubens, R² = 0.88), which exhibited strong height–diameter coupling (Kₕ 
= 0.60–0.67), reflecting their predictable structural patterns under 
boreal and mesic conditions (Kwon and Reynolds, 2005). In contrast, 
Sugar Maple (Acer saccharum), despite showing the highest overall 
model fit (R² = 0.89), had a relatively lower height coefficient (Kₕ =
0.39), indicating that DBH development in this species may also depend 
on site quality, stand history, or gap dynamics (Thomas and Knowles, 
2007). Red Maple (Acer rubrum) displayed a strong height effect (Kₕ =
0.66), reflecting its ecological flexibility and ability to respond rapidly to 
changes in light availability (Wright et al., 2011). White Spruce (Picea 
glauca), however, exhibited the weakest relationship (R² = 0.72; Kₕ =
0.10), suggesting that other unmodeled factors—such as microsite 
variation, soil fertility, or genetic diversity—play a greater role in 
shaping diameter growth (K¨orner and Oechel, 2008). These results align 
with earlier studies showing that shade-tolerant species often decouple 
height and diameter growth under stress (Poorter and Bongers, 2006), 
and that species with broad ecological ranges, such as White Spruce, 
tend to show less predictable allometric relationships (Botkin, 1993; 
McKenney et al., 2011).
Although height proved to be an important predictor, the accuracy of 
height-only models was limited in some species, particularly for Sugar 
Maple, Red Maple, and White Spruce. When RD was introduced into the 
models, predictive performance improved substantially, confirming the 
critical role of competition in regulating DBH. The inclusion of RD 
consistently produced negative coefficients (Kᵣ), indicating that higher 
stand density suppresses diameter growth for a given height. The 
strength of this effect, however, varied by species. Sugar Maple (Kᵣ =
–0.45) and Balsam Fir (Kᵣ = –0.34) were most strongly affected, 
demonstrating that dense canopy conditions can significantly restrict 
radial expansion in shade-tolerant or conservative species (Poorter and 
Bongers, 2006; Thomas and Knowles, 2007; Spies and Helms, 2007; 
Kwon and Reynolds, 2005). White Spruce also showed a notable nega­
tive RD effect (Kᵣ = –0.35), although its overall model accuracy 
remained low, suggesting that structural plasticity and broad habitat 
distribution complicate diameter prediction (Botkin, 1993; K¨orner and 
Oechel, 2008). By contrast, Black Spruce (Kᵣ = –0.07) and Red Spruce (Kᵣ 
= –0.06) exhibited weak RD effects, likely reflecting their ability to 
persist in saturated or nutrient-poor soils where competitive pressure is 
naturally reduced (Viereck and Johnston, 1990). Red Maple showed 
moderate sensitivity (Kᵣ = –0.23), consistent with its mid-successional 
role and high plasticity in crown expansion (Wright et al., 2011).
The comparative evaluation of Height-only versus Height+RD 
models revealed clear gains in predictive accuracy across nearly all 
species. For instance, R² values for Sugar Maple improved from 0.15 to 
0.89, and for Red Maple from 0.35–0.85, while errors (RMSE and MAE) 
were reduced by more than half. Red Spruce and Balsam Fir also showed 
substantial improvements, whereas White Spruce exhibited only modest 
gains (R² from 0.58 to 0.72). These findings confirm that RD substan­
tially enhances predictive accuracy for most species, particularly 
broadleaves such as Sugar Maple and Red Maple, while its effect is 
comparatively limited for White Spruce. This pattern is consistent with 
the theoretical framework of Reineke’s self-thinning law (Reineke, 
1933) and with studies emphasizing the role of competition in regu­
lating diameter growth in shade-tolerant conifers and hardwoods (Spies 
and Helms, 2007).
When viewed across species, these results highlight important con­
trasts between conifers and broadleaves. Broadleaf species (Acer sac­
charum, Acer rubrum) were especially sensitive to RD, showing large 
improvements in model performance when stand density was consid­
ered. In contrast, some conifers (Picea mariana, Picea rubens) showed 
relatively stable height–DBH relationships, with RD playing only a 
minor role. Such differences underscore that shade tolerance alone does 
not fully explain competitive responses, as previously noted by Poorter 
and Bongers (2006); rather, structural and life-history traits must be 
considered within a species-specific and region-specific framework.
The integration of these findings with prior ecological modeling 
studies further supports the robustness of the approach. For example, 
Kwon and Reynolds (2005) reported that balsam fir and red spruce 
exhibit predictable height–diameter scaling under both light- and 
shade-limited conditions, findings echoed in the strong performance of 
our models for these species. Similarly, Botkin’s (1993) forest dynamic 
models emphasized the necessity of incorporating demographic traits 
and competition to accurately represent forest structure, a principle 
directly supported by the inclusion of RD in our models. Wright et al. 
(2011) showed that North American species shift ecological strategies 
along environmental gradients, a trend also evident in our results: red 
maple’s

From an applied perspective, these results hold significant implica­
tions for remote sensing and forest management. Since tree height can 
now be readily obtained from LiDAR and other drone and airborne 
sensors, species-specific height–RD models offer a scalable means to 
estimate DBH across large forested landscapes (McKenney et al., 2011). 
This is especially valuable in mixed and dense forests, where measuring 
DBH directly is costly, labor-intensive, or infeasible. Incorporating RD 
alongside height further improves accuracy, particularly for species with 
strong competition sensitivity such as sugar maple and balsam fir. At the 
same time, species like white spruce may require additional varia­
bles—such as soil properties, slope, or climatic indices—to achieve 
reliable predictions (Reich and Oleksyn, 2004). By combining remotely 
sensed canopy height models with ground-based competition indices, 
forest managers can better estimate stand volume, density, and carbon

In summary, this study provides one of the first comprehensive 
evaluations of DBH prediction models in the mixed-species forests of 
New Brunswick using tree height and relative density as predictors. The 
findings confirm that height alone is a powerful predictor, but that RD 
greatly enhances model performance by capturing the suppressive ef­
fects of competition. Species-specific differences in height–DBH 
coupling and RD sensitivity reflect divergent ecological strategies, 
ranging from conservative hardwoods like sugar maple to structurally 
plastic conifers like white spruce. These insights not only extend clas­
sical allometric and self-thinning theory (Reineke, 1933; Poorter and 
Bongers, 2006; Spies and Helms, 2007) but also demonstrate their 
practical relevance for modern forest inventory systems, particularly in 
the context of LiDAR-based remote sensing. It should be noted that this 
study does not directly utilize remote sensing data. However, the 
developed DBH prediction framework is fully compatible with remotely 
sensed height products (e.g., LiDAR- or photogrammetry-derived can­
opy height models), and therefore can be readily integrated into remote 
sensing–based forest inventory systems.
Recent advances in remote sensing have enabled species identifica­
tion at varying levels of taxonomic resolution in Atlantic Canada. Mul­
tispectral satellite imagery (e.g., Sentinel-2) combined with seasonal 
phenology metrics typically yields ~75–85 % overall accuracy at genus 
or species-group level, while airborne LiDAR fused with multispectral or 
hyperspectral data can achieve ~85–95 % accuracy for dominant can­
opy species when individual tree crowns are delineated. Classification 
performance tends to be higher for conifer vs. broadleaf separation, but 
greater confusion is reported among shade-tolerant conifers such as 
Abies balsamea, Picea rubens, and Picea glauca, where class-wise F1 scores 
can fall to ~0.60–0.80. In the context of applying our species-specific 
DBH models, such classification errors would not affect the height 
input, but may result in model-selection error (assigning a tree to the 
wrong species-specific equation). This may introduce bias in predicted 
DBH, especially for species pairs with differing competitive responses. 
To address this, we recommend (1) using species-group (e.g., conifer vs. 
broadleaf) model forms when species-level confidence is low, (2) 
incorporating LiDAR-derived structural features to improve species 
classification, and (3) propagating classification uncertainty using 
confusion-matrix-based sensitivity analysis when applying these models 
in operational remote sensing workflows.
By integrating field-derived stand density indices with remotely 
measurable tree height, this study advances a hybrid approach to forest 
modeling that bridges ecological theory and operational management, 
offering a scalable and species-sensitive framework for sustainable 
forestry in the 21st century.
