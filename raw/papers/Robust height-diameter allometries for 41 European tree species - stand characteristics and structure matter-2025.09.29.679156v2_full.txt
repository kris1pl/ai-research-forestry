Abstract 
33 
Tree height is a key variable for assessing forest functioning and resources (e.g volume, carbon stocks) at both 
34 
tree and stand levels. However, direct field measurements are costly and time-consuming. Developing accurate 
35 
and unbiased height-diameter allometries applicable over large spatial scales is therefore crucial for forest 
36 
research and management.  
37 
Based on the French National Forest Inventory, with 269,460 tree-height observations on 49,120 plots measured, 
38 
we developed generalized, species-specific height-diameter allometries that integrate stand dendrometrical 
39 
characteristics and stand structure. The models cover 41 European tree species and the four main stand 
40 
structures: even-aged, uneven-aged, coppice-with-standards and coppice. To enhance local accuracy, we 
41 
developed an optional recalibration method at the plot level, assessing how many additional trees should be 
42 
measured and which ones to select to maximize the improvement of the model. By integrating basal area as stand 
43 
density and quadratic mean diameter as an indicator of stand development stage, our models enable us to assess 
44 
the influence stand dendrometrical characteristics on these allometries and evaluate how different species 
45 
responded to competition.  
46 
Results indicate that with increasing competition, tree height tends to be higher for a given diameter, and that 
47 
stand structure significantly influences 28 out of the 41 species. Local recalibration showed that measuring just 
48 
one to six trees (among the largest and thinnest diameter) per plot reduced prediction error by 10 to 70%, 
49 
depending on species.  
50 
This study provides a useful, robust and scalable tool for forest research and management, for the most 
51 
widespread species in Europe, while offering precision for local applications. 
52 
 
53 
Keywords: Height-diameter allometry, European tree species, stand structure, National Forest Inventory, 
54 
nonlinear mixed effects model, local recalibration.

 Introduction 
95 
Forest management involves not only assessing existing resources but also evaluating how they have changed, or 
96 
will change, over time. In this respect, along with diameter, tree height is a key variable to assess forest ecosystem 
97 
functioning and forest ecosystem services such as carbon storage or wood production (Hunter et al., 2013; 
98 
Teobaldelli et al., 2009). Studying height variability across individuals can be key to understanding important 
99 
ecological processes, such as competitive and facilitation interactions between individuals.  
100 
However, accurately measuring tree height is often time-consuming and technically challenging especially on 
101 
slopes or stands with low visibility. As a consequence, tree height measurements are often performed on a limited 
102 
sample of trees.  
103 
 
104 
In biological organisms, allometries defines how the size of an organ changes in relation to the size of another one, 
105 
or to the size of the whole organism. These allometries are used to understand how variables are interconnected, 
106 
and can serve as an alternative for variables that are difficult to measure directly. In forestry, allometric models 
107 
can be used to estimate tree characteristics such as biomass (Pilli et al., 2006) or volume (Vallet et al., 2006), being 
108 
common examples. For these two allometric models, the most accurate models include height measurements, or 
109 
height estimates. Tree height can also be estimated indirectly through allometric relationships, most commonly by 
110 
using diameter at breast height (dbh), a variable that is much easier to obtain in the field (Ciceu et al., 2023).  
111 
 
112 
Height-diameter allometries are known to differ across species, due to biological constraints, limitations and 
113 
growth strategies inherent to each species (Vázquez-Veloso et al., 2025). Allometries can therefore be species-
114 
specific (Adame et al., 2008; Ciceu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 1992) or can take species characteristics directly into 
115 
account in the model (Sharma et al., 2019b). 
116 
It is also essential to consider the specific environmental conditions influencing individual growth. When trees 
117 
grow in proximity to other individuals, they compete for resources in different ways. In dense areas, where space 
118 
is limited, trees may allocate more resources to vertical growth, which increases height, rather than to radial 
119 
growth. This phenomenon can be integrated into allometric models by considering the social status of the tree 
120 
(Aussenac et al., 2023; Trouvé et al., 2015). Social status can be used per se in the models, or can be included as 
121 
a competitive index as in Mugasha et al. (2019). 
122 
The dendrometrical characteristics of the stand also play a role in shaping height-diameter allometries. Stand 
123 
density variables such as basal area, for example, are commonly used to account for competition. As a result, trees

126 
Ciceu et al (2023) highlighted that dominant height is used to reflect the effect of stand developmental stage in 
127 
most studies working on height-diameter allometry. However, to obtain this variable, the height of a subset of trees 
128 
must be measured. The widespread inclusion of dominant height in empirical models is likely due to the strong 
129 
correlation between dominant height and individual tree heights. Alternative variables, such as the quadratic mean 
130 
diameter, also reflect stand development stage (Aussenac et al., 2023; Ciceu et al., 2020; Feldpausch et al., 2011; 
131 
Gonçalves, 2022; Mugasha et al., 2019; Temesgen and v. Gadow, 2004).  
132 
The structure of the stand (even-aged, uneven-aged and other configurations) also influences height-diameter 
133 
allometry(Gonçalves, 2022). Different stand structures reflect different stand dynamics and result in diverse tree 
134 
diameter distributions. For instance, even-aged stands are characterized by individuals of similar ages and heights, 
135 
whereas uneven-aged stands exhibit more diverse ages and height structures, potentially resulting in different 
136 
allometric patterns.  
137 
 
138 
Throughout the diversity of environmental conditions and forest structures, the French metropolitan forest area is 
139 
covered by 58% of even-aged stands. This leaves 42% of the stands that are managed under other regimes such as 
140 
uneven-aged, coppice or coppice-with-standards systems (described in figure S1, Supplementary Materials). The 
141 
even-aged structure has been the focus of most studies on forest structure, as it is one of the most represented and 
142 
constitutes a simple silvicultural system (Bettinger et al., 2017). However, since structure plays an important role 
143 
in growth, especially with different competitive relationships, it is necessary to study how height-diameter 
144 
allometries are affected by each structure specifically. This could prevent overestimations or underestimations 
145 
when assessing forest resources (Gonçalves, 2022).  
146 
Eventually, allometric models can be developed at different scales, from local to continental. While large-scale 
147 
models can provide higher genericity, they may introduce estimation bias when applied to specific local conditions. 
148 
Studies by Sullivan et al. (2018), Mensah et al. (2018) and Kearsley et al. (2017) have shown that locally calibrated 
149 
models perform better than large scale models. To improve the genericity and performance of these models, it can 
150 
be beneficial to incorporate local factors such as soil fertility, water availability or climate, thus allowing the model 
151 
to adapt to specific conditions. 
152 
 
153 
In this study, we developed height-diameter allometries for 41 species. We hypothesized that this relationship 
154 
would be influenced by individual-tree and stand characteristics such as stand basal area, mean diameter and stand 
155 
structure (even-aged, uneven-aged, etc.). We attempted to provide the most practical and generic models possible,

160 
 
Measurement protocol 
161 
We used data collected by the French National Forest Inventory (NFI) between 2005 and 2022 (IGN, 2023). The 
162 
plots were located throughout the French metropolitan area, on a systematic sampling grid of 1x1 km. Each year, 
163 
one twentieth of the grid was sampled, representing approximately 6000 plots per year. The plots are composed 
164 
of three concentric subplots with 6-, 9- and 15-meter radii. Very small trees (dbh [7.5-12.5cm [ ) and small trees 
165 
(dbh [12.5-22.5cm [ ) are measured in the 6-meter radius subplots, medium trees (dbh [22.5 – 37.5cm [ ) are 
166 
measured in the 9-meter radius subplots, and large (dbh [37.5 – 52.5 cm [ ) and very large trees (dbh over 52.5 
167 
cm) are measured in the 15-meter radius subplots. To account for the concentric circle sampling design, the NFI 
168 
assigns a weight to each tree based on its diameter class (very small, small, medium, large and very large). These 
169 
weights are then used to calculate stand-level variables, such as quadratic mean diameter and basal area.  
170 
 
171 
Until 2013, height was measured for six trees per diameter class and per species. From 2014 to 2015, only one tree 
172 
was measured per category and per species. In 2016, the very-small-tree category was added and additional height 
173 
measurements were carried out on very small trees. During the inventories, height and diameter are measured, 
174 
along with some other tree variables such as the vegetative state (living or dead) or stem singularities (broken, 
175 
abnormal, pollarded). The complete measurement protocol is described in Dalmasso et al. (2014). 
176 
 
177 
From 2005 to 2013, the NFI classified stand structures in five categories based on the characteristics of the stand, 
178 
combining the regeneration method (coppice, coppice-with-standards, even-aged forest, uneven-aged forest, no 
179 
structure) and vertical distribution. From 2014 to 2022, the NFI used different stand structure classifications, which 
180 
we aggregated to distinguish four stand structures: even-aged (EA), uneven-aged (UA), coppice-with-standards 
181 
(CWS) and coppice (C) (aggregation criteria are described in Supplementary Materials, table S1).  
182

Validation datasets 
208 
In addition to our calibration dataset, we used two other independent datasets for validation purposes. These 
209 
datasets enabled us to test both the performance and the robustness of our models, and were not used for the 
210 
calibration. To assess prediction bias, we conducted a t-test on the residuals (observed minus predicted heights) 
211 
to determine whether the mean of the prediction error differed significantly from zero.  
212 
 
213 
The internal validation dataset contains NFI data collected in 2023 (IGN, 2023). This data was collected 
214 
following the same protocol as the calibration dataset. It includes a large number of height measurements from a 
215 
large number of species and for the various stand structures. By applying the same criteria as for the calibration 
216 
dataset, we selected 8,771 height measurements from 2,548 plots, covering 40 species.  
217 
This second dataset will be referred to as the NFI_2023 dataset, and is described in Supplementary Materials, 
218 
table S3.

3.2.2 Inclusion of stand variables in relation to tree growth and biological 
256 
constraints 
257 
When developing our models, we considered practical constraints related to fieldwork. Diameter measurements 
258 
for all trees can be rapidly and efficiently measured, and are necessary when evaluating stand characteristics such 
259 
as basal area and quadratic mean diameter. The use of a stand development stage variable, usually characterized 
260 
.

278

279

281

284

3.4.2 Sub-samples and performance evaluation 
335 
We estimated 𝜆𝑚,𝑗 with different samples of tree height. These samples varied in terms of size (number of trees) 
336 
and in terms of relative tree diameter. We selected one to six trees according to three sampling strategies: 
337 
selecting only the thinnest trees, only the largest trees, or a combination of both diameter classes. For this last 
338 
strategy, we evaluated two scenarios. In the first scenario, large-diameter trees were predominant, following the 
339 
distribution: 1 large; 1 large with 1 small; 2 large with 1 small; 3 large with 1 small; 3 large with 2 small and 4 
340 
large with 2 small. In the second scenario, small-diameter trees were predominant and distributed as: 1 small; 1 
341 
small with 1 large; 2 small with 1 large; 3 small with 1 large; 3 small with 2 large and 4 small with 2 large. 
342 
 
343 
We applied this method to all FFA dataset plots, that had at least 13 tree records. The six largest diameter trees 
344 
and the six thinnest, which were used for site parameter estimation (following the different combination 
345 
described above), were then removed from the dataset. We then calculated the RMSE associated to the height 
346 
prediction for each type of sub-samples, for all remaining individuals. We then identified the best diameter-class 
347 
combinations based on the root-mean-square error RMSE difference relative to (i) the RMSE obtained when 
348 
height was predicted using fixed effects only – and (ii) the RMSE obtained using the optimal site-parameter 
349 
value, where height was predicted using 𝜆𝑚,𝑗 as the site parameter and all available trees.  
350 
 
351 
The best performing combination was then re-applied to the FFA dataset. In this second step, only the trees 
352 
involved in the selected combination (ie., 6 trees of the largest, smallest or a mix of both), were removed from 
353 
each plot, instead of systematically removing the six smallest and six largest diameter trees. RMSE was then 
354 
calculated using all remaining individuals. To evaluate the efficiency of the local recalibration method, and 
355 
identify the optimal number of trees needed, we calculated the relative RMSE improvement (RRI) as the 
356 
difference between the fixed-effect model RMSE and the recalibrated model RMSE, divided by the fixed-effect 
357 
RMSE.

RMSE value, and sub-samples of one to six trees selected among the largest and smallest diameters with 
420 
predominant large trees, for the FFA dataset. RRI is calculated relative to the fixed-effects baseline. 
421 
 Discussion 
422 
 
Effects of stand characteristics on height-diameter allometry  
423 
5.1.1 Influence of stand structure 
424 
Stand structure had a significant effect on height-diameter allometry for 30 species. However, a few species, 
425 
such as Populus tremula, showed no significant parameter estimates for any of the stand structures. Overall, the 
426 
stand structure parameter estimates (figure 2) highlight the influence of forest management on allometric 
427 
relationships. 
428 
In coppice stands, results were consistent with the regeneration method (vegetative sprouting from existing root 
429 
system), which generally leads to reduced tree height growth compared to even-aged stands for a given diameter. 
430 
However, the diameter at half asymptotic height (dha) was often greater than in even-aged conditions. This shift 
431 
suggests a reallocation of resources toward radial rather than vertical growth, a pattern observed in previous 
432 
studies (Corcuera et al., 2006; Deforce and Haneca, 2015).  
433 
In uneven-aged stands, competition between individuals is generally reduced compared to that in even-aged 
434 
stands, because density is lower. As highlighted by del Rìo et al. 2019, higher competition leads to greater tree 
435 
heights for a given diameter, due to the increased drive for vertical growth under denser conditions. This pattern 
436 
is consistent with the fact that reduced competition, particularly for light in uneven-aged stands, allows 
437

438

439 
trees in even-aged stands. Our results for broadleaved species were consistent with this observation. However, 
440 
coniferous species showed a positive effect on asymptotic height, which can partly be explained by the 
441

442 
section of the curves where height is greater for even-aged stands even if the asymptotic height is lower (the 
443 
same effect as in Supplementary Materials, section F).  
444 
Additionally, our results indicate an increase in dha in uneven-aged stands, which is consistent with lesser light 
445 
competition compared to even-aged stands. Under high competition, such as in even-aged stands, trees prioritize 
446 
height growth over radial growth, shifting the allometry curve to the left. Therefore, for a given diameter (among 
447 
small trees), trees in uneven-aged stands tend to be shorter than those in even-aged stands (trends for parameters 
448 
𝑎 and 𝑏 in the HossfledII equation (Vallet and Perot, 2016)). 
449 
 
450 
5.1.2 Influence of basal area 
451 
Tree height for a given diameter can increase with stand density. Basal area (BA), often used as a proxy for stand 
452 
density, also serves as an index for competition among trees. As basal area increases, competition for resources 
453 
intensifies, and trees tend to allocate more energy to vertical growth in order to maintain access to light. 
454 
Consequently, for a given diameter, tree height is expected to be greater in denser stands, as highlighted by del 
455 
Río et al. (2019) and Gonçalves (2022).  
456 
In our models, this effect is reflected through an increase in asymptotic height and a corresponding decrease in the 
457 
diameter at half asymptotic height (parameters 𝑎𝑠2 and 𝑏𝑠2), consistent with our expectations. As a result, the 
458 
height-diameter allometric curve shifts upward: for a given diameter, predicted height increases with basal area 
459 
(Figure 2). However, this trend did not apply uniformly across all species. For Corylus avellana, Pinus halepensis 
460 
and Tilia cordata, basal area had a significant negative effect on asymptotic tree height. As illustrated in figure 
461 
S12 (Supplementary Materials, section F), in the case of Tilia cordata, the high effect of basal area over diameter 
462 
at half asymptotic height, associated with a lack of observation for large diameters, tended to bend the allometric 
463 
curve to the left, inducing a reduction in asymptotic height by mathematical construction. Hence, as for some 
464 
effects in uneven-aged stands, for the observed range of diameters of these species, the height is still higher with 
465 
increasing BA even if the asymptotic parameter is negative.

In addition to its effect on asymptotic height, increased competition directly influences diameter growth. As 
467

468

470 
5.1.3 Influence of quadratic mean diameter 
471 
Quadratic mean diameter provides insights into the developmental stage of the stand (Aussenac et al., 2023; 
472 
Sharma et al., 2019a). The silvicultural system applied to each structure will induce different growth dynamics. 
473 
For example, even-aged stands are typically allowed to grow for several decades, enabling trees to reach greater 
474 
heights and diameters. In contrast, coppice systems imply periodic cutting, and the trees regenerate from stump 
475 
sprouts, resulting in shorter growth periods, and more limited development in size. In both uneven-aged and 
476 
coppice structures, Dg values tended to remain relatively stable over time, due to the continuous or cyclical nature 
477 
of their management regimes. 
478 
Across all species studied, Dg had a positive effect on the asymptotic height in the height diameter allometry. As 
479 
Dg increased, the height asymptote also increased. This pattern aligns with findings in the literature, which report 
480 
a strong relationship between Dg, dominant height, and height-diameter allometry (Schmidt et al., 2018; Sharma 
481 
et al., 2019a). The increase in asymptotic height with Dg reflects a typical stand development trajectory, where 
482 
older and more developed stands support taller dominant trees. 
483 
 
484 
 
Optimization of model accuracy and predictive performance  
485 
5.2.1 Quality of the models 
486 
Using National Forest Inventory (NFI) data enabled the inclusion of a wide range of species and tree records, 
487 
covering diverse climatic, structural and edaphic conditions across the French mainland territory. The robustness 
488 
of the dataset increases the confidence in applying our models across Europe as well, as was proven possible for 
489 
studies on growth (Mahnken et al., 2022). Additionally, the large size of the dataset reduced the influence of 
490 
outliers during calibration, enhancing parameter stability and reliability. The RMSE obtained for each species are 
491 
in line with other studies, where the models selected had a RMSE between 1 and 3.5 meters (Adame et al., 2008; 
492 
Gómez-García et al., 2015; Mehtätalo et al., 2015; Vázquez-Veloso et al., 2025).  
493 
To assess the efficiency of including a stand structure effect in our models, we evaluated the RMSE associated 
494 
with applying the even-aged calibrated model to plots with different stand structures and compared it to 
495 
structure-specific model errors. Estimations were done using only fixed effects. The relative error difference

Limits 
524 
We chose to develop a single model structure that could be adapted to different stand types by adding structure 
525 
parameters. While this approach simplifies generalization and cross-species comparison, it may have limitations. 
526 
For example, coppice systems, which are managed through frequent cutting cycles, pose particular modeling 
527 
challenges and can complicate convergence when the same stand variables as those applied to other structures 
528 
are used. In uneven-aged stands, the management process tends to maintain basal area and quadratic mean 
529 
diameter within a narrow range, unlike in even-aged stands. Consequently, using the same variables to 
530 
characterize structurally distinct stands may reduce model relevance or performance in some cases. 
531 
We also used a single functional form across species, selected based on the overall AIC comparison. For a few 
532 
less-represented species, alternative functional forms would have produced lower AIC values. Developing 
533 
species-specific functions could improve model accuracy, but maintaining a consistent equation enables 
534 
parameter comparisons across species and the development of generic models for practical use. 
535 
We also used species-specific models to test each species’ response to competition and stand dynamics.  
536 
 
537 
 
Implications for forest modelling and management 
538 
The models developed in this study account for both stand structure and dendrometrical characteristics, 
539 
providing highly accurate height estimates across different management systems (even-aged, uneven-aged, 
540 
coppice and coppice-with-standards). This represents a notable advancement in modeling, and can improve 
541 
estimates of other critical forest variables such as timber volume and carbon stocks.  
542 
Our validation results show strong predictive performance in plots with characteristics similar to the calibration 
543 
dataset. In plots with high fertility or atypical conditions, applying local recalibration reduced bias (see practical 
544 
method in Supplementary Materials, section C). The number of trees required for recalibration can be adjusted 
545 
depending on the desired accuracy of the height estimates, but as few as one to four trees could already strongly 
546 
improve the results (Table 5).

549 
 Conclusion 
550 
 
551 
Based on data from more than 40,000 stands surveyed by the French National Forest Inventory, we developed 
552 
height-diameter allometries for 41 European species that take into account stand characteristics.  
553 
We found that stand structure (even-aged, uneven-aged, coppice-with-standards and coppice) did significantly 
554 
impact height-diameter allometry, as did the quadratic mean diameter and the basal area of the stand. Given the 
555 
large diversity of climates, stand structures and soil fertility present in the French metropolitan area, the models 
556 
developed here could be applied all over Europe. Moreover, to address the loss of local accuracy when applying 
557 
a large-scale model at stand level, we developed a recalibration method requiring only a few height 
558 
measurements on the largest trees in the stand. Measuring one to four trees can considerably reduce estimation 
559 
errors.
