A B S T R A C T
Accurate estimation of tree diameter at breast height (DBH) is essential for forest inventory, biomass assessment, 
and ecological monitoring. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have emerged as powerful tools for remote DBH 
estimation, yet challenges remain in accurately capturing stem dimensions from aerial perspectives. This study 
compares two approaches for estimating the DBH of pine trees: (I) a data fusion method that combines vertical 
and oblique UAV imagery to generate high-density point clouds, and (II) a Random Forest-based allometric 
modeling approach based on metrics derived from vertical UAV data. The first approach evaluated three fusion 
configurations combining vertical (90◦) and oblique (30◦, 60◦) imagery: S1 (30◦/90◦), S2 (60◦/90◦), and S3 
(30◦/60◦/90◦). The second approach (S4) employed a Random Forest regression model using features derived 
from vertical UAV data, including tree height and crown size metrics to estimate DBH. Results demonstrate that 
the geometric approach using all three viewing angles (S3) achieved the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.985, RMSE =
2.47 cm), followed closely by S2 (R2 = 0.949), indicating the effectiveness of multi-angle image integration in 
improving DBH prediction. S4, while less accurate (R2 = 0.824), provided moderately reliable estimates, offering 
a simpler and more scalable alternative in operational settings. In contrast, S1 significantly overestimated DBH, 
especially for larger trees, with a high positive bias and the largest RMSE. When evaluated across DBH size 
classes, S3 consistently outperformed other methods, with strong agreement across small, medium, and large 
trees. These findings highlight the value of oblique–vertical image fusion in enhancing DBH estimation accuracy, 
particularly when multiple viewing angles are used. While this study focuses on open-canopy pine stands, future 
research should assess these methods in denser forests and explore deep learning algorithms for DBH estimation 
from complex point clouds.
1. Introduction
Tree diameter at breast height (DBH) is a fundamental metric in 
forest science, serving as a primary input for estimating tree volume, 
aboveground biomass, carbon storage, and for modeling forest growth 
and stand dynamics (Shao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a). It is also a 
critical variable in national forest inventories, forest health assessments, 
and climate change mitigation strategies (Woodall et al., 2005). In field 
surveys, DBH is typically measured manually at 1.3 m above ground 
using either a diameter tape or calipers. While this method is straight­
forward, it is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and logistically chal­
lenging, particularly in dense, mountainous, or remote forest 
environments. Moreover, manual measurements are susceptible to 
human error and inconsistencies, especially in sloped terrain, 
multi-stemmed trees, or when determining the exact breast height. 
Studies have shown that DBH measurement errors can affect subsequent

Given these limitations, there is a growing need for scalable and efficient 
alternatives that can provide consistent and accurate DBH estimates 
across large areas, especially in the context of modern forest monitoring 
and precision forestry.
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have emerged as powerful tools 
for forest monitoring, offering high-resolution spatial data, operational 
flexibility, and cost-effectiveness compared to traditional ground-based 
surveys and airborne LiDAR (Anderson and Gaston, 2013; Dandois and 
Ellis, 2013). Although ground-based LiDAR systems such as terrestrial or 
backpack scanners can provide near-perfect DBH estimates with 
centimeter-level precision, their application is often constrained by cost 
(Dassot et al., 2011), labor intensity and time demands (Brede et al., 
2017), limited mobility in complex terrain (Xie et al., 2020), and 
reduced efficiency for large-area surveys. Notably, Brede et al. (2017)
demonstrated that UAV data can achieve DBH estimates comparable to 
terrestrial LiDAR, highlighting UAVs as a promising, less labor-intensive 
alternative for DBH estimation in situations where ground-based LiDAR 
or traditional field measurements are impractical. UAV-based digital 
aerial photogrammetry (DAP), in particular, has become a widely used 
method for generating three-dimensional (3D) point clouds that repre­
sent forest structure. Most UAV surveys rely on vertical (nadir) imagery, 
which is effective for capturing canopy features and generating canopy 
height models (CHMs). However, vertical imagery provides limited 
visibility of the lower stem and forest floor, resulting in sparse or 
incomplete point cloud data derived from UAV-DAP at breast height 
(Zhang et al., 2025). UAV-derived point clouds therefore face challenges 
due to canopy occlusion and reduced point density near the stem base, 
which complicates direct DBH extraction. These trade-offs highlight the 
need to explore whether multi-angle UAV imagery, such as 
oblique-vertical fusion, can mitigate some of these limitations and 
improve DBH estimation accuracy and reliability across different forest 
conditions.
One of the most widely used methods for estimating DBH from 
remotely sensed data is allometric modeling, which predicts stem 
diameter based on measurable tree attributes such as height, crown 
width, crown area, and crown volume (Yao et al., 2012; Iizuka et al., 
2022). These models are grounded in empirical relationships that link 
aboveground tree structure to stem dimensions and are especially 
valuable when direct observation of the stem is not possible. However, 
the effectiveness of allometric models is often limited by their sensitivity 
to site-specific and species-specific factors. Applying generalized equa­
tions across diverse forest types or ecological zones can result in sig­
nificant estimation errors. As noted by Maltamo et al. (2014), one of the 
key limitations of height-based DBH prediction is its reduced reliability 
for larger or mature trees. While tree height tends to plateau as trees 
reach maturity, stem diameter may continue to increase, leading to 
greater variability in DBH for trees of similar height. Additionally, the 
relationship between DBH and height can vary notably between forest 
stands, further complicating the use of uniform allometric equations.
In UAV-based studies, allometric models commonly rely on features 
extracted from vertical imagery, such as tree height from CHMs and 
crown attributes derived through segmentation techniques (Jia et al., 
2024; Kwon et al., 2024; Tinkham et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025). How­
ever, vertical imagery offers limited visibility of the lower stem, 
resulting in sparse point cloud data near breast height and making most 
UAV-derived DBH estimates indirect and potentially less accurate. To 
overcome this limitation, oblique imagery has been increasingly com­
bined with vertical views to enhance 3D reconstruction of tree stems. 
Oblique images provide side perspectives of tree stems, significantly 
improving point density around the lower stem and enabling more 
precise geometry-based estimation of DBH and other tree attributes (Ye 
et al., 2019; Zhou and Zhang, 2020; Lin et al., 2015, 2018, 2021). This 
fusion of vertical and oblique imagery has shown strong potential for 
producing detailed, high-resolution 3D models of tree structures from 
crown to base, thus improving the accuracy and completeness of DBH 
estimation. Previous UAV-based studies using oblique imagery have 
mainly focused on parameters such as tree height, crown dimensions, 
aboveground biomass, or canopy indices rather than DBH itself. For 
example, Zhou and Zhang (2020) and Lin et al. (2018) used UAV oblique 
photography to estimate tree height and biomass in plantation and 
subalpine forests, while Lin et al. (2015) applied it for tree detection in 
urban environments, and Lin et al. (2021) for leaf area index (LAI) 
estimation. Although these studies demonstrate the utility of oblique 
views for improving structural characterization, they did not specifically 
address DBH estimation through oblique-vertical fusion.
Despite the growing use of UAVs in forest measurement, relatively 
few studies have conducted a direct comparison between allometric 
modeling and data fusion approaches for DBH estimation. While allo­
metric models using vertical UAV data are well established (Kwon et al., 
2024; Brede et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2022; Rudge et al., 2021; Khan et al., 
2025), the integration of oblique imagery with vertical views remains 
less explored, particularly for estimating DBH directly from point 
clouds. Oblique–vertical fusion offers the potential to improve stem 
visibility and 3D reconstruction at breast height, yet the accuracy 
trade-offs, data processing demands, and practical implications of this 
approach are not well understood. Moreover, the specific impact of 
different image acquisition angles on the quality and completeness of 
tree-level point clouds has received limited attention. Varying angles 
can influence shadowing, occlusion, and stem detail, all of which affect 
the fidelity of the 3D reconstruction and, consequently, the reliability of 
DBH estimates. In contrast to the prior studies, which relied on oblique 
imagery to improve crown delineation, height accuracy, or canopy 
structural metrics, our approach explicitly evaluates DBH estimation 
from fused oblique and vertical point clouds. This distinction is impor­
tant because DBH has been only indirectly linked to UAV-derived met­
rics (e.g., through height-biomass allometries (Zhou and Zhang, 2020; 
Lin et al., 2018)), leaving a gap in direct geometric assessment of stem 
diameters using multi-angle imagery. Therefore, there is a need to sys­
tematically evaluate how these two approaches compare in terms of 
accuracy and operational feasibility, particularly in the context of 
single-tree modeling in coniferous forest stands.
While UAV-based DBH estimation has advanced in recent years, 
relatively few studies have investigated the potential of oblique-vertical 
image fusion for improving stem reconstruction and diameter mea­
surement. Furthermore, the specific influence of image acquisition an­
gles on 3D reconstruction quality and DBH accuracy is still not well 
understood. Previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of 
including oblique imagery for stem recognition, but most efforts relied 
on single-angle acquisitions or indirect inference rather than systematic 
multi-angle fusion. For instance, Fritz et al. (2013) showed that oblique 
UAV imagery could reconstruct stems and approximate DBH in open 
stands, but their design used only a single 45◦tilt without combining 
vertical and oblique views. More recently, Swayze et al. (2021)
compared vertical and oblique flights and reported that oblique imagery 
improved DBH extraction rates. These studies indicate that UAV-based 
DBH extraction using angled cameras is feasible, yet most prior work 
has emphasized partial angle settings or indirect inference rather than 
systematic fusion across multiple angles. By contrast, few studies have 
explicitly contrasted direct geometric oblique-vertical fusion with con­
ventional allometric models derived from vertical UAV imagery. To 
address these gaps, this study compares two methods for estimating DBH 
from UAV-DAP point clouds in a pine open forest: (1) a data fusion 
approach that combines vertical and oblique imagery to generate 
high-density 3D models with enhanced stem visibility, and (2) a Random 
Forest-based allometric model based on structural features extracted 
from vertical imagery alone. We hypothesize that (H1) the obli­
que–vertical fusion approach will yield significantly higher DBH esti­
mation accuracy than the allometric model, and (H2) increasing the 
diversity of viewing angles (e.g., combining 30◦, 60◦, and 90◦imagery) 
will improve the geometric quality of 3D reconstructions and reduce

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Study area
North Khorasan Pardisan Park is located roughly 8 km from Bojnord 
city (Zone 40 -37◦28ʹ 57 ʺ N-57◦25ʹ 49ʺ E) with an average elevation of 
1,080 m above sea level (Fig. 1). The park is managed by North Khor­
asan’s General Department of Environmental Protection. It is fenced on 
two sides and predominantly consists of Eldar pine (Pinus eldarica 
Medw). The trees within this park were planted in 2004 and 2010 
roughly following a 3 × 3m grid. The region has a cold semi-arid climate 
based on the Koppen climate classification. According to Bojnord air­
port’s meteorological station (the closest station to the study area), 
Pardisan Park experienced an average annual rainfall of 260 mm and an 
average temperature of 15 ◦C over a period of ten years (2011–2021). 
The primary soil type found in this park is silty clay and silty loam. The 
park’s total area measures approximately 351 ha, with about 21 ha 
consisting of a pine forest, where the stand density was 46.3 trees per 
hectare. Due to the restrictions on permits for flying over and staying in 
this area, a 8-ha section was selected for investigation.
2.2. Field data collection
All pine trees located in open stands of the study area were surveyed 
during a five-day field campaign. DBH was measured at 1.3 m above 
ground using a standard metal caliper with a precision of ±1 mm. To 
ensure measurement consistency and accuracy across all trees, a fixed 
vertical staff was employed to consistently mark the DBH point on each 
stem. Tree locations were captured with a Raymand iRoG3B dual- 
frequency global positioning device integrating within the Shamim na­
tional positing network. The device provided high-precision positioning, 
with horizontal and vertical accuracies of 0.45 cm and 0.8 cm, respec­
tively. During measurements, horizontal accuracy was maintained 
below 0.5 cm and vertical accuracy below 1 cm to remain within 
operational limits of the device. A built-in tilt sensor was activated for 
each tree to ensure correct alignment, minimizing potential errors 
caused by stem inclination or operator misalignment. Crown diameter 
was recorded using a metal measuring tape by projecting the outermost 
edges of the crown vertically onto the ground and measuring the large 
and its perpendicular crown diameters (i.e., CD1 and CD2). Tree height 
and height to crown base (HCB) were measured from the base of the 
stem to the top of the crown and to the point where the crown begins, 
respectively, using a Leica TS02 total station. Additionally, the 
geographic coordinates of each tree were recorded using a Raymand 
iRoG3B dual-frequency GPS receiver (Raymand, Tehran, Iran) (Fig. 1).
2.3. UAV imaging
A DJI Phantom 4 Pro UAV (DJI, Shenzhen, China), equipped with 
vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) capabilities, was utilized for aerial 
imaging. This drone features a 20-megapixel camera and a lightweight 
design (1,380 g), offering cost-effectiveness alongside advanced imaging 
functionality, including adjustable shutter speed and viewing angle. The 
Phantom 4 Pro captures images in the red, blue, and green spectral 
bands in JPEG format. The integrated three-axis gimbal ensures image 
stability by minimizing deviation and vibration during flight. Addi­
tionally, the combined use of GPS and GLONASS satellite navigation 
systems enhances flight stability and enables rapid positioning and 
connectivity during image acquisition.
Aerial images were captured on March 4, 2021, at 14:30 local time 
(UTC 11:00). To generate an accurate 3D model and establish a ground 
reference, 14 ground control points (GCPs) were surveyed using dual- 
frequency GPS receivers under the Shamim system (Property Posi­
tioning Network), ensuring adequate spatial distribution across the 
study area. Images were acquired at a flight altitude of 40 m above 
ground level, with 80 % longitudinal and 40 % lateral overlap. The flight 
path was planned in the longitudinal direction and executed automati­
cally to maximize efficiency and ensure continuous coverage. A total of 
2,034 vertical or nadir (90◦) and oblique images were captured at two 
crossing angles (30◦and 60◦) along the same preplanned flight path, 
achieving a Ground Sampling Distance (GSD) of 2 cm over the 8-ha 
study area (Fig. 1). Specifications of the camera and digital aerial im­
ages are detailed in Table 1.

Following image acquisition, each UAV image was inspected for 
quality issues such as motion blur, excessive tilt, and lighting distortions 
caused by strong sunlight. A total of 39 images that did not meet the

georeference the imagery accurately, 14 ground control points (GCPs) 
(Fig. 1) were used, each with a horizontal and vertical error of less than 
1 cm. The remaining high-quality images were then processed in 
Pix4Dmapper Pro v4.5.6 (Pix4D SA, Lausanne, Switzerland) to generate 
an initial 3D model using the Structure from Motion (SfM) method. SfM 
is a photogrammetric technique that reconstructs three-dimensional 
(3D) structures from two-dimensional (2D) image sequences. It works 
by detecting common features across multiple overlapping images and 
analyzing their parallax from different viewpoints to estimate camera 
positions and 3D geometry (Fritz et al., 2013; Swayze et al., 2021; Mao 
et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Wallace et al., 2016). This method, widely 
used in remote sensing, forestry, archaeology, and computer vision, was 
further applied for advanced processing steps.
To ensure comprehensive tree canopy reconstruction, UAV images 
were collected at three different view angles: vertical (nadir, 90◦), and 
two oblique angles (30◦and 60◦). The vertical images yielded a point 
cloud density of approximately 0.6 points/cm2, while the 30◦and 60◦
oblique images resulted in point cloud densities of 0.8 and 0.9 points/ 
cm2, respectively. Each dataset (90◦, 30◦, 60◦) was processed indepen­
dently using a combination of SfM and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) algo­
rithms. The processing pipeline included feature detection, image 
alignment, camera calibration, sparse point cloud generation, dense 
reconstruction, and final point cloud densification.
To enhance structural completeness and reduce occlusion effects, 
especially in complex forest canopies, multi-angle image datasets were 
fused into three higher-density composite point clouds, i.e., S1: fusion of 
90◦(vertical) and 30◦(oblique) images (1.0 points/cm2); S2: fusion of 
90◦
and 60◦
images (1.14 points/cm2); S3: fusion of all three 
angles—90◦, 30◦, and 60◦(1.27 points/cm2). Each initial image set was 
first processed independently using SfM_MVS to generate a dense point 
cloud. Fusion was achieved by co-registering the respective point clouds 
using shared tie points, followed by a unified bundle adjustment to 
jointly optimize camera positions and intrinsic parameters across all 
included images. A final dense point reconstruction step was then per­
formed on the fused datasets to generate the final high-resolution 
composite point cloud for each dataset. This workflow ensures that 
the fusion considers complementary perspectives from multiple angles, 
enhancing canopy completeness and reducing occlusion effects, rather 
than simply stacking independent SfM outputs. To reduce high- 
frequency noise and further improve spatial accuracy, a median filter 
was applied to the resulting dense point clouds.
Unlike LiDAR-derived point clouds, which contain discrete returns 
(first, intermediate, last), UAV-DAP point clouds produced through SfM 
lack return order information. Consequently, these point clouds repre­
sent continuous surfaces without intrinsic classification into ground, 
canopy, or understory layers. Previous studies (Cao et al., 2019; 
Mielcarek et al., 2025; St-Onge et al., 2015; Rahlf et al., 2014) have used 
Airborne Laser Scanning (ALS) data to normalize UAV-DAP point clouds 
derived from SfM. However, because ALS data were not available in the 
study area, ground points were removed using a custom algorithm 
implemented in MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) to 
isolate individual trees and exclude ground interference. Ground surface 
points were separated based on elevation and neighborhood topology, 
allowing clean segmentation of above-ground tree structures and accu­
rate identification of the 1.30 m height level required for DBH estima­
tion. Using the classified point cloud from the vertical (90◦) imagery, a 
Digital Terrain Model (DTM) and a Digital Surface Model (DSM) were 
generated via Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation. Sub­
tracting the DTM from the DSM yielded a Canopy Height Model (CHM) 
with a spatial resolution of 2 cm, consistent with the GSD of the imagery.
2.5. DBH estimation from multi-angle UAV data (S1–S3)
The DBH estimation for the three datasets (S1, S2, and S3) initially 
employed a RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus)-based circle fitting 
algorithm aimed at robustly fitting circles to noisy and incomplete point 
cloud data. The CHM was segmented using a marker-controlled water­
shed algorithm, identifying local maxima as tree tops and delineating 
individual tree crowns with high spatial precision by partitioning the 
canopy based on elevation gradients. Normalized point clouds, cor­
rected for ground elevation, were spatially clipped by these crown 
polygons to isolate points belonging to each tree.
For DBH estimation, points within a vertical slice centered around 
1.3 m above ground (specifically between 1.2 m and 1.4 m) were 
extracted, consistent with standard forestry breast height measure­
ments. Within this slice, only the two-dimensional horizontal co­
ordinates (X and Y) of points were used for fitting. The RANSAC 
algorithm was intended to robustly fit circles by iteratively sampling 
triplets of points to estimate candidate circles, selecting the one with the 
highest number of inliers within a 2 cm tolerance to handle noise and 
outliers common in terrestrial, UAV LiDAR and UAV-DAP datasets. 
However, it was found that for most trees, especially in the S1 dataset, 
the number of points within the 1.2–1.4 m height band was too low for 
RANSAC to perform effectively due to insufficient data density and oc­
clusions, which limited the algorithm’s ability to identify a robust cir­
cular consensus. Therefore, to maintain consistency and reliability 
across all datasets and trees, a direct least-squares circle fitting method 
within the 1.2–1.4 m height band without random sampling was used 
exclusively. As successfully implemented in previous studies (Fritz et al., 
2013; Swayze et al., 2021; Swayze and Tinkham, 2022; Michałowska 
et al., 2023), this method fits a circle to all available points simulta­
neously by solving the linear system for circle parameters (center co­
ordinates and radius) via ordinary least squares.
Implementation details include: the vertical slice for DBH estimation 
was strictly between 1.2 and 1.4 m; the estimated radius from the best-fit 
circle was then doubled to derive the DBH in centimeters. This approach 
effectively accounted for partial occlusions and irregular point distri­
butions by leveraging all available data points in the slice, ensuring 
robust DBH estimation despite low point densities.
2.6. Random Forest modeling (S4)
Given the nonlinear relationships and potential interactions among 
UAV-derived features, an allometric model based on the Random Forest 
(RF) algorithm was selected as the most suitable modeling approach due 
to its robustness, ability to handle multicollinearity, and high predictive 
performance without requiring strict parametric assumptions. This 
method is particularly effective with medium-sized datasets and can 
capture complex patterns between remote-sensing variables and tree 
dimensions (Xu et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2023; Dalla Corte et al., 2020; Ye 
et al., 2025). An RF regression model was developed to estimate tree 
DBH using UAV-derived metrics as predictors (S4).

The model utilized five explanatory variables, i.e., UAV-measured 
tree crown area, the large and its perpendicular crown diameters (i.e., 
CD1E and CD2E), height, and crown volume. All these attributes were 
extracted from vertical (90◦) images. Crown area was obtained by 
applying marker-controlled watershed segmentation to the CHM, 
allowing precise delineation of individual tree crowns. The CD1E and 
CD2E were measured directly from the segmented crown polygons, 
representing the longest and the perpendicular widths of each crown, 
respectively. Tree height was derived from the CHM by calculating the 
maximum height value within each individual crown. Finally, crown 
volume was estimated by first normalizing the point cloud so all height 
values represent height above ground, ensuring a consistent vertical 
reference. Points below the HCB were then excluded to isolate only the 
crown. For each crown, volume was computed using a 3D alpha 
shape—a generalization of the convex hull that captures concave ge­
ometries. The alpha radius (α) controls the shape’s detail: smaller values 
reveal fine structure, while larger ones yield smoother volumes. To 
improve robustness across varying crown forms and point densities, the 
script tests a range of alpha values (α = 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10), selecting the first 
that produces a valid volume (Colaço et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024b; Di 
Gennaro and Matese, 2020).
Model training was conducted using the randomForest package in R, 
with a holdout validation approach to assess model performance (Liaw 
and Wiener, 2002). The dataset was randomly split, using 80 % for 
training and 20 % for testing. A grid search was performed to tune three 
key hyperparameters: mtry (ranging from 1 to 20 with a step of 1), ntree 
(100, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500), and nodesize (1, 5, 10, 15, 20). For 
each parameter combination, a model was trained on the training data 
and evaluated on the test data using the root mean squared error (RMSE) 
and coefficient of determination (R2). The combination that yielded the 
lowest RMSE was selected as the final model. Variable importance scores 
were calculated as part of the modeling process, and no additional data 
preprocessing (e.g., scaling or normalization) was required, as RF is 
inherently robust to different variable scales. The final model was then 
applied to predict DBH for all trees with complete UAV-derived data.
2.7. Accuracy assessment of DBH estimation
To evaluate the performance of UAV-based DBH estimation, three 
geometric and one allometric modeling were considered: S1 (DBH 
estimated from point clouds generated by fusing 90◦and 30◦UAV im­
ages), S2 (from point clouds of fused 90◦and 60◦images), S3 (from 
fusion of all three viewing angles: 90◦, 30◦, and 60◦), and S4 (DBH 
predicted using an RF-based allometric model trained on features 
derived from point clouds of vertical UAV images). The accuracy of 
these methods was assessed by comparing their estimated DBH values 
against field-measured DBH for individual trees. Statistical evaluation 
was carried out in several steps. First, the distribution of residuals (i.e., 
the difference between field and estimated DBH) for each method was 
assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on the result, 
either a paired t-test or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to 
determine whether the mean difference between the field and estimated 
DBH was statistically significant.
In addition to evaluating the DBH estimation methods, a correlation 
analysis was conducted to compare the individual explanatory variables 
used in the RF-based allometric model. These included crown area 
(derived from segmented CHM), estimated crown diameters (CD1E and 
CD2E), and tree height (measured both in the field and from vertical 
UAV-derived CHM). The goal was to confirm the consistency between 
UAV-based measurements and ground truth data, thereby validating the 
accuracy of the input variables used in the RF-based allometric model 
for DBH estimation.Next, five standard error metrics were calculated for 
each method: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), RMSE, relative RMSE 
(rRSME%) Coefficient of Determination (R2), and Bias. These metrics 
provided a comprehensive view of the accuracy, precision, and sys­
tematic deviation of the predictions. Based on the field-measured DBHs, 
three DBH size classes (i.e., Small, Medium, and Large) were defined 
using tertiles of the observed distribution to ensure roughly equal 
sample sizes. To visualize and further interpret estimation performance 
for all sample trees and across different size classes, two main plots were 
generated. A correlation plot displayed the relationship between esti­
mated and field DBHs, stratified by estimation method and tree size 
class. Each subplot reported the R2 value to quantify goodness-of-fit. A 
Bland–Altman plot was also created to illustrate the agreement between 
field and estimated DBHs, with the mean difference (Bias) and Limits of 
Agreement (LoA) (±1.96 SD) annotated on each subplot. Additionally, 
the distribution of absolute errors across methods was visualized using 
violin and box plots to reveal potential differences in variability and 
error magnitude. Together, these statistical and visual analyses allowed 
for a robust comparison of three geometric (S1-S3) and one allometric 
(S4) DBH modeling in relation to field-measured DBHs.
3. Results
In this study, a total of 324 pine trees, representing a range of DBH 
classes, were measured through fieldwork. A summary of the recorded 
measurements is illustrated in Fig. 2. These violin plots, which combine 
box plots with kernel density estimates, provide valuable insights into 
the structural variability of the sampled trees. The DBH distribution is 
moderately symmetrical, with most values concentrated around 30–50 
cm and a few outliers, indicating a mix of younger and mature trees. The 
Height variable shows a slightly right-skewed distribution, with most 
trees between 6 and 10 m tall, reflecting some variation in age or growth 
conditions. The Crown area shows a wider and more right-skewed dis­
tribution, with several high-value outliers, suggesting substantial vari­
ability in canopy spread, likely influenced by light avail-ability. Both 
CD1 and CD2 have distributions centered around 5–6 m and 4–5 m 
respectively, with relatively less skew, although CD1 has a few notable 
outliers. The box plots within each violin plot further highlight the 
interquartile range and median of each attribute, illustrating central 
tendency and spread. Overall, the data highlight structural heteroge­
neity within the study area, which is important for evaluating methods 
of DBH estimation.
To evaluate the correspondence between field-measured trees and 
UAV-derived observations, high-precision tree locations were recorded 
using a dual-frequency GPS device with horizontal and vertical accu­
racies of less than 1 cm. UAV imagery had a GSD of 2 cm, and the 
average spacing between trees was approximately 15 m, all facilitating 
the identification of individual trees. These measures allowed reliable 
matching of field trees to UAV-detected trees, particularly in the S3 and 
S4 datasets, whereas S1 and S2 did not capture all trees. The correlation 
analysis between field-based and UAV-derived explanatory variables 
revealed significant correlations across all assessed variables. High 
correlation coefficients were observed for height (0.98), large crown 
diameter (CD1 vs. CD1E; 0.96), perpendicular crown diameter (CD2 vs. 
CD2E; 0.92), and crown area (0.92), all of which were statistically sig­
nificant (p < 0.05). These results suggest that the explanatory variables 
estimated from vertical UAV images are sufficiently reliable to be 
implemented in DBH estimation using S4 (RF-based allometric 
modeling).

contrast, methods S2 and S3 showed no statistically significant differ­

4. Discussion
The structural diversity of the sampled pine trees provided a robust 
basis for evaluating both hypotheses in this study (H1 and H2). With 324 
individuals spanning a broad range of DBH classes from younger, 
thinner stems to mature trees with large diameters (Fig. 2) the dataset 
captured significant variability in tree morphology. This was further 
reflected in the distribution of variables such as height, crown area, and 
crown diameters, each exhibiting distinct patterns of skewness and 
spread. The presence of both symmetrical and right-skewed distribu­
tions, as well as high-value outliers in crown metrics, suggests hetero­
geneity in growth conditions, canopy development, and spatial 
competition. Such diversity is critical for testing H1, as it allows for a 
meaningful comparison between the oblique–vertical fusion approach 
and the allometric model across a range of tree forms. It also supports H2 
by providing varied canopy structures and complexities where im­
provements in 3D reconstruction quality achieved by integrating multi- 
angle imagery can be reliably assessed. The variability in tree

The structural characteristics of the point cloud data reveal signifi­
cant differences in stem representation across the three acquisition 
strategies (S1–S3). As illustrated in Fig. 4, the point cloud generated in 
S1 exhibited a sparse and uneven distribution of points around the trunk 
cross-section, with notable gaps likely caused by occlusions and limited 
viewing angles inherent in purely vertical or shallow oblique imagery. In 
contrast, S2 improved lateral surface coverage, albeit modestly, by 
incorporating additional oblique perspectives. The most complete and 
uniform stem reconstruction was achieved in S3, where the integration 
of vertical and multiple oblique images (including up to 60◦) enabled 
dense, continuous point distributions around the stem perimeter. This 
multi-angle fusion effectively reduced occlusions and improved surface 
realism, enhancing the accuracy of geometric reconstruction at breast 
height. Analysis of tree detection performance across methods further 
supports the importance of viewpoint diversity. In S1 and S2, 9.6 % and 
3.4 % of trees, respectively, were not detected due to insufficient point 
density at the critical DBH measurement height (1.2–1.4 m), preventing 
successful circle fitting. These undetected cases were not confined to a 
specific DBH class but were mostly associated with smaller stems (<30 
cm), suggesting that detection failures were more likely driven by lim­
itations in coverage, resolution, or algorithmic sensitivity rather than by 
tree size alone. These findings align with observations by Swayze and 
Tinkham (2022) and Swayze et al. (2021), who also reported that the 
smallest trees exhibited the greatest DBH estimation errors and were 
often underrepresented in UAV-derived vertical and oblique (20◦–35◦) 
datasets. Similarly, Ye et al. (2019) combined vertical and oblique im­
agery (35◦–50◦) to generate single point clouds for DBH estimation of 
American sweetgum in an urban setting. However, they did not compare 
different angle combinations, and their study included only 49–63 trees 
(10–30 cm DBH), lacking analysis by DBH class. Notably, Ye et al. found 
that lower oblique angles (e.g., 35◦) produced poorer feature detection 
and lower point density due to greater camera-to-object distances and 
broader fields of view. These observations support our findings that 
wider oblique angles (e.g., 60◦) substantially improve stem visibility and 
measurement reliability, particularly for small or partially occluded 
trees. Moreover, previous studies have highlighted the advantages of 
fusing vertical and oblique imagery for improving DBH and tree height 
estimations. Swayze et al. (2021) reported detecting only 40 % of trees 
in a 1-ha plot (890 trees) using fusion-based point clouds, whereas our 
study achieved higher detection rates, even in S1 (90.4 %) and S2 (96.6 
%), likely due to the lower canopy occlusion in our single-layer 
coniferous stand and increased point density from multi-angle image 
fusion. This aligns with Fritz et al. (2013), who successfully extracted 
DBH values for 70 % of trees in a leaf-off broadleaved stand using 
oblique imagery (45◦), though their analysis was limited to a single 
angle. Collectively, these findings (Ye et al., 2019; Fritz et al., 2013; 
Swayze et al., 2021; Swayze and Tinkham, 2022) support the conclusion 
that combining vertical and a range of oblique image angles, particularly 
higher obliques, is more effective than using narrow oblique ranges 
alone.
In addition to improved DBH estimation, visual evidence from Fig. 3
and previous literature suggest that multi-angle fusion may also enhance 
height estimation. Swayze et al. (2021) and Lei et al. (2022) noted that 
UAV point clouds can be affected by factors such as platform vibration 
and canopy shadows, leading to uncertain height estimation, issues that 
appear mitigated by fusion in our study. Zhu and Zhang (Zhou and 
Zhang, 2020) and Lin et al. (2018) used fused UAV imagery to extract 
tree height and crown area for conifer species but did not report or 
compare specific oblique angles, nor did they evaluate DBH. The 
consistent improvement in our DBH results suggests that fusion is a 
promising solution not only for trunk but also crown and vertical attri­
butes. Unlike previous studies which highlighted UAV-DAP limitations 
in canopy penetration (Cao et al., 2019), we did not experience major 
occlusion challenges due to the relatively open canopy structure of our 
site. However, as Lei et al. (2022) emphasized, results from SfM 
photogrammetry may vary across forest types. Therefore, while our 
approach was successful in this open, single-layer coniferous stand, 
future research should test multi-angle fusion in denser, multilayer, or 
mixed-species forests to evaluate its generalizability and robustness 
under more complex structural conditions. As mentioned by Tomaˇstík 
et al. (2017), canopy openness is a prerequisite for effective terrain and 
stem modeling in photogrammetry, a condition met in our case but 
which may limit results in more closed-canopy settings.
The fitted circles in the stem point clouds appear slightly inside the 
outermost points (Fig. 4) because they are generated using a least- 
squares circle fitting method, which minimizes the total distance error 
between the circle and all available points. In natural tree stem data, 
particularly from UAV-DAP point clouds, variability arises from bark 
irregularities, noise, occlusion gaps, and imperfect surface reconstruc­
tion. Consequently, not all points align perfectly with the actual stem 
surface. The fitting algorithm computes an average center and radius 
that best represent the general shape of the stem rather than adhering to 
its outermost boundary. This typically results in fitted circles that fall 
within the point cloud rather than intersecting all edge points, which is 
both expected and acceptable in practice. It provides a stable and robust

even partial angular diversity can substantially improve DBH estimation

occlude relevant features. The accuracy assessment of S4 suggests that

diameter, especially in mature pine trees (Fig. 5). For instance, Iizuka 
et al. (2022) reported R2 values of 0.824 and 0.834 for coniferous forests 
using SVR, highlighting how accuracy varies by species and forest 
structure. Similarly, Xu et al. (2025) achieved an R2 of 0.78 and RMSE of

canopy structure, height, density, and intensity, while our RF model

and forest context. Moreover, Dalla Corte et al. (Dalla Corte et al., 2020) 
reported rRMSE = 13.3 %, R2 = 0.41, and MAE = 3.02 cm for DBH 
estimation in dense eucalyptus stands (>350 trees/ha) using height and 
crown metrics from UAV-LiDAR in a RF model (mtry = 6). Compared to 
the results of the current study (Table 2), the lower rRMSE and MAE 
reported in (Dalla Corte et al., 2020) suggest that UAV-LiDAR-derived 
metrics may better correlate with DBH. However, the lower R2 
observed in (Dalla Corte et al., 2020) might indicate either model 
instability or potential overfitting, possibly influenced by the higher 
mtry value (6 in (Dalla Corte et al., 2020) vs. 3 in this study), which can 
re-duce model generalization if not properly tuned. In comparison, Ye 
et al. (2025) used over 90 UAV-LiDAR-derived metrics to predict DBH 
with RF, achieving RMSE = 4.21 cm, rRMSE = 26.6 %, and R2 = 0.77, 
suggesting that the complexity of RF model and input diversity can 
significantly affect accuracy. These findings are consistent with our 
observed performance trends across methods and forest types. 
Furthermore, our S4 findings resonate with those of Tinkham et al. 
(2022), who noted reduced accuracy for trees under 20 cm DBH and 
advocated for hybrid approaches using terrestrial LiDAR or SfM to fill 
data gaps. Our DBH range and stratification into three DBH classes also 
mirrors their approach, further validating our framework. Additional 
limitations as-sociated with UAV-DAP are also evident. As noted by Zhu 
and Zhang (Zhou and Zhang, 2020), shadows, occlusions, and foliage 
motion (e.g., due to wind) degrade CHM quality and crown detection 
accuracy. In our study, these issues were mitigated to some extent 
through vertical–oblique image fusion, improving structural complete­
ness and reducing under-segmentation.
Further analysis of DBH estimation performance across tree size 
classes (small, medium, and large DBH) reinforces the overall superi­
ority of S3 and highlights important trends in method reliability. S3 
consistently achieved the highest accuracy across all DBH classes, 
indicating strong linear agreement between estimated and field- 
measured DBH regardless of tree size. The Bland–Altman plots further 
support this reliability, showing narrow limits of agreement and mini­
mal bias across all classes (Fig. 5). These results confirm that multi- 
angular fusion not only improves overall DBH estimation accuracy but 
also maintains performance consistency across the structural spectrum 
of the forest, making S3 the most dependable method under varying tree 
sizes. S2 demonstrated accuracy metrics closely aligned with S3, sug­
gesting that even partial multi-angle fusion can capture sufficient 3D 
structural detail for reliable DBH estimation. The similarity in perfor­
mance between S2 and S3, particularly for small and medium trees, 
points to the critical role of oblique imagery (especially around 60◦) in 
enhancing stem visibility and geometric fidelity in the resulting point 
clouds. S4 exhibited lower R2 values and greater variability across DBH 
classes. Its largest weakness was observed for small DBH trees, where 
underestimation was evident along with wider limits of agreement, 
suggesting that the model is less sensitive to subtle structural differences 
in younger or smaller trees when relying only on canopy features. 
However, for medium and large DBH classes, its bias and error metrics 
improved, reinforcing its potential utility in forest inventory applica­
tions where resource constraints preclude multi-angle imaging. S1, on 
the other hand, consistently underperformed, particularly for medium- 
sized trees, and showed severe overestimation across all classes, as re­
flected in its high positive bias and broad error ranges (Fig. 5). This 
again illustrates that combining 90◦and 30◦imagery may be suboptimal 
for accurate DBH reconstruction, likely due to occlusion and geometric 
distortion effects from insufficient angular diversity. In summary, these 
findings not only reinforce robustness and generalizability of S3 across 
DBH classes but also highlight close comparative performance of S2, 
which may offer a practical compromise when full-angle acquisition is 
limited. While S4 underperforms relative to geometric approaches, its 
consistency and simplicity make it a viable alternative, especially in 
settings where vertical UAV imagery is more accessible or where time 
and budget constraints restrict data collection.
This study builds on earlier work using oblique UAV imagery for tree 
stem reconstruction but extends it in two important ways. First, we 
systematically tested the effect of combining multiple acquisition angles

(30◦, 60◦, 90◦) on point cloud quality and DBH accuracy (S1-S3), 
demonstrating clear benefits of multi-angle fusion. Second, we directly 
contrasted this geometric approach with an allometric RF model derived 
from vertical imagery (S4), highlighting trade-offs between accuracy 
and operational simplicity. These contributions clarify the specific ad­

pine plantations and distinguish our approach from prior oblique- 
imagery applications. Overall, these findings confirm both hypotheses: 
H1 is supported by the superior performance of geometric methods (S2,

performance gains observed with increased diversity of viewing angles. 
For future applications, these results advocate for multi-angular UAV 
acquisition in forest inventory workflows and suggest that image fusion 
strategies significantly influence UAV-DAP point cloud quality and DBH 
estimation accuracy. It is important to acknowledge, however, that 
UAV-based photogrammetry cannot yet match the centimeter-level 
precision of terrestrial LiDAR for DBH estimation, particularly in 
dense or multi-layered forests. Nevertheless, it can fill the gap airborne 
and terrestrial LiDAR as noted by Fritz et al. (2013). The present study 
demonstrates that methodological innovations such as oblique-vertical 
image fusion can mitigate some of these known limitations by 
improving stem visibility and circle-fitting success in pine plantations. 
Rather than positioning UAV approaches as a replacement for 
ground-based LiDAR, our results highlight their value as a comple­
mentary tool, capable of delivering sufficiently accurate DBH estimates 
in operational contexts where ground-based LiDAR deployment is 
impractical due to scale, terrain, or cost. In this sense, oblique-vertical 
fusion represents a step forward in narrowing the performance gap for 
direct DBH estimation from UAV imagery.
5. Conclusion
This study comprehensively evaluated four UAV-based methods for 
estimating DBH in a structurally diverse population of open-stand pine 
trees. Among the methods tested, S3, which utilized fused point clouds 
from three viewing angles (90◦, 60◦, and 30◦), consistently out­
performed others, achieving the highest overall accuracy and reliable 
performance across all DBH classes. S2 showed nearly equivalent per­
formance to S3, confirming that oblique imagery (particularly from 60◦) 
significantly enhances point cloud fidelity and DBH estimation accu­
racy, even when full-angle coverage is not feasible. These findings un­
derscore the effectiveness of multi-angular fusion in capturing tree 
structure comprehensively and validate both study hypotheses (H1 and 
H2). In contrast, S4, the RF-based allometric model trained on vertical 
UAV-derived structural features exhibited moderate accuracy but 
demonstrated consistent underestimation, particularly for small trees. 
The importance of height and crown volume in this model suggests that 
future RF-based allometric approaches should prioritize the accurate 
extraction of these attributes, especially when relying solely on vertical 
imagery.
While these results provide clear guidance on UAV data collection 
and modeling strategies, some limitations must be noted. The analysis 
focused exclusively on pine trees in open stands, where individual crown 
delineation and stem visibility are relatively high. Therefore, the 
generalizability of these findings to denser, mixed-species forests re­
mains limited. Future research should extend these evaluations to more 
complex environments, where occlusion and overlapping canopies may 
diminish the effectiveness of current methods. In such settings, the 
application of deep learning-based algorithms for DBH estimation in 
denser and more intricate point clouds warrants exploration, potentially 
offering improved robustness and feature abstraction compared to 
traditional models. In summary, multi-angular UAV image fusion, 
particularly involving 60◦oblique views, offers the most accurate and 
consistent method for DBH estimation. These insights provide practical 
recommendations for forest inventory workflows, balancing accuracy, 
data availability, and resource constraints.
