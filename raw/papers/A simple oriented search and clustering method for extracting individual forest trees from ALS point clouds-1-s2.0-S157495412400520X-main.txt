A B S T R A C T
Accurate extraction of individual trees has theoretical and practical significance for improving forest man-
agement and productivity levels. Although airborne laser scanning (ALS) technology is frequently utilized for
large-scale forest mapping and the detection of individual trees, the challenges of detecting individual trees
in multi-layered and deciduous forests remain for most canopy-based methods. This study aimed to develop
an efficient individual tree detection method capable of delineating trees with various heights, especially for
sub-canopy trees. Inspired by the ‘‘from bottom to top" growth characteristics of tree branches, we propose an
oriented search and clustering method, which clusters tree points upwards to the local tops, making it more
adaptable to the roughly conical growth characteristics of a forest’s tree canopy. On the NEWFOR dataset,
our approach demonstrated comparable overall performance to that of state-of-the-art methods. In the non-
dominant layers of multi-layered forests, our method achieved 𝑅𝑀𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎvalues of 30% in the 2–5 m range,
31% in the 5–10 m range, and 55% in the 10–15 m range, demonstrating the best extraction performance. A
practical case study was conducted on selected plots of ALS point clouds acquired from the Bavarian Forest
National Park (BFNP), with 1704 trees identified. This work provides a more reliable and efficient method
for the future forest point cloud segmentation of individual trees; the method better meets the needs of forest
inventory and resource management and lays a foundation for good forest management.
1. Introduction
As the most significant carbon sinks and pools, forests are important
in preventing soil erosion and degradation and influencing climate
change (Wiśniewski and Märker, 2019; Van Kooten, 2020; Huang
et al., 2022). In order to accurately monitor changes in forests and
to enhance forest resilience and biodiversity, key parameters (such as
biomass) are often mapped to indicate the distribution of the biological
and geometrical characteristics of forests (Wagner et al., 2018; Niţă,
2021). To accurately and precisely estimate the biomass of forests,
individual tree segmentation and precise measurement of individual
tree parameters can provide a precise and optimized solution in forest
ecology research (Miraki et al., 2021). Through individual tree segmen-
tation, precise structural parameters (e.g., tree height, crown width,
and diameter at breast height) can be obtained, providing essential data
for monitoring forest growth dynamics and assessing tree health (Yao
et al., 2012; Hu et al., 2023). Thus, accurately segmented individual

However, detecting individual trees within forest environments poses

• Terrain variability: Sloped or uneven terrain can distort the

In recent decades, light detection and ranging (LiDAR) has been
widely used in the field of forestry due to its all-day observation ability,
high precision, and high-level automation compared with traditional
field inventory work. Among different platforms equipped with LiDAR,
airborne laser scanning (ALS) can be used to obtain a set of high-density
3D points covering a large-scale area with high efficiency. Extracting
individual trees from ALS point clouds is usually an essential step and
has been widely investigated (Gülci et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2020).
However, there are still semantic gaps between raw 3D ALS forest point
clouds and the detached 3D structural information of individual trees.
Individual tree extraction methods can generally be categorized
into the following: canopy height model (CHM)-based methods and
point-cloud-based methods. CHM-based methods include the water-
shed, marker-controlled watershed, and polynomial fitting methods
(Wang et al., 2004; Chen et al., 2006; Popescu, 2007; Stereńczak et al.,
2020; Yu et al., 2024). Although the CHM-based method improves the
processing efficiency, it is reliant on the accuracy of the CHM. More-
over, considering that the points in the middle and lower layers are
barely utilized, the accuracy of detecting individual trees, especially for
the sub-canopy and understory, is limited. Point-cloud-based methods
consist of three major categories: clustering, standardized cutting, and
layer stacking (Reitberger et al., 2009; Morsdorf et al., 2009; Ayrey
et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022). These methods have the advantage of
directly utilizing the 3D coordinates of points. Still, they suffer from low
processing efficiency, low precision, weak universality, and dependence
on hyper-parameters, especially in the case of large-size point clouds.
More importantly, most studies usually examine their methods under
specific forest conditions, and the accuracy evaluation methods vary,
making it difficult to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of these
methods with a unified standard.
The key challenge in detecting individual trees, especially for multi-
layered mixed forests, is to extract the sub-canopy trees occluded
by the upper canopy (Polewski et al., 2018). Traditional CHM-based
segmentation methods can only recognize the top canopy of the upper
trees and are inefficient in extracting the lower trees. It is also difficult
to accurately extract lower trees in point-cloud-based methods since
the sparse points can hardly delineate the clear structure of lower
trees. Many studies have demonstrated that bottom-up approaches offer
advantages over top-down methods in the understory and in broadleaf
forests (Ferraz et al., 2012; Jaskierniak et al., 2021). However, exist-
ing methods often rely on conditions such as the leaf-off period (Lu
et al., 2014), specific trunk features (Chen et al., 2018), and point
cloud intensity (Shendryk et al., 2016) or density (Wang et al., 2020),
limiting their practicality in the diverse forest environment. Therefore,
we propose an improved point cloud clustering method that solely
utilizes 3D coordinates, focusing on detecting trees from multi-layered
mixed structures of forests. This method provides an efficient and
effective approach that is well suited for ecological applications. The
key innovations of our approach are oriented search and clustering,
validity checking, and point updating.
• Oriented search and clustering: We introduce a novel bottom-
up search and clustering method that leverages tree growth char-
acteristics based solely on 3D coordinate information. This im-
proves the overall applicability, especially in the understory of
multi-layer sample plots where traditional methods struggle.
• Validity checking and point updating: Our method includes
an innovative validity-checking and point-updating mechanism,
which enhances the precision and F1 score in dense forest en-
vironments. This makes our approach more robust and reliable
across varying forest densities.
The remainder of this article is structured as follows. First, a brief
review of methods working on extracting individual trees from LiDAR
point clouds is provided in Section 2. Afterward, in Section 3, the
proposed method is described. Subsequently, experiments are described
in Section 4, and the performance of the proposed approach is eval-
uated in Section 5, with comparisons and discussions being provided
in Section 6. Finally, conclusions are drawn in Section 7 based on the
analysis and discussions.
2. Related work
2.1. CHM-based methods
The most widely used individual tree detection method is the
canopy height model (CHM)-based method, which involves four major
steps, namely, point cloud filtering, the generation of a CHM, seg-
mentation based on the CHM, and point cloud segmentation based
on the projected coordinates (Hyyppa et al., 2001; Duncanson et al.,
2014; Ma et al., 2021). Point clouds are filtered to obtain forest point
clouds and ground point clouds. Then, a digital surface model (DSM) is
generated by interpolating forest point clouds, and the interpolation of
ground point clouds can be used to generate a digital terrain model
(DTM). The CHM is obtained by calculating the differences (Koch
et al., 2006). The ALS point clouds of forests have the following
characteristics: the crown vertex elevation is larger than the crown edge
elevation. Thus, the maximum height of an individual tree crown vertex
is generally selected as the treetop (Yang et al., 2020). Most studies
using CHM height variation for tree segmentation mainly determine
the treetop through a local maximum search and use edge detection
and feature extraction algorithms to determine the tree crown (Liu
et al., 2015). After segmentation in the CHM, the coarse point clouds
can be segmented according to their 2D coordinates projected onto the
CHM (Yang et al., 2020).
The authors of Wang et al. (2004) developed a two-phase strat-
egy that included edge identification and marker-controlled watershed
segmentation. They first obtained the initial crown boundaries using
edge detection to avoid background errors. The radiometric and spatial
characteristics unique to treetops were used to obtain the treetop mark-
ers, which were finally determined to guide watershed delineation. The
authors of Chen et al. (2006) introduced a marker-controlled watershed
segmentation algorithm that utilized a variable window to search for
the local maxima for detecting the treetops in a CHM. They proposed
that the lower limit of the regression curve between tree height and
crown width can determine the variable window size. The authors
of Popescu (2007) proposed a polynomial fitting method to detect the
crown. The polynomial fitting method was utilized to fit the crown
boundaries according to the position of the treetop and the shape of
the individual tree. The authors of Marinelli et al. (2019) presented
a novel approach based on Bayes’ rule to obtain the minimum error
in tree detection by exploiting the bitemporal data information. To
estimate the statistical parameters utilized for the composite approach,
we obtained the main geometric features of each candidate treetop.
Multitemporal information was considered by estimating the transition
probabilities iteratively while considering the temporal dependence
between LiDAR acquisitions. The authors of Stereńczak et al. (2020)
developed a self-calibration method that was adaptive to new study
areas. A hierarchical analysis of the CHM was also conducted for pro-
cessing segments with different heights. Different settings were applied
to filter the CHM for every tree height range. Following filtering, a
pouring method (Soille, 1999) was used to carry out a hierarchical
segmentation beginning at the uppermost layer of the forest canopy.
This method achieved superior results, especially for deciduous species.
The authors of Deng et al. (2024) proposed an approach to segmenting
individual trees depending on the detection of tree trunks. Initially,
prospective tree trunk positions (PTPs) were identified via a trunk point

by adopting a trunk-based region-growing segmentation method. The
application of the TPDI-based strategy was especially effective for a

of the existing methods, especially local maximum (LM)- and CHM-

window size into account, the authors of Yu et al. (2024) presented
an adaptive crown-shaped approach for individual tree segmentation.
To acquire the forest’s surface points, they first twisted and occluded
at various angles to acquire the normalized point clouds. After that,
the treetops were identified as seed points, and the crown borders
were drawn by examining the crown profiles. Eventually, individual
tree segmentation was achieved by using region growing determined
by seed points. Many previous methods focus on the parameterization
of the developed algorithms for correcting errors in individual tree
detection methods. However, in dense and mixed forests with distinct
stand structures, it is challenging to apply these methods. In order
to better accurately identify individual trees, the authors of Lisiewicz
et al. (2022) devised an approach to adjusting the results from different
CHM-based individual tree segmentation methods. They first derived
individual segmentation faults using the classification method, and
then, they employed an automatic correction method to refine under-
and over-segmentation mistakes.
Although algorithms based on a CHM can improve operation effi-
ciency, the common problem is their dependence on the CHM. After
interpolation and other operations to generate a CHM, the point clouds
are transformed into a mesh, resulting in a certain spatial deviation and
a loss of 3D information (Persson et al., 2002). In addition, the grid
size greatly influences the segmentation of individual trees (Popescu
et al., 2002). A larger grid size may lead to under-segmentation.
Conversely, over-segmentation may occur when the grid size is too
small. Meanwhile, these methods delineate the position and crown
width of individual trees on the CHM, which is formed by the topmost
point cloud (Peuhkurinen et al., 2011). Then, each point is classified
according to its projected coordinates on the CHM. Due to the low
utilization rate of the 3D points in the middle and lower layers of the
canopy, it is difficult to effectively extract middle and lower trees with
complex forest structures (Kostensalo et al., 2023). In this case, the
accuracy and application range are quite limited.
2.2. Point-cloud-based methods
Point-cloud-based methods usually achieve individual tree segmen-
tation in point clouds by understanding the actual growth characteris-
tics of the trees, using the spatial coordinates and intensity information
of the LiDAR point cloud, and setting discriminative rules to judge
and segment all of the point clouds (Dai et al., 2018; Harikumar
et al., 2018). Compared with CHM-based methods, point-cloud-based
methods directly process 3D points and the radiometric information of
point clouds and avoid the errors introduced during the interpolation
process (Lee et al., 2010).
The authors of Reitberger et al. (2009) used normalized cut in
combination with a trunk detection method to achieve the 3D seg-
mentation of individual trees. Additional information extracted from
the waveform decomposition of reflected laser signals served as a
significant feature for identifying tree species. In this way, trees lying
in the sub-canopy can also be detected. The authors of Morsdorf et al.
(2009) applied the 𝐾-means clustering method for individual tree
segmentation. First, the local highest point could be identified based
on a DSM as the clustering seed point. Each point in the point clouds
was assigned to the nearest seed point. Subsequently, the position
of the center of each tree was recalculated until the center point’s
location remained constant or the predetermined number of iterations
was reached. The authors of Lu et al. (2014) proposed a bottom-up
segmentation method for LiDAR point clouds. Since the points that
delineate the main trunks usually outnumber those of the branches and
dead leaves, the first step is to separate trunk points from whole point
clouds. Starting from the main trunk points, all points are classified into
the nearest tree based on the distance threshold, and additional rules
are set to prevent branches or small shrubs from being misclassified
into trees. The authors of Ayrey et al. (2017) introduced a layer-
stacking algorithm to slice whole forest points cloud with a fixed
height interval, i.e., one m, separate trees at each layer, and merge
the representative tree contours of all layers. A novel hierarchical
region-merging algorithm was created by Hao et al. (2021). It initially
over-segmented the whole forest point cloud using the local density
as a guide. The over-segmented partitions were then combined into
groups using a step-by-step optimization process, yielding the final
segmentation result. To transition from the local recognition of canopy
characteristics to the overall compactness of forest point clouds, a
global combination cost was incorporated into the region-merging
approach. The authors of Yun et al. (2021) designed a dual Gaussian
filter with automated adaptive parameter assignment and a screening
strategy for false treetops. This preserved the geometric features of
sub-canopy trees and eliminated false treetops. Second, an anisotropic
water expansion controlled by the energy function was applied for
accurate canopy segmentation. The authors of Wang et al. (2022)
developed a novel model-based framework named ‘‘crown guess and
selection’’. This framework automatically produced crown candidates,
and then the features of candidates and their neighboring information
were taken into consideration. Eventually, tree crowns were selected
from the candidates simultaneously.
Point-cloud-based extraction methods deal directly with 3D points,
which allows better segmentation of small trees, especially for sub-
canopy trees. However, they suffer from slow speed, low accuracy,
weak generalization, and hyper-parameter dependence when dealing
with large-scale point clouds (Xu et al., 2023; Latella et al., 2021).
There is an urgent need for a robust, automatic, accurate, and general-
ized individual tree extraction method, serving as the basis for further
accurate forest inventory.
3. Methodology
In this work, we present a novel method of extracting individual
trees from ALS point clouds based on a bottom-to-top-oriented search,
coinciding with the growth characteristics of natural trees. Our method
is designed for individual tree segmentation in both sparse and dense
airborne forest point clouds, showcasing robustness in sparse data and
achieving high precision in dense data. The input data is required to
be in pcd format, with support for other common formats, such as
LAS/LAZ, through format conversion. Potential applications include
extracting individual tree structural parameters, estimating carbon stor-
age, and supporting ecological monitoring as well as forestry resource
management.
Our proposed method consists of three major steps: point cloud
preprocessing, oriented search and clustering, and validity checking
and updating of tree points. The first step consists of denoising the
points through low-pass filtering, ground removal using a cloth simu-
lation filter (CSF) (Zhang et al., 2016), and separating tree points via a
height filter, with the aim of generating normalized tree points from the
initial point clouds. Then, an oriented search and clustering approach is
introduced to obtain the raw clusters of tree points. Finally, the validity
of the raw point clusters is checked based on the characteristics of trees
to address the situation where certain tree branches are incorrectly
assigned to trees. Finally, the points assigned to each cluster of tree
points are updated. The flowchart in Fig. 1 illustrates the detailed
processing steps and the corresponding intermediate results.

The efficient CSF method then achieved the separation of ground
and non-ground points. The cloth resolution was 0.5 m, the maxi-
mum number of iterations was 500, and the classification threshold
was 0.5 m. Based on the separated ground points, the height of the
tree points was normalized by subtracting the terrain. Compared with
the original points, the normalized points eliminate the influence of
topographic factors caused by the surface undulation in the study area.
Meanwhile, this operation completely retains the hierarchical charac-
teristics of the canopy structure, which is extremely useful for im-
proving the accuracy of individual tree extraction. Specifically, ground
points are interpolated to obtain a digital terrain model (DTM), and
point cloud elevations are subtracted from the corresponding DTM
elevations to obtain the normalized point cloud data. For example,
each point cloud’s 𝑍-value represents the point’s vertical height from
the ground. Fig. 2(a) and (b) demonstrate the comparison between the
initial and normalized tree points.
In addition to the normalization of points, it is still difficult to
distinguish tree points from different layers, such as the layers of
the overstory, understory, and understory shrubs; thus, this influences
the accurate extraction of trees. Our choice to use a threshold-based
method is mainly due to the standard definition of the height of a tree
and the shrub characteristics in the NEWFOR dataset. Here, we use a
threshold defined in many forestry studies (United Nations Framework
Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), 2005): a tree must reach
a minimum height of 2 to 5 m at maturity in situ. Additionally, the
NEWFOR dataset includes a significant presence of understory shrubs,
which complicates the segmentation process. When using this dataset, a
height-filtering step with a fixed threshold of 2 or 3 m is usually applied
to mitigate these challenges (Wang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023). In
our proposed method, we adaptively determine the threshold based on
the point distribution along the vertical axis. First, we count the point
distribution along the vertical direction, as depicted in Fig. 3(a). The
probability function is smoothed with a Gaussian function to reduce
the influence of slight amplitude movements; then, the first derivative
is calculated. Troughs were obtained from the first derivative, taking
the filter height where the first trough was located. Fig. 3(b) shows
the remaining tree points after the height filter. Some sample plots do
not contain understory shrubs. Therefore, the filter height is set to a
maximum of 3 m, i.e., below the height of the lowest trees.
3.2. Oriented search and clustering
Based on the retained tree points, we further extracted tree points
using our proposed oriented search and clustering method, which
was inspired by the classic mean-shift and 𝑘-means algorithms. The
traditional 𝑘-means method requires prior knowledge of the number
of classes (i.e., the 𝑘-value), which is generally difficult to predict and
is susceptible to the initial clustering center. Mean-shift clusters point
to the density centroid of points; they do not need the 𝑘-value to be
preset but generate a new ‘‘process center point’’ and increase the
algorithm complexity. Instead, we cluster points upwards to the local
tops, making this method more adaptable to the roughly conical growth
characteristics of the forest’s tree canopy. In addition, this approach
does not need the 𝑘-value to be preset and does not generate a new
‘‘process center point’’, which reduces the algorithm’s complexity.
The clustering of tree points is converted into a classification pro-
cess. The process of a point being classified is the process of the point
being recognized as a specific tree. After the clustering, a cluster of
points means that an individual tree is extracted and annotated as one
class. Here, 𝐶(𝑃) represents the cluster to which the point 𝑃belongs.

3.3. Validity checking and point updating
Through the previous step, the raw clusters of tree points are
obtained. However, the irregularity in the trees’ geometry and the

4.1.3. Treetop-matching criteria
The NEWFOR dataset (Eysn et al., 2015) only provides treetop
positions for validation and introduces a fully automated tree-matching
procedure for linking the different results to the reference treetops.
To be specific, this automated tree-matching method for the NEWFOR
dataset includes three main steps: candidate search, candidate voting,
and candidate testing.
• Candidate search: The test trees are arranged in descending or-
der of height, initiating the matching procedure with the tallest
test tree. For each test tree, reference trees are included in the
candidate set if their horizontal difference (𝛥𝐷) and vertical
difference (𝛥𝐻) fall within a predefined threshold range, which
was determined by the height of the test tree (𝐻𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡) (refer to
Table 4).
• Candidate voting: Other local candidates are systematically tested
for a superior 𝛥𝐻from the nearest candidate. If a candidate
exhibits a better 𝛥𝐻, and its 𝛥𝐷is at most 2.5 m greater than the
initial candidate’s 𝛥𝐷, the candidate becomes the new best-voted
candidate.
• Candidate testing: The matching challenge is reciprocal. A test
tree must identify the best-matching reference tree and vice versa.
Success in matching is only acknowledged when both the test tree
and the reference data mutually emerge as the best-voted trees for
each other.
The evaluation parameters and their calculation methods are de-
fined as follows:
• 𝑁𝑡𝑒𝑠𝑡: Number of extracted trees;
• 𝑁𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎ: Number of matched trees;
• 𝑁𝑟𝑒𝑓: Number of reference trees;

4.2. Field experiment
Apart from experiments using benchmark datasets, we also con-
ducted a field experiment in the Bavarian Forest National Park (BFNP)
(49◦3′19′′N, 13◦12′9′′E), which is located along the border between
Germany and the Czech Republic (Wong et al., 2024). The authority’s
policy protects the park from human intervention, resulting in a total
tree mortality rate of 22% (Müller and Job, 2009); it is dominated by
Norway spruce (i.e., Picea abies), European beech (i.e., Fagus sylvatica),
European silver fir (i.e., Abies alba), and other spruce trees (Van der
Knaap et al., 2020).
ALS point clouds measuring trees’ leaf-on situation were acquired in
2016 for the BFNP’s administration within the framework of the data
pool initiative for the Bohemian Forest Ecosystem (Latifi et al., 2021).
Specifically, three transects of ALS point clouds, covering a total area
of 13.5 km2, were acquired with an LMS-Q680i 400 kHz scanner on
a flight operated by Milan Geoservice GmbH. The flying altitude was
300 m above sea level, and the point density was estimated at around
70 points/m2. The raw data were processed to LAZ 1.2 format, with
𝑥, 𝑦, 𝑧, and intensity. We applied our proposed individual forest tree
extraction method to 25 plots of point clouds from all three transects
of the ALS point clouds. Fig. 8 shows a visualization of the ALS point
clouds, their locations at the BFNP, and examples of tested plots.

treetops, corresponding to local peaks of understory points.
6. Discussion
6.1. Sensitivity analysis
Plot 3 of the NEWFOR dataset is taken as an example to discuss
the parameter sensitivity of the method. As shown in Fig. 14, as the
radius increases, some small or secondary trees are partially ignored,
and 𝑅𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎslightly decrease. However, as cases of detecting a single
tree with multiple treetops as multiple trees decrease, both 𝑅𝑒𝑥𝑡𝑟𝑎
dramatically decrease. According to the results, we set the radius for
plot 3 to 1.8 m as a more appropriate value. Overall, the segmentation
effect is less affected by the radius, so the matching rate remains
stable.
6.2. Analysis of the impacts of components on segmentation performance
We conducted a detailed ablation study to assess the impacts of
different components of our model on the segmentation performance.
Specifically, we evaluated the influence of three key steps: shrub re-
moval, bottom-up search and clustering, and subsequent validity check-
ing and updating. We implemented three experimental groups, modify-
ing each step of the pipeline to isolate its contribution. The overall and
layered results are shown in Tables 8 and 9. The control group, which
involved shrub removal, bottom-up search and clustering, and validity
checking and updating, achieved an overall extraction 𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑒𝑥𝑡𝑟𝑎value
of 136% and an 𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎvalue of 58%. For the 2–5 m interval, the
𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎvalue was 30%, and for the 5–10 m interval, it was 33%.
The results reflect the excellent extraction ability of our method, espe-
cially in the lower layer of the forest. In the first experimental group,
shrub removal was omitted, and bottom-up clustering was performed,
followed by updating. Although the 𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎvalue of 37% in the 2–
5 m interval was marginally higher than that of the control group, the
overall 𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎvalue increased to 163%, indicating significant over-
segmentation. These findings suggest that shrub removal plays a crucial
role in preventing over-segmentation in multi-layered forests. In the
second experimental group, shrub removal was implemented, and the
clustering method was replaced with the top-down distance-threshold
clustering method proposed by Li et al. (2012), followed by updating.
This method sequentially classifies points from high to low, comparing
each point with its nearest neighbor tree. If the distance exceeds the
threshold, a new tree is created; otherwise, the point is merged with
the nearest tree. This approach is available in the lidR package (Roussel
et al., 2020). However, this configuration resulted in poor performance,
with an 𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎvalue of only 4% in the 2–5 m interval, indicating
that our original clustering method is more effective, especially in the
low layers. In the third experimental group, shrub removal and bottom-
up clustering were maintained, but the validity checking and updating
step was omitted. The lack of significant improvement in this config-
uration was likely due to the sparse nature of the NEWFOR dataset

6.4. Performance on field data
In the context of individual tree extraction, there is relatively lim-
ited research published in this journal that specifically addresses the use
of airborne LiDAR (You et al., 2023). Much of the existing literature
focuses on other data or other techniques, such as imagery (Miraki
et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023), ground-based LiDAR, or deep learning
methods (Martins et al., 2021; Weinstein et al., 2020). We present a
novel application of airborne LiDAR for individual tree segmentation
in forest environments, which may contribute to an application in fast
key parameter estimation (such as for biomass) using individual tree
segmentation results. Our proposed method offers several significant
advantages over traditional approaches.
• Efficiency: It is capable of completing large-scale forest surveys
within a short period of time, significantly improving the speed of
data collection compared with conventional field-based methods.

• Low dependency on prior knowledge: Unlike methods that rely
heavily on prior knowledge or specific datasets, our approach
shows broad adaptability across different geographic locations,
tree species, and forest densities. It is not overly dependent on
specific parameters, making it a versatile tool for global forest
monitoring.
• Performance in multi-layered forests: One of the key strengths
of our method is its ability to perform exceptionally well in multi-
layered forests, especially in lower canopy layers. In these chal-
lenging environments, it outperforms some of the latest methods
in the field.
However, we also acknowledge that our method has notable limita-
tions. In some dense forest plots, where trees are closely spaced and
their crowns overlap, our method struggles to distinguish between
neighboring trees that lack clear treetop features, leading to some
degree of under-segmentation. Despite these limitations, the overall
performance of our approach suggests that it could provide fast and
accurate individual tree segmentation, enabling the further analysis of
forests.
7. Conclusions
The rising demand for aerial LiDAR in forest planning and manage-
ment has brought individual tree detection to the forefront of research.
Nevertheless, accurate segmentation methods for multi-layered and
deciduous forests remain a pressing challenge. We propose a new
oriented search and clustering method based on tree shape and growth
characteristics that consists of two key procedures. The first one is
oriented search and clustering, which enables tree detection in multi-
layered and mixed forest structures using only 3D coordinates. The
second part is a mechanism for point updating and validity checking
that enhances the robustness and reliability of our method across forests
with varying densities. Compared with other methods, our method
achieves the highest 𝑅𝑀 𝑆𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐 ℎvalue of 58% on the NEWFOR dataset.
For layers with elevations of 2–5 m, 5–10 m, and 10–15 m, our method
shows the best performance, with values of 30%, 31%, and 55%, which
reflects its excellent extraction ability in the lower layer of forests.
However, the method presented in this work relies on local elevation
maxima to extract tree vertices, which is excellent for coniferous forests
but less effective for broad-leaf trees. In future work, we will consider
the topology of broad-leaf trees by applying graph-based methods to en-
hance the performance in individual tree detection. Moreover, further
calculation of forest parameters (such as biomass) will be conducted
based on the former individual tree segmentation results.
