 Stem volume equations and basic 
density for grey alder and common 
alder in Sweden 
 TORD  JOHANSSON * 
 SLU, Department of Bioenergy, Box 7060, S-750 07 Uppsala, Sweden 
 *  E-mail:  tord.johansson@bioenergi.slu.se or  tordj@mail1.slu.se 
 Summary 
 The objective was to determine stem volume models for grey and common alders and, based on the 
models, stand volume for naturally regenerated grey and common alder stands was summarized. 
Basic density for grey and common alders and mean annual growth for stands was estimated. Net 
volume accretion data were collected from 24 stands of grey alder ( Alnus incana (L.) Moench) and 
31 stands of common alder ( Alnus glutinosa (L.) Gaertner) in Sweden. The stands ranged in latitude 
from 58 to 64° N and from 56 to 62° N for grey and common alder, respectively. The mean age of 
grey and common alder stands was 41 years and 48 years, respectively, the mean stand density 1726 
stems ha ␣-␣1  and 1078 stems ha ␣-␣1 , and the mean diameter at breast height (over bark) was 20 cm and 
21 cm. Stem volume equations were developed for grey and common alders. The adopted model for 
grey alder was based on diameter at breast height and height. For common alder, crown height was 
added to diameter and height. Mean standing volume (over bark) for grey and common alder stands 
was 428 and 374 m 3  ha ␣-␣1 . Mean annual growth for grey and common alder stands was 12.0 m 3  and 
8.4 m 3  a ␣-␣1  ha ␣-␣1 , respectively. Basic density (under bark), for grey and common alder stems was 359 
and 427 kg m ␣-␣3 , respectively. The basic density (under bark) for the lowest twigs in the crown and in 
the lateral part of the crown was 415 and 421 kg m ␣-␣3  for grey alder and 423 and 423 kg m ␣-␣3  for 
common alder. 
 Introduction 
 Common ( Alnus glutinosa (L.) Gaertn.) and grey 
( Alnus incana (L.) Moench.) alders are widely 
distributed throughout the northern hemisphere 
and common alder is also planted in North Amer-
ica ( Evans, 1984 ). Common alder can be found 
throughout the Nordic countries south of  latitude 
60° N as well as in middle and southern Europe 
and parts of Russia. If climate data for the whole 
 natural range of common alder are taken into 
account, the mean annual temperatures range 
from 1 to 17°C and the mean annual rainfall 
ranges from 400 to 1300 mm. The species grows 
on peat and eutrophic soils as well as on light 
clays, coarse sands and gravels. Common alder is 
frost hardy but the resistance varies from season 
to season ( Matthews, 1986 ). The species is very 
tolerant of ﬂ ooding, but will not grow well in dry 
conditions.

 Grey alder is distributed throughout the Nor-
dic countries, extending far to the north (lat. 71° 
30′ N, 1100 m a.s.l.) and is found in most parts 
of Europe and Asia. Grey alder was introduced to 
Denmark in 1765 ( Møller, 1965 ). This Danish 
author stated that grey alder grows taller than 
common alder but has a shorter rotation period. 
The species is frost hardy ( Rytter, 1996 ). Grey 
alder does not tolerate long periods of ﬂ ooding. 
The species is able to grow on acid and infertile 
soils. In Sweden, common alder grows mainly in 
the southern to middle part of the country, while 
grey alder is found in central and northern 
 Sweden. Co-occurrence is limited to central 
 Sweden. According to the Swedish National Sur-
vey, the growing stock of common and grey alder 
is 23.0 and 9.2 million m 3 , respectively, which 
corresponds to 0.9 and 0.3 per cent of the total 
growing stock ( Anon., 2004 ). 
 Alders generally grow in small stands (0.1 – 
1.0 ha). Both species have nitrogen-ﬁ xation 
 nodules on their roots with a capacity to ﬁ x 
atmospheric nitrogen. This ability is exploited 
successfully in reclamation work. By ﬁ xing nitro-
gen, alders can thrive on otherwise nitrogen-
 deﬁ cient soils and produce nitrogen-rich foliage. 
Consequently, the soil will be enriched by the lit-
ter, which is readily decomposed. Alders are often 
used as nurse stands for other species such as com-
mon ash ( Fraxinus excelsior L.), common hazel 
( Corylus avellana L.), silver birch ( Betula pendula 
Roth), downy birch ( Betula pubescens Ehrh.) and 
Norway spruce ( Picea abies (L.) Karst.).  Rytter 
(1996) reviewed knowledge concerning the use of 
grey alder in forestry.  Schrötter (1983 ,  1996 ) and 
 Melzer (1990) studied grey alder in middle Europe, 
mostly with regard to the utilization and silvicul-
ture of mixed stands of alder and Scots pine ( Pinus 
sylvestris L.) or alder and Norway spruce. 
 Studies on yield of alder for Nordic conditions 
have been conducted during the last decade. In 
Sweden there is no available tree volume equation 
for grey alder. But  Eriksson (1973) has developed 
volume functions for common alder. In Norway, 
 Børset and Langhammer (1966) presented tree 
volume equations and yield tables for grey alders 
growing in 47 localities (lat. 59 – 64° N). The 
stands ranged in age between 14 and 47 years. 
They reported a volume production for the 47 
stands of 90 – 470 m 3  ha ␣-␣1  depending on stand 
age. In Finland,  Kärki  et al . (1999) presented 
 volume functions and tables for grey alder 
growing in Finland, and  Appelroth (1952) reviewed 
the occurrence and yield of grey alder. He studied 
the invasion of grey alder on areas close to the 
Baltic Sea where the yield was 175 m 3  ha ␣-␣1  for 
20- year-old stands.  Murnieks (1950) studied grey 
alder growing in Latvia and presented volume 
tables for 5- to 49-year-old trees. In Scotland, 
 Mitchell  et al . (1981) measured three stands of 
common alder and presented yields of 12- to 
22-year-old alder stands. In Germany,  Schrötter 
(1983) studied grey alder stands and presented 
tree volume functions and yield tables and  Lockow 
(1995) presented tree volume functions for com-
mon alder. The interest in managing broadleaved 
trees has increased during the last decade 
 ( Johansson, 1999 ). Therefore, tree  volume func-
tions for grey and common alder based on stands 
growing in a wide range of latitudes are still 
needed especially for Swedish  conditions. 
 There are several methods for estimating tree 
volume ( Clutter  et al ., 1983 ). The volume of tree 
sections is calculated, for example, by using for-
mulae for a cylinder where section length and 
diameter are parameters. Complete tree volume is 
found by summation of the sections. Functions 
describing tree volume have tree height and diam-
eter as the main parameters, but also height to the 
crown and crown length are used as supplements 
in some functions. Including crown height in 
 volume equations has been successful because of 
the correlation between stem form and crown 
length. Theoretically, stem form is better  estimated 
without crown length ( Larson, 1963 ). 
 Basic density of stem and twigs for common 
and grey alder has been studied. However, there 
are incomplete data on basic density for stem and 
twigs for grey and common alders growing in 
stands of different stem density and site index. 
Samples for predicting basic density of a stem are 
taken at different heights of a tree. However, in a 
Finnish study the sample taken at breast height 
predicted the density well and the true stem aver-
age was best calculated from a sample taken at 
25 per cent height of an alder ( Hakkila, 1970 ). In 
a Norwegian study the basic density for grey 
alder stems increased with increasing height at 
which the sample was taken ( Nagoda, 1968 ). In 
Finland,  Hakkila (1970) and  Lehtonen  et al . 
(1975) reported a basic density for grey alder of 
361 kg m ␣-␣3  and common alder of 415 kg m ␣-␣3 ,

 The objective of the present study was to deter-
mine stem volume models for grey and common 
alders. Based on the models, stand volume for 
naturally regenerated grey and common alder 
stands was summarized. Variation in basic den-
sity between grey and common alder and stand 
characteristics was examined. Mean annual 
growth for stands was estimated. 
 Materials and methods 
 Data from grey and common alder stands in Swe-
den growing in 24 and 31 localities, respectively, 
were used ( Figure 1 ). All stands were naturally 
regenerated.  Johansson (1999) used these stands 
in his study of site index for grey and common 
alders. Two of the grey alder stands appearing in 
Johansson’s study have since been clearcut and 
were excluded. Some of the alder stands had been 
thinned once prior to the present study. Stands 
accepted for this study had to be free from severe 
damage caused by herbivore wild game. The 
stands of grey alder ranged in age from 22 to 67 
years and those of common alder from 22 to 92 
years ( Table 1 ). 
 In each stand the number of stems per hectare 
and their diameters at breast height were observed. 
The mean number of stems per hectare was calcu-
lated based on the numbers of stems counted in 
the 0.10 – 0.15-ha stands. A 5-m-wide buffer strip 
was established along the border of each stand 
sampled in order to avoid the effects of wind, 
open areas, ditches and shading by adjacent 
stands, etc. Site index was calculated for the stand 
( Johansson, 1999 ). Soil types for the alder stands 
were adapted from  Johansson (1999) . The root-
ing depth was 15 – 40 cm and the groundwater 
level stood at 20 – 40 cm depth. 
 Twenty trees per stand were selected for meas-
uring tree height, live crown height and diameter 
at breast height ( Appendix A ). The diameter 
range for the selected 20 trees was based on the 
mean diameter and the diameter range for the 
entire stand. Five trees in each stem diameter 
quartile were selected. The difference in mean 
diameter between the total number of stems on 
the stand and the sampled stems was ␣-␣2.5 mm 
(␣-␣33 to +29) for grey alders and ␣-␣1 mm (␣-␣23 
to +15) for common alders. 
 Construction of a stem volume model for 
grey and common alders

common alder stands were used for estimation of 
tree volume ( Table 1 ). The remaining stands 
(9 and 11, respectively) were used as test material 
for the predicted stem volume functions. The dif-
ferences in mean age, diameter, height and crown 
height between stands used for prediction and 
test of the function are small ( Table 1 ).

 Model selection criterion 
 The observed ( V  o ) and estimated ( V  e ) volume for 
individual stems was evaluated. The corresponding 
standard deviations were also assessed. The mean 
differences were assessed with two-tailed  t tests. 
The total Type 1 Error was controlled using the 
Boniferroni  t test ( Miller, 1981 ). 
 Calculations of stand volume 
 Using data from the subsample (20 trees per 
stand), cf.  Appendix A , calculations were made on 
single stem volume for the alder stands. For every 
stand, height/diameter and crown height/diameter 
curves based on the 20 sample trees per stand 
were made. Then predicted height and live crown 
height based on diameter for each of the trees in 
the stand was estimated. The stand  volume for 
stands was then calculated by summarizing esti-
mated stem volumes by the stem models 2 and 4 
for grey and common alders, respectively. The 
mean annual increment (MAI) for the stands was 
correlated with different stand parameters (total 
age, stem number and site index). 
 Estimation of basic density of stems and twigs 
 Basic density was estimated according to the 
water-immersion method described by  Andersson 
and Tuimala (1980) . One tree per stand growing 
outside the plot was used for basic density estima-
tion. The tree evaluated had a diameter close to 
the mean weighted basal diameter. A disc was cut 
from the chosen tree at the height of 4 m accord-
ing to the methods from long-term experiments at 
the Forest Faculty. A sample of a twig from the 
lowest branches of the crown and one sample in 
the lateral part of the crown were cut. The 10-cm-
long samples were cut close to the stem. The stem 
disc and the twig samples were saturated in water 
for 24 h and the fresh weight was converted to 
volume. The volume of the disc and the twig sam-
ples was determined. The dry matter content of 
the wood proportion (kg) of the disc and the twig 
samples was determined after drying at 105°C in 
an air-ventilated oven for 24 h. Dry weight to 
fresh volume of the debarked disc and twigs was 
then calculated as basic density (kg m ␣-␣3 ).

 Results 
 Stem volume estimations 
 As shown by  Table 2 the asymptotic standard errors 
for the parameters were lower than the parameter 
values. A comparison between observed volume 
( V  o ) and volume estimated ( V  e ) by the above models 
was made ( Table 3 ). Model (2E) for grey alder has 
the lowest level of unexplained variation (RMSE, 
31.6 × 10 ␣-␣6 ) with model (4E) having the lowest for 
common alder (40.2 × 10 ␣-␣6 ) ( Table 3 ). The pre-
dicted mean volume for grey alder by model (2E) 
was the same as the observed mean volume and the 
predicted mean volume for common alder by model 
(4E) was closest to the observed mean volume 
( Table 3 ). Based on the above it was decided to 
adopt model (2E) for the estimation of stem volume 
for grey alder and model (4E) for common alder.

 Model (4E) estimated stem volumes for common 
alder with lower mean residuals than model (8E). 
Model (8E) for common alder underestimated stem 
volume ( Figure 2 ). The mean residuals between 
observed and estimated volume were lowest

 Stand volume and increment 
 When summarizing stand volumes, the mean 
stand volume for grey alder stands was 428 m 3  
ha ␣-␣1  and 314 m 3  ha ␣-␣1  for common alder stands 
( Table 4 ). The MAI for grey alder was 12.0 ± 
0.7 m 3  a ␣-␣1  and for common alder 8.4 ± 0.5 m 3  a ␣-␣1  
( Table 4 ). Different parameters for the models 
were tested, but the coefﬁ cient of determination 
( R  2 ) was low for all of them. MAI for grey alder 
was better correlated with total age than com-
mon alder ( Figure 3 ). 
 Basic density 
 Basic density for grey alder stems was 359 (230 –
 440) kg m ␣-␣3  and for common alder 427 (370 –
 490) kg m ␣-␣3  ( Table 5 ). A pair-wise  t test of the 
basic density for twigs showed no statistically 
signiﬁ cant differences between the two crown 
levels for either species. A pair-wise  t test of 
means of twigs from the two crown levels and 
the stem did not show any statistically signiﬁ -
cance differences. The results were 415 (320 –
 460) kg m ␣-␣3  and 421  (250 – 510) kg m ␣-␣3  for the 
lower twigs, and 423 (310 – 510) kg m ␣-␣3  and 423 
(270 – 490) kg m ␣-␣3  for the lateral twigs of grey 
and common alder crowns,  respectively. 
 Discussion 
 Stem volume 
 Stem form has been deﬁ ned as the relative rate of 
change in stem diameter with increasing tree height, 
which is synonymous with stem taper ( Larson, 
1963 ). When estimating stem volume, a model, 
which can describe the stem taper, is important. In 
the present study, a model, which contained both 
stem height and live crown height, gave the best 
correlation with measured common alder stem vol-
umes. For grey alder, crown height did not add to 
predictive ability perhaps because most of the grey 
alder stands were dense even if some of them were

 Stand volume and increment 
 When comparing the yield estimated in the 
present study with corresponding estimates made 
in previous studies, it is important to keep in 
mind that some of the stands in the present stands 
had not yet been thinned. Measured values 
 represent actual stand volume. On some localities 
the observed stand volume was higher than 
expected according to the calculated site 
index. Mostly these phenomena depend on poor 
or no management of the stands causing a high 
number of stems. Further, as some of the 
stands have not been properly managed, the vol-
ume of mortality by competition (self-thinning) 
and damage caused by pathogens and insects is 
not known. 
 The stand volume for grey and common alder 
stands in the present study is in accordance with 
the ﬁ ndings of other studies and comparisons 
with old long-term experiments. However, most 
of the published yield values for grey alder are 
based on conditions of management similar to 
those of the present study. Optimal yields will be 
found if the alder stands are cleaned and thinned 
according to recommended programmes. The 
range of stem diameter is smaller in a managed 
stand than in an unmanaged stand. This means 
that timber yields in managed stands of old alders 
(50 – 70 years) could be higher than predicted by 
the present model. 
 The mean annual increment (MAI) was 12.0 ± 
0.7 m 3  ha ␣-␣1  a ␣-␣1  for grey alder and 8.4 ± 0.5 m 3  
ha ␣-␣1  a ␣-␣1 . According to  Møller (1965) grey alder in 
Denmark had a height of 14.4 m and mean annual 
increment of 6.6 m 3  ha ␣-␣1  at 37 years of age. In the 
present study, stand nos 1, 18 and 24 correspond 
in age with the Danish stand. MAI for these 
stands are 12.3, 7.5 and 8.3 m 3  ha ␣-␣1  a ␣-␣1 . How-
ever, the alders in the Swedish stands were taller 
(14.9 – 22.5 m) than in the Danish stand.  Otherwise 
ﬁ ndings in the present study correspond with 
those presented by others. 
 Basic density 
 In the present study, basic density for common 
alder stems was higher (427 kg m ␣-␣3 ) than for grey 
alder (359 kg m ␣-␣3 ). Density for twigs was at the 
same level for both species. Different studies 
 support the present ﬁ nding. In the study by 
 Hakkila (1970) basic density was higher for 
fast-growing alders than for slow-growing ones. 
He could not explain the stem-to-stem variation 
by external tree characteristics such as age, 
growth rate and stem size. The best individual 
variable, age, explained only 10 per cent of the 
variance. Hakkila stated, ␣‘␣A considerable propor-
tion of the basic density variation is probably 
caused by genetic factors␣’␣.

 Conclusions 
 In the present study alder stands have not been 
managed at all or thinnings have been made too 
late with a light removal with the result that the 
stands may have too many stems. The most 
important operation is to thin the alder stands at 
an early phase, e.g. when the trees are 3 – 4 m high. 
After thinning the stem number should not exceed 
3000 stems ha ␣-␣1 . Otherwise competition will 
decrease timber yield.
