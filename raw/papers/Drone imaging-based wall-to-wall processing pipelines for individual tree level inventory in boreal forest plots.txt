A B S T R A C T
Precise individual tree data are essential for forest management, strategic planning, efficient commercial 
forestry, and accurate carbon stock assessments. In this study, a wall-to-wall drone-imaging-based forest in­
ventory processing pipeline was developed and assessed. Different cameras and data analysis methods were 
assessed for individual tree detection and attribute estimation at the tree and plot levels. The experiment was 
conducted in Finland in six boreal forest study areas, with three major tree species: Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris), 
Norway spruce (Picea abies), and birch (Betula pendula and Betula pubescens). RGB and multispectral (MS) 
cameras provided single-sensor solutions for the forest inventory pipeline, whereas a hyperspectral (HS) camera 
was used in combination with the RGB camera to enhance species classification. High-quality RGB data per­
formed better than MS data for tree detection and attribute estimation. The best tree detection rates were 56–84 
% in areas with mostly dominant and co-dominant trees. The two evaluated tree detection methods (local 
maximum and segmentation) provided similar tree detection rates and tree attribute estimation accuracies. Tree 
level attributes were estimated with root mean square errors (RMSEs) of 0.97 m (5.1 %) for tree height, 3.1 cm 
(14 %) for diameter at breast height (DBH), 129.6 cm2 (25 %) for the basal area, and 0.13 m3 (23 %) for the 
volume. The HS camera yielded the highest tree species classification performance, with maximum f-scores of 
0.81 for RGB, 0.88 for MS, and 0.89 for combined HS + RGB data. At the plot level, RMSEs for stem density, basal 
area, and volume were 855.7 ha-1 (74.6 %), 6.9 m2 ha−1 (24.2 %), and 48.6 m3 ha−1 (17.6 %), respectively. This 
study was the first to assess entire inventory pipelines with a comprehensive camera setup and proved that low- 
cost RGB and MS cameras provide acceptable performance for tree inventories in boreal forests. These results can 
guide the implementation of low-cost forest inventory processes.
1. Introduction
Precise individual tree data are fundamental for optimized multi­
functional forest management, strategic planning, efficient commercial 
forestry, and accurate carbon stock assessments. Such data enable 
detailed individual tree analyses and plot-level assessments by aggre­
gating at lower spatial resolutions.
Conventionally, individual tree level data are collected in field 
measurements. Recent advancements in close-range remote sensing 
technologies have made it possible to conduct similar in-situ forest 
measurements with less labor-intensive fieldwork (Liang et al., 2022). 
One such is drone-based remote sensing (Wallace et al., 2016; Torresan 
et al., 2017; Hyypp¨a et al. 2020a, 2022; Liang et al., 2019). Although 
drone remote sensing often misses suppressed trees, limiting the com­
plete delineation of individual trees (Wang et al., 2016), it remains a 
critical source of training data for large-area forest inventories based on 
remote sensing (Hyypp¨a et al., 2022). In addition, the top canopy layer 
accounts for most of the aboveground biomass in the forests (Wang 
et al., 2019).
In drone-based sensing, camera systems are considerably more cost- 
effective than lidar-based systems. By employing photogrammetric 
methods, the cameras can produce three-dimensional (3D) information

and estimate tree height, crown width, and diameter-at-breast-height 
(DBH), similar to drone-based lidar (or airborne laser scanning) sys­
tems (Liang et al., 2022). In addition, multispectral (MS) and hyper­
spectral (HS) cameras can provide spectral information beneficial for 
tree species classification (Yu et al., 2017; Nevalainen et al., 2017; Chen 
et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2025). Tree species information is not only 
important for forest inventories but is also essential for allometric 
equations used in tree volume estimation. Consequently, methodologies 
that can provide tree species information along with tree height and 
DBH, are of great interest.
Machine learning is the fundamental technique for extracting species 
information and to estimate tree attributes from remote sensing data. 
Traditional machine learning relies on handcrafted features, while deep 
learning automatically learns the relevant features during training. One 
advantage of traditional machine learning methods is their interpret­
ability, feature relevance can be more easily assessed, which is useful for 
selecting or designing sensors tailored to specific tasks in individual tree 
level forest inventories.
Previous studies have explored various phases of forest inventory 
using drone-based laser scanning (Liang et al., 2019; Corte et al., 2020; 
Gyawali et al., 2022; Hyypp¨a et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2022; Qin et al., 
2022), and imaging (Iglhaut et al., 2019; Fujimoto et al., 2019; Nezami 
et al., 2020; Kuzmin et al., 2021). However, image-based approaches 
have focused only on a few steps in the inventory process, such as tree 
detection or classification. In contrast, when aiming to implement a fully 
digital forest inventory workflow, a comprehensive wall-to-wall pipeline 
is preferred, i.e., one that integrates all phases from individual tree 
detection to the final inventory output. Implementing such pipelines 
involves several aspects to consider regarding the use of remote sensing 
data and analysis methods.
In this study, a wall-to-wall drone image-based forest inventory 
pipeline was developed and assessed. Various camera combinations 
were evaluated for their effectiveness in detecting individual trees and 
estimating commonly used forest inventory parameters, such as tree 
height, DBH, basal area, species, and volume. The camera setups tested 
included a dual-RGB camera system (RGB), an MS camera, a combina­
tion of the RGB and MS cameras, and a combination of the RGB and HS 
cameras. These setups differ in terms of cost and operational complexity. 
The evaluation encompassed various approaches for individual tree 
analysis. These included two techniques for tree detection (segmenta­
tion and local maxima-based), three strategies for tree crown delinea­
tion, two methods for DBH estimation (random forest and linear 
regression), and several feature sets for classifying tree species. The 
experiment was conducted in a boreal forest in Finland in six study areas 
with varying forest complexities (Liang et al., 2018).
The primary research questions are as follows: 1) How do RGB and 
MS cameras perform in measuring commonly used forest inventory 
parameters at both the individual tree and plot levels? 2) To what extent 
does a high-cost HS camera enhance tree species classification compared 
to more affordable MS or RGB cameras? 3) Can 3D data from MS images

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Study areas and reference data collection
The study areas are boreal forests located in Evo, H¨ameenlinna, 
Finland (61.19◦N, 25.11◦E). Data were collected from six different areas 
with varying forest densities and species compositions (Fig. 1). The 
study areas were classified into three difficulty classes: easy, medium, 
and hard, based on forest densities and assumed difficulties according to 
the stem visibility (the level of possible occlusion effects) at the ground 
level, stem density, and DBH distribution in the sample plots (Table 1). 
The ground reference data collected during the summer of 2014 
included the tree location, species, height, and DBH (Fig. 2, Table A.1). 
Logged and fallen trees were removed from the reference data after­
wards using airborne laser scanning (ALS) data collected in 2017. More 
details about the forest conditions and field measurements can be found 
in Liang et al. (2018). Trees in all study areas, except in area 5, were 
classified into four crown classes: dominant, co-dominant, intermediate, 
and suppressed (Fig. 3, Wang et al. (2016)).
We employed plot- and species-wise stratified sampling to partition 
the reference data into training and testing datasets. This approach 
ensured that the test set comprised 20 % of the reference data in each 
study area while maintaining equal proportions of tree species in both 
the test and training sets. The training set was used for method devel­
opment, model training, and cross-validation. This includes tree detec­
tion parameter tuning and the training of machine learning methods for 
tree species classification and DBH estimation. We used only pine, 
spruce, and birch trees in this study because there were too few in­
dividuals from other species. The training and test set trees are shown in 
Fig. 1.
2.2. Drone data collection and processing
The drone flights were carried out on 3rd and August 8, 2018 using a 
drone system with a Gryphon Dynamics quadcopter frame, Pixhawk 
autopilot, Trimble’s APX-15 EI UAV GNSS-inertial OEM positioning 
system consisting of a multiband GNSS, an Inertial Measurement Unit 
(IMU), and a Harxon HX-CHX600A Antenna (Fig. 4). RGB images were 
collected using a dual camera system with two Sony A7R II digital 
cameras equipped with a Sony FE 35 mm f/2.8 ZA Carl Zeiss Sonnar T* 
lens. The HS camera was based on a 2D image format and utilized a 
tunable Fabry-P´erot interferometer (FPI) to scan the spectral bands 
(Senop Ltd., Oulu, Finland). The camera was set to collect 46 spectral 
bands in the spectral range from 500 to 900 nm, with full width at half 
maximum (FWHM) varying from 4 to 10 nm (see Table 2 for band de­
tails). The MS images were captured using a five-band MicaSense 
RedEdge-M camera (AgEagle Aerial Systems Inc., Wichita, Kansas, 
USA). During data collection, the illumination conditions varied from 
cloudy to sunny, and sun zenith angles from 44.1◦to 53.1◦(Table 3). 
The flying speed was 4 m/s and the flying height was 120 m at the 
starting position. Owing to the varying canopy heights, the distance 
from the top of the canopy was approximately 100 m. At least 75 % 
forward and side overlap were achieved at the treetops for every sensor 
(Table 4). For different plots, five to six ground control points (GCPs) 
were measured with a Topcon Hiper HR RTK dual-band receiver with an 
expected accuracy of 32.4 mm horizontally and 19.0 mm vertically. 
Additionally, an ASD Field Spec Pro spectrometer (Malvern Panalytical 
Ltd., Malvern, United Kingdom) was installed to measure irradiance 
during data collection to support radiometric processing.
Photogrammetric processing was conducted using Agisoft PhotoScan 
1.4.3 (Agisoft LLC, St. Petersburg, Russia). The GCPs and GNSS/IMU 
camera orientation data were used for georeferencing the RGB images. 
The post-processed kinematic (PPK) GNSS/IMU image positions and 
angles of the dual-camera images were calculated using single-base 
differential GNSS-inertial processing in the Applanix POSPac UAV 
(Version 8.3). Virtual reference station (VRS) data for the base station 
were obtained from the National Land Survey of Finland (NLS) RINEX 
service 
(https://www.maanmittauslaitos.fi/en/finpos/rinex). 
The 
initial values for the MS image processing were obtained from the 
camera interior orientation parameters (IOPs), GNSS data, and radio­
metric parameters contained in the image metadata. After georefer­
encing, the estimated accuracy (RMSEs of residuals at GCPs) was 
generally less than one GSD (4–8 cm for horizontal and 4–7 cm for 
vertical coordinates). Agisoft PhotoScan was also used for combined 
processing of the RGB data and two bands of the HS images. For the 
remaining HS bands, the EOPs were calculated using in-house software 
(Honkavaara et al., 2017). Study area 5 had an estimated RMSE of 
approximately two GSDs for the combined RGB and HS processing. The 
RGB dense point clouds were generated using the “high” density and 
“mild” depth filtering and the dense MS point cloud with “ultra high” 
density and “mild” depth filtering options.
The reflectance transformation of MS images was performed based 
on calibration panel images and a downwelling light sensor (DLS) in­
formation (“Use reflectance panels” and “Use sun sensor” options in 
Agisoft Photoscan). The MS reflectance orthomosaics (16-bit orthor­
ectified GeoTIFF file) were calculated using the orthomosaic blending 
mode. Radiometric processing and orthorectification of the HS data 
were performed using an in-house software (Honkavaara and Khoram­
shahi, 2018). Reflectance transformations for the HS and RGB data were 
performed using the empirical line method and two radiometric panels 
(3 % and 10 % reflectance).
The outputs for each study area included RGB, HS, and MS 
orthoimages with GSDs of 2, 8, and 8 cm, respectively, and photo­
grammetric point clouds for the RGB (1200–1400 points/m2) and MS 
data (170–210 points/m2). The georeferencing accuracy was estimated 
to be 1–2 GSDs for each coordinate axis direction.
2.3. Drone image-based method for individual tree level inventory
The developed individual tree level inventory method provides es­
timates of tree location, species, height, DBH, crown diameter, basal 
area, and stem volume. Fig. 5 presents an overview of the tree inventory 
method, whose main steps are (1) computation of the canopy height 
model (CHM), (2) tree detection from the CHM, (3) feature extraction, 
(4) tree species classification, and (5) tree attribute estimation.
2.3.1. Digital terrain model (DTM) and canopy height model (CHM)

from the RGB and MS photogrammetric point clouds. This was possible 
because of high-resolution point clouds and forests that contained open 
areas and relatively flat terrain. Alternatively, available ALS data could 
have been used to derive the DTM, but our goal was to evaluate the 
individual tree inventory method without external data sources. DTM 
from the ALS data was, however, used in the development phase to 
visually check that the photogrammetric DTMs did not include rough

2.3.2. Tree detection
Two different tree detection methods based on CHM were tested: a 
method utilizing local maxima detection by Popescu and Wynne, 2004
(FUSION: CanopyMaxima) and a method based on watershed segmen­
tation. The local maxima method has been proven to work well for 
managed boreal forests, where trees do not significantly overlap with 
each other (i.e., forests with dominant and co-dominant trees only), and 
they have narrow single peaks (Nevalainen et al., 2017). The outputs of 
the local maxima method (FUSION: CanopyMaxima) include the loca­
tion, height, and crown diameter of the trees.
A limitation of the local maxima-based approach is its inability to 
accurately delineate the actual boundaries of the tree crowns. Thus, the 
actual data of the tree (pixels from the images and points from the point 
cloud) must be extracted using a predefined, generally circular, sam­
pling area around the detected tree location. The extent of this sampling 
area can be predefined or based on, for example, the estimated crown 
width.
We hypothesized that tree data extracted from actual tree crown 
boundaries would provide better results for tree attribute estimation and 
tree species classification, as was the case for classification in Liang et al. 
(2024). Thus, we implemented a segmentation-based tree detection 
pipeline that delineates tree crown boundaries. As a segmentation al­
gorithm, we used the watershed segmentation algorithm available in the 
EBIMAGE R package (Pau et al., 2010). A detailed description of the tree 
detection pipeline is provided in Supplementary S1.2. The tree location 
was defined as the location of the maximum height within the 
segmented crown in the CHM.
We evaluated the performance of tree species classification and tree 
attribute estimation using three tree crown delineation approaches: 
local maxima with a constant 1-m radius sampling area, local maxima 
with sampling area radius based on estimated tree crown width, and 
watershed segmentation.
2.3.3. Feature extraction
Various feature combinations were evaluated in the classification of 
tree species. These included spectral features derived from RGB, MS, and 
HS imagery, textural features from RGB imagery, and structural features 
from photogrammetric point clouds. While all feature types were 
employed for tree species classification, only structural features were 
utilized for estimating the DBH. In the segmentation approach, features 
were calculated from the pixels or points within the segments. For the 
local maxima method, two delineation approaches were tested: (1) a 
constant 1 m radius around the tree and (2) a radius equal to half of the 
estimated crown width.
The spectral features from the MS and HS data included the mean 
and median reflectance features and continuum-removed spectra 
(Table B.1), similar to those of Nevalainen et al. (2017). In addition, we 
computed several existing vegetation indices (VIs) found from the 
literature, mainly from Main et al. (2011) (Table B.2). The closest 
available bands in the MS and HS data were used as the spectral bands in 
the VI. First, the VI was calculated for the individual pixels extracted 
from a tree, and the average of these pixels was used as the VI of the tree. 
With the MS data, we only computed VIs whose spectral bands were 
reasonably close to the MS sensor bands (own evaluation). The VIs 
computed for the MS data are listed in Table B.2.
The RGB features were calculated for both the RGB and MS data. The 
features included spectral features based on the calibrated red, green, 
and blue bands (Table B.3), and textural features (Table B.4). The RGB 
textural features included 13 gray-level co-occurrence matrix features 
(GLCM), i.e., Haralick features (Haralick et al., 1973), Local Binary 
Patterns (LBP; Ojala et al., 1994), Zernike Moments (Teague, 1980), and 
Threshold Adjacency Statistics (TAS; Hamilton et al., 2007). These were 
calculated from grayscale images derived from the RGB data and using 
the Mahotas computer vision package (https://mahotas.readthedocs.io 
/; Coelho, 2013). Textural features and their parameterizations are lis­
ted in Table B.4. Parametrization was different for the RGB and MS data 
because of their different spatial resolutions.
The structural features included point cloud features depicting the 
vertical structure of the tree and the estimated crown width (Table B.5). 
For tree species classification, the structural features were normalized 
by the estimated tree height to avoid classification based on the absolute 
height of the tree. In the case of the DBH estimation, the structural 
features were not normalized, and the height was included as a regres­
sion feature. The structural features were computed from the point 
clouds using the LAStools software.

2.3.4. Tree species classification
Tree species classification was performed using the Scikit-learn 
(Pedregosa et al., 2011) random forest classifier (Breiman, 2001), 
which is a fast and robust algorithm that is not prone to overfitting with 
a high number of features. In addition, it readily provides a ranking of 
features and performed well in our previous study on tree species clas­
sification (Nevalainen et al., 2017). The number of decision trees was set 
to 100 and the Gini impurity index was used as a quality metric for 
splitting the decision trees. Decision tree nodes were expanded until all 
the leaves were pure or contained fewer than two samples. Before 
feature selection and classifier training, the features were standardized 
by removing the mean and scaling to unit variance. We also evaluated 
feature selection (recursive feature elimination using cross-validation, 
Guyon et al., 2002) combined with hyperparameter tuning (parameter 
grid search, Scikit-learn GridSearchCV), but they did not have a notable 
effect on the results and were thus excluded from this study.
The tree species classifier was trained for pines, spruces, and birches 
by using the detected training set trees. Other tree species were omitted 
due to the small number of available training data. We performed two 
separate performance evaluations: one with the detected training set 
trees using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), and the other with 
detected test set trees.
2.3.5. Tree attribute estimation

provided by the CanopyMaxima function in FUSION software. With 
watershed segmentation, the tree height corresponded to the maximum 
height of the CHM within each segment, and the crown width was 
defined as the diameter of a circle with an area equal to the tree segment.
Two different methods were tested for DBH estimation: (1) linear 
regression with a two-parameter model, where the independent vari­
ables were the estimated tree height and crown diameter (Equation (1)), 
and (2) random forest regression using structural features.

2.4. Plot-level estimates
The entire inventory pipeline was tested in a plot-level inventory, 
where the estimated attributes included stem density, basal area per 
hectare, and volume per hectare. This approach included the following 
steps: (1) detection of all trees within the study area, including both 
training and test set trees; (2) tree and feature extraction; (3) species 
classification; (4) tree height, DBH, basal area, and volume estimation of 
individual trees; and (5) aggregating the individual tree results to the 
plot level. Because the tree species classifier and DBH regression could 
only be developed for pines, spruces, and birches, the plot-level esti­
mates were possible only for the study areas that included only these 
species (i.e., study areas 1, 2, 5, and 6).
2.5. Performance evaluation
The performance of tree detection using the watershed segmentation 
and local maxima methods was evaluated by calculating the detection

3. Results
3.1. Tree detection
Fig. 6 shows the tree detection results using RGB and MS data. There 
was no significant difference between the segmentation (SEG) and local- 
maxima (LM) methods, as both exhibited nearly identical tree detection 
and commission rates for RGB and MS data. Overall, the results were 
highly specific to the area. In areas classified as “Easy” (areas 1 and 2), 
detection rates ranged from 56 to 84 % for RGB data and 46–82 % for MS 
data. In “Medium” areas (areas 3 and 5), detection rates were 16–44 % 
for RGB data and 13–35 % for MS data. In “Hard” areas (areas 4 and 6), 
detection rates were 16–30 % for RGB data and 14–19 % for MS data. 
The poorest results were observed in area 5 (“Hard”), with detection 
rates of 14–17 % and commission rates of 34–49 %. Surprisingly, tree 
detection performance was worse in “Medium” area 3, with detection 
rates of 14–26 % for dominant and co-dominant classes and a minimum 
commission rate of 54 %, compared to “Hard” area 6, which had 
detection rates of 53–82 % for dominant and co-dominant classes.
RGB data performed better with co-dominant trees than MS data, 
particularly in study areas 4 and 6 (64 % vs. 36 % detection rates in area 
4 and ~80 % vs. ~45 % in area 6). Area 6 included most of the birches, 
and the MS SEG performed poorly there. As a result, the MS SEG training 
set consisted of only four birches. In total, there were 226 dominant and 
co-dominant trees in the test plots, of which 160 and 139 were detected

3.2. Tree species classification
Tree species classification was evaluated for four different cases: 
Case 1 (RGB only); Case 2 (RGB + MS); Case 3 (RGB + HS); and Case 4 
(MS only). In addition, the three tree crown delineation approaches 
were evaluated in each case (SEG: segmentation, LMC: local maxima 
method with tree crown delineation using constant radius circle; and 
LMV: local maxima method with tree crown delineation using variable 
radius circle). We used the f-score as the main metric to evaluate the 
feature set combinations.
Fig. 7 (and Table D.1 and D.2) presents the results for all cases. The 
highest f-score was 89.0 % in Case 3, using SEG and feature set including 
only spectral features from HS data (feature set {HS: sp}). The lowest f- 
score was 51.6 % with Case 1 using the LMC and feature set including 
only structural features from RGB data (feature set {RGB: 3D}). On 
average, the best f-scores for Cases 1–3 were achieved using SEG tree 
crown delineation (average f-score 79.3 %), followed by LMV (average f- 
score 76.1 %). The LMC tree crown delineation had notably poorer re­
sults (average f-score 68.1 %).
The highest f-score of 89.0 % was also achieved in Case 3 using a 
feature set including spectral features from HS data and blue band from 
MS data (feature set {HS: sp, MS: blue}). This feature set was created 
because the blue band was a significant feature with MS data in Cases 2 
and 4 but was missing from the HS data. Nonetheless, the inclusion of 
this feature did not improve significantly the f-score compared to the 
feature set {HS: sp}. However, incorporating RGB features into the HS- 
based features negatively impacted performance; the f-score dropped by 
11.1 % with the feature set {HS: sp, RGB: 3D} compared to the feature 
set {HS: sp}.
The best results in Case 1 were achieved using LMV tree crown 
delineation with the feature set {RGB: 3D} (f-score 81.4 %). However, 
the same feature set produced the worst results when combined with the 
LMC tree crown delineation. In Case 2, the best f-score was obtained 
with the SEG and a feature set including vegetation indices from MS data 
(feature set {MS: VI}; f-score 82.9 %).
In Case 4, the best performance was achieved with the LMV using 
feature set including vector-normalized spectral features from MS data 
(feature set {MS: sp_norm}), resulting in an f-score of 88.2 %. The 
second-best performance came from SEG with the same feature set, 
achieving an f-score of 78.0 %. In Case 4, the textural feature set {MS: 
tx} performed the worst (average f-score 45.9 %), and incorporating it 
into any other feature set resulted in a decrease in f-scores. When using 
MS-based features (Cases 2 and 4), better outcomes were observed in 
Case 2, which utilized RGB data for tree detection.
A few observations can be drawn from the results of the training data 
(Supplement S2.1). The highest f-scores achieved with the training data 
for Cases 1, 2, and 3 were 92.5 %, 92.8 %, and 90.3 %, respectively, 
suggesting that the HS data did not outperform in species classification. 
Whereas with the test data, Case 3 performed the best; the highest f- 
scores being 81.4 %, 82.9 %, and 89 % for Cases 1, 2, and 3, respectively. 
In Case 4, the highest f-score with the training data was 93.8 % using the 
feature set {MS: VI}, whereas with the test data, the highest f-score of 
88.2 % was achieved using the feature set {MS: sp_norm}. For the test 
data, the most favorable outcomes for Cases 2, 3, and 4 were obtained by 
employing only spectral features from the spectral camera. The inclu­
sion of RGB textural and 3D features did not enhance the results.

3.3. Height estimation
The SEG and LM tree detection methods were assessed individually 
across different study areas, as well as collectively in a dataset encom­

trees are illustrated for the RGB and MS data in Fig. 8 (and Supplement

As there was a four-year difference between the reference data 
collection and drone measurements, we calculated the random error and 
bias between the reference and measurements; a negative bias indi­
cating that the reference heights were smaller. The biases were notably 
area-specific; areas 1, 3, 4 and 5 had negative bias from −0.29 m to 
−1.29 m, whereas area 2 had near-zero bias and area 6 positive bias 
(0.69–0.73 m). The negative bias is either caused by tree height growth 
after the field measurements or by possible systematic errors in the 
CHM. The random error reflects the accuracy in the absence of bias. For 
instance, in area 1, a small random error compared to the RMSE suggests 
that the RMSE is primarily influenced by bias. In area 2, the random 
error matched the RMSE, indicating no significant bias was present.

3.4. DBH
The DBH estimation models were assessed individually for various 
combinations of tree detection and crown delineation methods. We 
conducted evaluations using both RGB and MS data at the species level, 
as well as with combined all-species sets (see Figs. 10 and 11, Supple­
ment S2.3 for training set results).
With RF regression, the model performance with RGB data remained 
consistent across all tree detection and crown delineation methods, with 
an RMSE ranging from 3.8 to 4.3 cm (Fig. 10). In contrast, linear 
regression showed greater variability between methods, with RMSE 
values spanning from 3.1 to 4.8 cm. The best performance across species 
was achieved through segmentation using linear regression, yielding an 
RMSE of 3.1 cm and a rRMSE of 14 % (Fig. 11). The local maxima-based 
methods showed significant variability with spruces, as there was an 
approximately 19–20 % difference in the rRMSEs between the RF and LR 
regression results. This variation is likely due to the test set containing 
only three spruces. Such discrepancies were not observed in the training 
set (Fig. S6), where the results were consistent with those of other tree 
species.

those obtained using the RGB data. Segmentation employing linear 
regression yielded the best results (RMSE 3.7 cm, rRMSE 17 %, Fig. 11), 
although all other methods exhibited a rRMSE within 4 %. The vari­
ability in the results for spruces was notably more pronounced, as the

3.5. Basal area estimation
The basal area was directly calculated using the estimated DBH 
values. Like DBH, it was assessed separately for various combinations of 
tree detection, tree crown delineation, and DBH estimation methods. 
Fig. 12 displays the species-level results using the test set from the RGB 
and MS data (training set results available in Supplement S2.4).
For both RGB and MS data, the best performance was achieved using 
segmentation with linear regression (all-species rRMSE of 29 % with 
RGB data and 27 % with MS). The basal area of birches was more 
accurately estimated using linear regression (rRMSEs 12–15 % larger 
with RF). Such a distinct difference is not observed with pines; however, 
for spruces, the results are influenced by the small sample size, similar to 
the case with DBH.
3.6. Volume estimation
Fig. 13 presents the volume estimation results with species-specific 
allometric equations using estimated height and DBH for the RGB and 
MS data (training set results in Supplement S2.5). The volume estima­
tion was evaluated separately for different combinations of tree detec­
tion, tree crown delineation, and DBH estimation methods.
When using RGB data, the difference in results between RF and linear 
regression were within 4 % rRMSE when employing local maxima-based 
methods. However, with segmentation, linear regression exhibited a 10 
% lower rRMSE compared to RF. The most significant species-wise dif­
ferences between linear regression and RF were observed with birches, 
where the rRMSEs differed by 16–17 %. With MS data, the combined 
results of all species with MS data showed similar trend between RF and 
linear regression as was observed with the RGB results; segmentation 
with RF and LR had a 20 % difference in the rRMSE, and with other 
approaches, the differences were within 3 %.
Again, the model that performed best using both RGB and MS data 
was the segmentation with linear regression, achieving RMSE and 
rRMSEs of 0.13 m3 and 29 % with RGB data, and 0.12 m3 and 25 % with 
MS data, respectively. Additionally, the small sample size is again 
evident in local maxima-based methods with spruces.

4. Discussion
4.1. Assessment of results
The goal of this study was to develop and assess a wall-to-wall drone 
imaging-based forest inventory methodology. The methodology consists 
of four main phases: tree detection, feature extraction, species classifi­
cation, and tree attribute estimation (height, DBH, basal area, and vol­
ume). Various sensor configurations and data analysis techniques were 
investigated to optimize performance. Three remote sensing camera 
setups were tested individually and in different combinations: a dual 
RGB camera system, an MS camera, a combination of RGB and HS 
cameras, and a combination of RGB and MS cameras. Additionally, 
multiple analytical approaches were evaluated, including two tree 
detection methods, three tree crown delineation approaches, two DBH 
estimation methods, and various features for tree species classification. 
Tree detection and tree height estimation were conducted using unsu­
pervised techniques, whereas supervised techniques were used for tree 
species classification, DBH, basal area, and volume estimation. The 
methodology was evaluated at the individual tree and plot levels, with a 
summary of individual tree level estimation results provided in Table 7
and a summary of plot level attribute estimation in Table 8.

The most critical aspect of our tree detection method is the quality of 
the CHM. The CHM should be smooth, with distinguishable treetops and 
boundaries between trees. We attempted to identify general parameters 
that could be applied across all study areas, and we successfully found 
parameters that worked well in five of the six study areas. An important 
future development topic is the development of an automated method­
ology for identifying area-specific parameters. In addition, our study 
areas were forests with flat terrain and open areas, which allowed us to 
use DTMs derived from the photogrammetric data. This might not be 
applicable in forests with denser canopies or in forests with rougher 
terrain and large height differences. Presently, national level LiDAR 
datasets that are becoming widely available in different countries could 
be used as the terrain reference.
The best results in tree species classification were achieved with the 
HS data, showing the benefits of hyperspectral data in capturing the 
distinct spectral features of different tree species. MS data, however, 
performed well and were clearly more consistent with different feature 
sets compared to using only RGB data. The classification results with 
RGB data varied greatly among the feature sets and tree crown delin­
eation approaches. The best-performing classifier with MS data was 
better when tree detection was also performed using MS data (Case 4) 
instead of RGB data (Case 2); however, in general, the classification 
results with different tree crown delineation approaches were better in 
Case 2.
Tree species classification was influenced by the tree crown delin­
eation approach, with the LMC yielding significantly poorer results than 
the LMV or SEG methods. This difference was most notable when only 
RGB data were used. These results suggest that moving from constant- 
sized circles in tree crown delineation to variable-sized circles or seg­
mentation improves tree species classification. The likely reason for this 
improvement is that LMV and SEG utilized more accurately captured 
crown data, whereas LMC either captured only part of the tree crown or 
extended beyond it, incorporating data from the surrounding area, 
which likely led to a less precise classification.
Tree height was estimated with similar accuracy using all tested tree 
crown delineation methods, whereas the high-resolution RGB data 
yielded slightly better results than the MS data. However, a four-year 
difference between the drone measurements and reference data collec­
tion introduced some uncertainty, particularly in tree height estimation. 
It is highly recommended that reference and drone data collection 
should always be performed at close dates.
Linear regression performed slightly better in DBH estimation than 
the random forest when the segmentation approach was used. Other­
wise, tree detection and tree crown delineation methods did not have a 
strong impact on DBH estimation. Basal area and volume estimations 
were highly connected to the estimation performance of DBH; thus, they 
were also best estimated using a linear regression approach and were 
unaffected by the tree detection or tree crown delineation approach.
The plot-level results revealed that while many small undetected 
trees within a plot could affect tree detection rates, their omission did 
not significantly impact the overall plot-level estimates. However, un­
derestimation of basal area and volume at the plot level was still notable, 
except in Area 1, where the forest growth between the field and drone 
measurements led to overestimation of the plot-level estimates. Plot- 
level estimates would be best evaluated using a completely indepen­
dent study area. Unfortunately, this was not possible due to the species- 
wise imbalance in the study areas.
4.2. Comparison to previous studies
To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to evaluate a wall- 
to-wall individual tree inventory pipeline using image data as compre­
hensively as we did. Furthermore, data from all three cameras (RGB, MS, 
and HS) were collected simultaneously, ensuring a fair comparison 
under identical illumination conditions. Recent studies have focused on 
testing drone-based laser scanning in some or all parts of an individual 
tree inventory (Liang et al., 2019; Gyawali et al., 2022; Hyypp¨a et al. 
2020b, 2022), but photogrammetric data have also been used (Fujimoto 
et al., 2019; Nezami et al., 2020; Kuzmin et al., 2021).
Liang et al. (2019) evaluated the accuracy of various laser scanning 
methods—terrestrial, mobile, and drone-based—in estimating tree 
location, height, DBH, stem curve, and volume across three study areas, 
specifically areas 1, 4, and 6, which are also included in this paper. Their 
approach involved estimating all tree attributes directly from the stems 
visible in the point cloud data. In contrast, we employed machine 
learning to indirectly estimate DBH and used allometric equations to 
determine the volume. Direct measurements eliminate the need for 
separate feature engineering, model training, or the application of 
allometric equations. With drone-based laser scanning data, they ach­
ieved tree detection rates of approximately 55 %, 35 %, and 13 % in 
areas 1, 4, and 6, respectively). Compared with the results of this study, 
the detection rates were lower, as we achieved 82–84 %, 43–44 %, and 
29–30 % detection rates with RGB data in areas 1, 4, and 6, respectively. 
They estimated tree height with rRMSEs of approximately 10 % in areas 
1 and 4 and 13 % in area 6, while in our study, the rRMSEs for height 
were 6.2–6.6 %, 4.9–5.9 % and 5.0–5.3 % for areas 1, 4, and 6, 
respectively. The rRMSEs for the DBH estimation in their study were 
approximately 17 %, 31 %, and 52 % for areas 1, 4, and 6, respectively, 
whereas we achieved an average rRMSE of 13.9 % at best across all 
study areas (RGB data with segmentation and linear regression). The 
tree volumes in Liang et al. (2019) were estimated with approximate 
rRMSE of 60 %, 120 %, and 220 % for areas 1, 4, and 6, respectively. We 
achieved volume estimates with a rRMSE of 23–32 % (combined 
all-species), depending on the methods and data source used. We were 
able to estimate DBH and volume only for pines, spruces, and birches 
because of the need for training data, whereas Liang et al. (2019)

estimated the DBH and volume for all species. The lower detection rates 
and estimation accuracies reported by Liang et al. (2019) may stem from 
their tree detection and attribute estimation approach, which was 
tailored for ground-based laser scanning data rather than drone-based 
laser scanning data. In the same study, higher detection rates and 
attribute estimation accuracies were achieved using ground-based laser 
scanning methods.
Hyypp¨a et al. (2020b) assessed six mobile laser scanning method­
ologies, including an above-canopy drone-based individual tree 
approach, for automated field reference data acquisition in boreal forest 
environments. In the obstructed plot, tree detection rates ranged from 
83 % to 86 %, while the volume estimation errors were approximately 
50 %. The study concluded that under-canopy mobile laser scanning 
significantly outperformed the above-canopy individual-tree approach. 
Hyypp¨a et al. (2022) improved the method of Hyypp¨a et al. (2020b) for 
above-canopy individual tree reference data collection and used point 
cloud data from a high-resolution ALS system on a helicopter for tree 
detection and height, diameter, and volume estimation. At best, they 
achieved detection rates of 87–97 % and rRMSEs of DBH and stem 
volumes of 8–10 % (RMSE 2.2–2.9 cm) and 12–21 % (RMSE 0.1–0.15 
m3), respectively. These results are better than we achieved and are a 
result of the high-resolution point cloud data, which enabled them to 
estimate the stem curve and height, and therefore, volume more directly 
from the data without the need for allometric equations. However, the 
original concept relied on high-cost sensors and multi-angle tree mea­
surements. While achieving high detection rates in some instances, the 
system was designed to generate high-quality reference samples, rather 
than comprehensive plot-level tree inventories. Additionally, the study 
focused on spruce forests, which exhibit a slightly simpler volume esti­
mation than pine or birch, which limits direct result comparability.
High-density point clouds were also used in tree detection by Hakula 
et al. (2023). They used airborne lidar point clouds (>1000 pt/m2) with 
a segmentation method that utilized the full 3D information of the point 
clouds. They achieved 89.5 % and 78 % detection rates for the dominant 
and co-dominant trees, respectively. This confirms the quality of our tree 
detection results, with limited potential for further improvement.
Gyawali et al. (2022) estimated individual tree height from both 
laser scanning and photogrammetric point clouds from drones in 
southeastern Finland. With photogrammetric point cloud data, the 
estimation RMSEs were 2.13 m for pine, 2.08 m for birch and 1.26 m for 
spruce. In our study, tree height was best estimated using the RGB data 
and segmentation approach, with an RMSE of 0.97 m.
Kuzmin et al. (2021) employed an MS camera similar to the one used 
in our study for classifying tree species in boreal forests in Finland. They 
utilized RGB data alongside two MS cameras (Parrot Sequoia and 
MicaSense RedEdge-M), achieving an overall classification accuracy of 
83.3 % for four tree species: pine, spruce, birch, and aspen. This accu­
racy is over 9 % lower than what we achieved using the MS data 
(Table D.2).
Nezami et al. (2020) achieved high tree species classification accu­
racies by employing a hyperspectral (HS) camera similar to the one used 
in this study. They utilized deep learning convolutional neural networks 
to classify tree species—pine, spruce, and birch—using RGB, HS, and 
CHM bands, attaining an overall accuracy of 98 % using an independent 
test set. This is 8 % higher than the best accuracy achieved in this study. 
It is important to note, however, that the tree data in Nezami et al. 
(2020) comprised clearly distinguishable trees. Nonetheless, this in­
dicates that the classification component of the method introduced in 
this study might benefit from being replaced by a deep learning-based 
approach with reduced feature engineering.
Fujimoto et al. (2019) used drone-based photogrammetric data for 
tree detection and DBH estimation. They achieved 92.3 % tree detection 
rate and an RMSE of 4.7–7.5 cm for DBH estimation. The detection rates 
were clearly better than ours, but the results were obtained in a sparser 
forest. The DBH results, on the other hand, are in the same range as our 
results.
The first study to estimate plot level forest inventory variables using 
photogrammetric point clouds was conducted by Puliti et al. (2015). 
They used structural features from photogrammetric point clouds and 
spectral features from the red, green, and near-infrared bands. The 
RMSE (and rRMSE) based on LOOCV were 538.2 ha-1 (39.2 %), 4.5 m2 
ha−1 (15.4 %) and 38.3 m3 ha−1 (14.5 %) for the stem density, basal 
area, and volume, respectively. The study was conducted in a boreal 
forest in Norway. Similarly, Goodbody et al. (2017) used drone-based 
photogrammetric data to estimate plot-level volume and achieved a 
rRMSE of 18.5 %. Our RMSEs (and rRMSEs) using RGB data were 855.7 
ha-1 (74.6 %), 6.9 m2 ha−1 (24.2 %), and 48.6 m3 ha−1 (17.6 %) for stem 
density, basal area, and volume, respectively. The results of Puliti et al. 
(2015) and Goodbody et al. (2017) are not directly comparable to ours, 
as they generated plot-level estimates using plot-level features without 
aggregating results from individual tree-level estimates. In contrast, 
Kukkonen et al. (2021) and Puliti et al. (2020) used drone-based laser 
scanning data to derive plot-level results from individual tree-level es­
timates, achieving plot-level volume estimates with rRMSEs of 18.8 % 
and 32.2 %, respectively.
The MS and RGB single-sensor solutions proposed in this study 
provide high-quality plot-level estimates in mature managed forest 
plots. This is relevant for various applications, including the standing 
timber trade and property value assessment.
4.3. Future developments
The MS and RGB cameras used in this study enabled photogram­
metric point cloud generation and a wall-to-wall forest inventory pro­
cess. However, the MS camera outperformed the RGB camera in species 
classification, whereas the RGB camera excelled in point cloud analyses. 
Furthermore, tree species classification in forests with high biodiversity 
and forest health assessments will continue to benefit from HS data. 
Miniaturized camera technologies are advancing rapidly, with new MS 
cameras already offering enhanced spatial resolutions. For instance, the 
novel Micasense Altum-PT provides a 2.5 cm GSD in pansharpened 
mode from a 120 m flight altitude, compared to the 8.9 cm GSD of the 
RedEdge camera used in this study, thus approaching the performance 
of RGB cameras (https://ageagle.com/drone-sensors/altum-pt-came 
ra/). Moreover, emerging MS camera systems, such as the Agrowing, 
equipped with 14 spectral bands and a wide-angle RGB camera, are 
approaching the capabilities of HS cameras while also providing the 
added advantage of high-quality RGB data (https://agrowing.com/). 
However, our results are also applicable to emerging sensor technolo­
gies, suggesting that the next generation cameras will incorporate the 
advantages identified with different sensor types. These advancements 
are expected to deliver high-performance results to single-sensor 
solutions.
Deep learning methods are widely utilized and continuously devel­
oped for analyzing remote sensing data (Paheding et al., 2024). They 
have demonstrated outstanding performance in classifying tree species 
using drone data from HS cameras (Nezami et al., 2020; Sothe et al., 
2020; Miyoshi et al., 2020) and RGB cameras (Onishi and Ise, 2021). 
Additionally, they have been applied to tree detection (Cloutier et al., 
2024; Lee et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024) and height estimation (Fu 
et al., 2024). The complexity of deep learning methods has made it 
difficult to gain insight into the features on which the deep learning 
model relies. In this study, we focused on machine learning methods for 
which the features are engineered, and the importance of each feature 
can be easily evaluated. This is beneficial for designing future sensors or 
sensor combinations and provides insights into the reasons why a spe­
cific sensor or method performs as it does.
In a study by Hyypp¨a et al., (2024), it was shown that the new na­
tional ALS data of Finland (5 pt/m2) can be used to detect individual 
trees and estimate their attributes (height, DBH, and volume) with ac­
curacies similar to those in this study. This makes individual tree level 
forest inventories possible on a large scale in regions where such ALS

data are available. However, each of the tested methods in this study 
outperformed the national ALS data in terms of species classification 
performance. In addition, a small-scale inventory is needed to maintain 
and improve forest information between large-scale ALS campaigns, 
which is required for various forestry applications, such as standing 
timber trade and property value assessment.
Although the detection of suppressed trees using drone-based laser 
scanning or photogrammetry presents a significant challenge, their 
presence can be inferred through the application of theoretical distri­
bution functions. For example, Maltamo (2004) employed two distinct 
methodologies to predict the suppressed trees. The first method utilized 
parameter prediction models to estimate the parameters of a complete 
Weibull distribution, thereby deriving the suppressed tree population 
from the predicted tree height distribution. The second method involved 
fitting a two-parameter left-truncated Weibull distribution directly to 
the detected tree height distribution.
5. Conclusions
A wall-to-wall drone image-based forest inventory processing pipe­
lines using a dual-RGB camera system (RGB), a multispectral camera 
(MS), a combination of RGB and MS, and a combination of RGB and 
hyperspectral (HS) cameras was developed and assessed in this study.
The best tree species classification results were achieved using a 
combination of RGB and HS cameras. However, both the RGB and MS 
cameras also provided a single sensor solution for a wall-to-wall indi­
vidual tree-level forest inventory pipeline with reasonable accuracy. 
Tree detection, however, was only feasible for dominant and co- 
dominant trees, which remains a challenge for airborne remote 
sensing technologies. The RGB data, with its higher spatial resolution, 
provided slightly better results than the MS data for tree detection and 
attribute estimation. The tree detection rates and attribute estimation 
accuracies were similar for both the local maxima- and segmentation- 
based tree detection methods.
Our results demonstrated that the RGB and MS single-camera ap­
proaches offer sufficient performance for forest inventory pipelines in 
boreal managed forests, offering efficiency considering data processing 
and costs. However, in forests with higher biodiversity, HS data are 
likely to outperform in tree species classification. Due to the challenges 
of detecting trees growing beneath dominant and co-dominant trees, the 
methodology developed in this study is particularly well-suited for 
evaluating mature managed forests, making it highly relevant for ap­
plications such as timely standing timber trade and property value 
assessment.
The ongoing advancements in drone camera technologies combine 
the benefits of RGB, MS, and HS datasets, incorporating the advantages 
of each sensor type identified in this study. This holds a significant po­
tential for developing low-cost drone-based forest inventory pipelines. 
To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to implement a 
complete wall-to-wall forest inventory pipeline while comparing 
different camera technologies.
