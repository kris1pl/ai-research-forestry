ABSTRACT
Segmenting individual trees from airborne LiDAR point cloud data is critical for forest
management, urban planning, and ecological monitoring but remains challenging due to complex natural
environments, diverse tree architectures, and dense canopies. Traditional supervised methods rely on
extensive, manually annotated datasets that are often impractical to obtain. In this study, we propose
a novel self-supervised learning framework that eliminates the need for manual labeling by integrating
transformation-invariant feature extraction, an energy-based segmentation loss, and soft clustering. The
framework operates in two stages: a pretext task applies geometric transformations—rotation (from –45◦to
+45◦), translation (between –1 and 1 units), and scaling (between 0.5 and 2.0)—to learn robust features,
while an unsupervised segmentation step leverages an energy function that combines height, density,
and slope attributes to cluster points corresponding to individual trees. We evaluated our approach on a
high-density LiDAR dataset acquired from the Estonian Land Board (LAS format, version 1.4) comprising
over 850,000 points. Our method achieves up to 87% convexity, 78% solidity, and an elliptical fit error as
low as 0.12, substantially reducing over-segmentation compared to baseline clustering techniques. These
results demonstrate that our self-supervised framework offers a scalable, label-free solution for precise tree
segmentation, with significant advantages in accuracy and efficiency over traditional supervised methods.

II. RELATED WORK
The segmentation of individual trees from LiDAR data
has garnered significant interest in remote sensing, forestry,
and urban planning due to its potential for contributing to
ecological monitoring, forest management, and urban green
space development. This section reviews the state-of-the-
art in tree segmentation techniques from LiDAR data and
identifies research gaps.
A. CURRENT RESEARCH
Existing research on tree segmentation can be broadly
categorized into raster-based and point-based approaches.
Raster-based methods transform the LiDAR point cloud
into a 2D representation, such as digital surface models
(DSMs) or canopy height models (CHMs). These methods
then process the rasterized images to detect tree tops using
various algorithms. Watershed segmentation applies the
watershed algorithm to CHMs to delineate tree crowns but is
prone to over-segmentation, particularly in dense forests [4],
[27]. Region growing utilizes seed points, typically the local
maxima in CHMs, to segment tree crowns by expanding
regions based on height similarity. While effective in
uniform forests, it struggles with heterogeneous canopies [3],
[28], [29]. Machine learning approaches have also been
applied to rasters, such as convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) trained on CHMs for tree segmentation, achieving
high accuracy but requiring extensive labeled datasets and
potentially losing spatial resolution compared to point-based
methods [30], [31].
Point-based segmentation techniques work directly on
3D LiDAR point clouds, preserving geometric information
and enabling more precise segmentation. Clustering-based
approaches such as DBSCAN [32], [33] and k-means
clustering [34], [35] attempt to group points corresponding
to individual trees. However, they often require fine-tuning
parameters like point density thresholds, making them
dataset-dependent. Graph-based segmentation methods con-
struct graphs where points act as nodes, and edges define
spatial relationships. Spectral clustering techniques are then
used to segment trees, but these methods are computationally
expensive [36], [37], [38], [39]. Deep learning approaches
for 3D point clouds, including PointNet [40], [41] and its
variants [42], [43], [44], have demonstrated promising results
in tree segmentation, but they require large amounts of
training data and significant computational resources.
B. RESEARCH GAPS
Despite advancements in tree segmentation, several key
challenges remain unaddressed. Many existing methods rely
on specific tree species, forest types, and LiDAR acquisition
parameters, limiting their generalizability across diverse
ecosystems [45]. Methods such as watershed segmentation
often suffer from over-segmentation in dense forests where
canopy overlap is high [46]. Current techniques also struggle
in mixed and sparse forests, where tree structures and canopy
densities vary significantly. Supervised deep learning models
require extensive annotated datasets, making them expensive
and time-consuming to develop. Computational complexity
remains an issue, as deep learning and graph-based methods
demand high processing power, making real-time deploy-
ment on UAVs and edge devices impractical.

airborne LiDAR point cloud data for segmentation. The
objective is to improve data quality by mitigating sensor
noise, removing outliers, and standardizing the spatial
representation of points. These processes ensure that the
dataset is optimized for self-supervised learning, enabling
robust feature extraction and segmentation of individual trees.

B. SELF-SUPERVISED LEARNING FRAMEWORK
This research introduces a novel self-supervised learning
framework designed for the segmentation of individual
trees from airborne LiDAR point cloud data. It leverages a
dual-task structure, focusing on a pretext task for features
extraction and a main task for accurate tree segmentation.
This innovative approach aims to deeply understand the
geometric and topological nuances of LiDAR data for
enhanced segmentation performance.
1) PRETEXT TASK: GEOMETRIC TRANSFORMATIONS
The geometric transformations pretext task aims to enhance
the network’s ability to learn robust, transformation-invariant
features from the LiDAR point cloud data. This task
involves applying various geometric transformations to the
point cloud data and training the network to predict these
transformations. By focusing on this task, the model can
develop a deeper understanding of the geometric struc-
tures and relationships within the data, which is crucial
for accurate tree segmentation. The transformation values
were carefully selected based on empirical observations
of natural tree structures in aerial LiDAR datasets. These
values ensure that the model learns meaningful geo-
metric variations without introducing artificial distortions.
Specifically:
• Rotation −45◦to +45◦: Trees exhibit variations in
orientation due to environmental factors such as wind,
terrain inclination, and slight sensor tilts. This range
captures realistic tree rotations without excessive defor-
mation. Expanding beyond this range (e.g., ±90◦) could
introduce unrealistic orientations, while reducing it (e.g.,
±20◦) might limit the model’s ability to generalize
across varying forest conditions.

4) MAIN TASK: TREE SEGMENTATION
Leveraging the pretext task’s learned features, and the
geometric transformation step, the segmentation main task
embarks on distinguishing individual trees with high pre-
cision. This task is handled using a novel soft clustering
mechanism that works directly on the transformed features,
optimizing tree segmentation without the need for additional
neural network layers. The clustering process is guided
by custom-designed loss functions that ensure the correct
grouping of points into clusters representing individual trees.
The core of the segmentation process is the soft clustering
mechanism, which assigns points to centroids that represent
potential tree clusters. Unlike hard clustering techniques,
which assign each point to a single cluster, soft clustering
enables points to have varying degrees of membership across
different clusters. This flexibility is crucial for handling
the natural variability in tree shapes and sizes, as well as
managing points that lie near the boundaries between trees.
(1) Soft Clustering Mechanism
Traditional hard clustering techniques, such as k-means
or DBSCAN, assign each point to one cluster, limiting
their ability to handle ambiguous regions where tree crowns
overlap or where there is a gradual transition between tree
structures. To overcome these limitations, we introduce a soft
clustering mechanism, where each point has a probabilistic
membership, guided by energy values calculated previously,
across multiple clusters (trees). This approach allows for
more flexibility and precision, particularly in dense forest
environments where tree boundaries are not always well-
defined. The energy values provide cues related to the
tree tops and dense canopy regions. The number of trees
(clusters) is unknown, so an adaptive clustering mechanism
is employed to dynamically estimate and refine the number
of clusters during the process.

VI. ADVANTAGES AND LIMITATIONS
The proposed self-supervised learning framework for tree
segmentation from airborne LiDAR data introduces signifi-
cant advancements, but it also has certain limitations. This
section discusses both aspects.
A. ADVANTAGES
• Eliminates the Need for Labeled Data: Unlike super-
vised methods, the proposed framework does not rely on
manually annotated training data, reducing the cost and
time associated with data labeling.
• Improved Segmentation Accuracy: The integration
of an energy-based loss function and adaptive soft
clustering enhances segmentation quality, reducing both
over-segmentation and under-segmentation errors.

• Generalizability Across Forest Types: By leveraging
geometry-aware feature extraction, the model adapts
well to varying tree structures, improving segmentation
robustness in mixed and sparse forests.
• Preserves Spatial and Structural Integrity: Unlike
raster-based methods that lose 3D spatial details, the
proposed approach maintains point cloud geometry,
leading to more precise individual tree segmentation.
• Scalability for Large-Scale Forest Monitoring: The
framework is designed to process large LiDAR datasets
efficiently, making it suitable for applications such
as large-scale environmental monitoring and forest
management.
B. LIMITATIONS
While our framework has several advantages, it has some
limitations that we will address in future work:
• High Computational Cost: The combination of self-
supervised learning and soft clustering requires signifi-
cant computational power. However, this is concerning
only in training the model, and in the inference phase the
model can be used in reasonable settings.
• Limited Testing on Diverse LiDAR Data: Although
the framework generalizes well across different forest
structures, additional validation on datasets with varying
LiDAR acquisition parameters (e.g., pulse density, scan
angle) is needed to ensure robustness.
• No Explicit Tree Species Classification: While the
framework effectively segments individual trees, it does
not perform species classification.
• Sensitivity to Hyperparameter Selection: The frame-
work relies on several hyperparameters, such as those
used in the energy function and clustering process,
which require fine-tuning to achieve optimal results
across different datasets.
• Potential Challenges in Highly Dense Canopies:
While the model reduces over-segmentation, distin-
guishing closely packed trees in highly dense forests
remains a challenge due to overlapping canopies and
occlusions.
Despite these limitations, the proposed framework offers
a significant step forward in tree segmentation research,
providing an annotation-free, scalable, and robust solution for
processing airborne LiDAR data.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a self-supervised learning framework
for segmenting individual trees from airborne LiDAR point
clouds, addressing the challenges of labeled data scarcity,
dense canopies, and diverse tree structures. The frame-
work integrates transformation-invariant feature learning,
geometry-aware refinement (height, density, slope), and
adaptive soft clustering for accurate segmentation across
various forest types.
The proposed method achieved high segmentation accu-
racy with convexity values up to 0.83, solidity of 0.78, and
an elliptical fit error as low as 0.30 in dense forests. It also
demonstrated reliable performance in mixed and scattered
forests, highlighting its adaptability and robustness.
Future work will focus on optimizing computational
efficiency and expanding dataset variability for broader
applicability.
VIII. FUTURE WORK
Despite the promising results of the proposed framework,
there are several directions for future research that could
further enhance its performance, versatility, and usability.
• Expanding Dataset Variability: While the current
model performs well on the dataset used in this
study, further evaluation is needed on datasets with
varying LiDAR acquisition parameters, such as pulse
density, scan angle, and sensor type. These variations
can significantly affect point cloud quality, requiring
fine-tuning of the framework for optimal performance
across diverse environments.
• Multimodal Data Integration: Incorporating addi-
tional remote sensing data, such as hyperspectral and
multispectral imagery, alongside LiDAR data, could
enhance segmentation accuracy. This would be partic-
ularly beneficial in dense forest areas with substantial
canopy cover. Multimodal approaches may also improve
model generalization across different forest types and
ecological conditions.
• Real-Time Segmentation: With the increasing volume
of LiDAR data, optimizing the model for real-time
segmentation on edge devices or UAVs is crucial.
Enhancing computational efficiency while maintaining
segmentation accuracy would enable real-time appli-
cations in forest monitoring, urban planning, and
ecological studies.
• Post-Segmentation Analysis: Further research could
explore advanced techniques for analyzing segmented
tree clusters. These include tree species classification
and forest biomass estimation, which would increase the
practical utility of segmentation outcomes in ecological
monitoring and forest management.
