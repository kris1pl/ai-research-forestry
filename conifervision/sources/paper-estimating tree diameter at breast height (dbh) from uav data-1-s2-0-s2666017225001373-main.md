# Estimating tree diameter at breast height (DBH) from UAV data: A comparison of oblique–Vertical imagery fusion and allometric modeling

## Summary

This paper investigates individual tree diameter at breast height (DBH) estimation for Eldar pine (*Pinus eldarica*) in an open stand using UAV digital aerial photogrammetry (UAV-DAP). The authors compare two main paradigms:
1. **Direct geometric fitting** from SfM point clouds generated by combining vertical (90°) and oblique (30°, 60°) images. Three multi-angle fusion configurations were tested:
   - **S1:** 90° + 30°
   - **S2:** 90° + 60°
   - **S3:** 90° + 30° + 60°
   Cross-sectional point slices at breast height (1.2–1.4 m above ground) were fitted with a 2D least-squares circle.
2. **Allometric modeling (S4):** A Random Forest (RF) regression model predicting DBH using 5 structural metrics derived strictly from nadir (90°) UAV imagery (tree height, crown area, perpendicular crown diameters CD1/CD2, and 3D alpha-shape crown volume).

The full three-angle fusion configuration (**S3**) achieved the highest accuracy ($R^2 = 0.98$, $\text{RMSE} = 2.46\text{ cm}$, $\text{rRMSE} = 6.7\%$, $\text{bias} = 0.21\text{ cm}$), closely followed by **S2** ($R^2 = 0.86$, $\text{RMSE} = 4.68\text{ cm}$, $\text{bias} = -0.59\text{ cm}$). Vertical-only Random Forest allometry (**S4**) yielded moderate accuracy ($R^2 = 0.82$, $\text{RMSE} = 8.79\text{ cm}$, $\text{rRMSE} = 23.9\%$), exhibiting noticeable underestimation on small DBH trees. Shallow oblique fusion (**S1**) severely degraded performance ($R^2 = 0.79$, $\text{RMSE} = 18.30\text{ cm}$, $\text{bias} = +11.40\text{ cm}$) and suffered 9.6% detection failure due to sparse point density near stem bases.

## Key claims

- **Viewing angle diversity dictates stem reconstruction fidelity:** Incorporating wider oblique angles (60°) dramatically enhances stem point density and coverage at breast height compared to nadir alone or steep/shallow oblique (30°) alone. Point cloud density reached 1.27 points/$\text{cm}^2$ for S3 vs 1.0 points/$\text{cm}^2$ for S1 and 0.6 points/$\text{cm}^2$ for nadir alone.
- **Direct geometric circle fitting outperforms allometric regression when stem visibility permits:** S3 ($R^2 = 0.98$, $\text{RMSE} = 2.46\text{ cm}$) and S2 significantly outperformed vertical-only RF allometry S4 ($R^2 = 0.82$, $\text{RMSE} = 8.79\text{ cm}$).
- **Least-squares vs RANSAC in low-density SfM slices:** While RANSAC is standard in TLS/LiDAR cylinder fitting, point densities in the 1.2–1.4 m slice of UAV-DAP were too low for RANSAC to consistently reach consensus, necessitating ordinary least-squares circle fitting over all in-slice points.
- **Relative feature importance in vertical allometry:** For the RF model (S4), tree height was the most important predictor (62.06% IncMSE), followed by 3D alpha-shape crown volume (30.38%), crown area (21.54%), and crown diameters (14–17%).
- **Detection failure on smaller stems:** In S1, 9.6% of stems (31 trees) could not be fitted due to missing stem returns; S2 missed 3.4% (11 trees). S3 and S4 successfully predicted all 324 trees.

## Implications for our pipeline

- **DBH and allometry downstream of detection:** The current production pipeline ([[project/pipeline-overview]]) operates on 2D rasters (CHM + orthophotos) to detect tree instances and assign DINOv3 species. If individual DBH or stem volume is added downstream ([[concepts/tree-allometry]]), nadir-derived features (height from CHM, crown area/diameter from bounding polygons, and alpha-shape volume from photogrammetric LAZ) can achieve moderate allometric prediction ($R^2 \approx 0.82$) without changing flight acquisition protocols.
- **Flight acquisition limitations:** Direct geometric stem extraction requires oblique imagery (ideally 60° tilt) and open canopies. In operational forestry surveys with nadir-only flights, direct stem circle fitting is impossible due to canopy occlusion at breast height.
- **Crown volume feature extraction:** For allometric modeling from nadir data, computing 3D alpha shapes ($\alpha \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10\}$) from height-normalized LAZ points above height-to-crown-base (HCB) adds significant explanatory power beyond simple 2D crown area.
- **Forest density boundary condition:** The study site had an exceptionally low stand density (46.3 trees/ha, roughly 15 m spacing). In dense stands before thinning ([[concepts/dense-stand-detection]]), oblique lines of sight to stems are occluded by neighbouring crowns, meaning direct geometric circle fitting from UAV-DAP will break down, making allometry ([[concepts/tree-allometry]]) or LiDAR the only viable paths.

## Replication pseudocode

### Prerequisites

- UAV flight dataset including nadir (90°) and oblique (30°, 60°) overlapping RGB imagery with survey-grade GCPs (<1 cm precision).
- Structure from Motion / Multi-View Stereo software supporting joint bundle adjustment (e.g. Pix4Dmapper or OpenDroneMap).
- Digital Terrain Model (DTM) and Digital Surface Model (DSM) generation, or point cloud ground classification tools.
- Individual tree crown segments (e.g., from marker-controlled watershed on CHM or instance segmentation masks).

### Procedure

```text
# High-level steps for multi-angle DBH extraction (S3) vs RF Allometry (S4)

1. Photogrammetric Reconstruction:
   a. Process nadir (90°), oblique 30°, and oblique 60° image sets with SfM.
   b. Co-register multi-angle point clouds via shared tie points and GCPs.
   c. Run joint bundle adjustment across all 3 viewing angles.
   d. Perform dense MVS reconstruction to generate composite dense cloud (S3).
   e. Apply median filter to remove high-frequency noise.

2. Point Cloud Normalization & CHM:
   a. Classify ground points using elevation and neighborhood topology (or CSF filter).
   b. Generate DTM and DSM via IDW interpolation at 2 cm GSD.
   c. Compute CHM = DSM - DTM.
   d. Subtract DTM ground elevations from point cloud Z coordinates to get height above ground.

3. Individual Tree Delineation:
   a. Detect canopy local maxima on CHM as tree tops.
   b. Segment individual crowns using marker-controlled watershed segmentation.
   c. Clip normalized point cloud into individual tree point clusters using crown polygons.

4. Approach I: Direct Geometric Stem Fitting (S1, S2, S3):
   for each tree point cluster:
       a. Extract vertical point slice where 1.2 m <= Z <= 1.4 m.
       b. if point_count < TBD:
              mark stem as undetected / insufficient density.
              continue
       c. Project slice points onto horizontal 2D plane (X, Y).
       d. Fit circle parameters (cx, cy, r) using ordinary least-squares regression:
              minimize sum((x_i - cx)^2 + (y_i - cy)^2 - r^2)^2
       e. DBH_est = 2 * r * 100  # radius in meters to diameter in cm

5. Approach II: Vertical RF Allometric Modeling (S4):
   for each tree segmented from nadir data:
       a. height = max(CHM within crown polygon)
       b. area = polygon_area(crown polygon)
       c. CD1, CD2 = major_axis_length, minor_axis_length(crown polygon)
       d. Isolate points above crown base height (HCB).
       e. Compute crown volume via 3D alpha shape, selecting first valid alpha in [0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10].
   Train Random Forest Regressor on ground-truth DBH:
       features = [height, volume, area, CD1, CD2]
       tune hyperparameters: mtry in [1..20], ntree in [100..2500], nodesize in [1..20]
       predict DBH for target trees.
```

### Gaps / not specified in paper

- **Ground classification algorithm details:** The paper notes a "custom algorithm implemented in MATLAB R2024b based on elevation and neighborhood topology" was used rather than ALS normalization, but does not provide specific parameter thresholds (cloth resolution, iteration tolerances, or search radii).
- **Minimum point threshold for circle fitting:** When least-squares was used instead of RANSAC, the exact minimum point count floor required to declare a detection failure was not explicitly quantified (labeled "too low for RANSAC").
- **Alpha shape selection criteria:** The paper states it tests $\alpha \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10\}$ and selects the "first that produces a valid volume", but the exact geometric sanity check definition for "valid volume" is unspecified.
- **Tree segmentation in dense canopies:** Delineation used simple marker-controlled watershed on CHM, which performs well in this open 46.3 trees/ha plantation but oversegments or undersegments heavily in dense unthinned stands.

## Quotes

- **On stem visibility and oblique fusion necessity:**
  > "Most UAV surveys rely on vertical (nadir) imagery, which is effective for capturing canopy features and generating canopy height models (CHMs). However, vertical imagery provides limited visibility of the lower stem and forest floor, resulting in sparse or incomplete point cloud data derived from UAV-DAP at breast height..." (p. 2)
- **On RANSAC failure in DAP point clouds:**
  > "However, it was found that for most trees, especially in the S1 dataset, the number of points within the 1.2–1.4 m height band was too low for RANSAC to perform effectively due to insufficient data density and occlusions, which limited the algorithm's ability to identify a robust circular consensus. Therefore, to maintain consistency and reliability across all datasets and trees, a direct least-squares circle fitting method within the 1.2–1.4 m height band without random sampling was used exclusively." (p. 4)
- **On viewing angle results:**
  > "Results demonstrate that the geometric approach using all three viewing angles (S3) achieved the highest accuracy ($R^2 = 0.985$, $\text{RMSE} = 2.47\text{ cm}$), followed closely by S2 ($R^2 = 0.949$), indicating the effectiveness of multi-angle image integration in improving DBH prediction. S4, while less accurate ($R^2 = 0.824$), provided moderately reliable estimates, offering a simpler and more scalable alternative in operational settings." (p. 1)
- **On canopy openness constraints:**
  > "As mentioned by Tomaštík et al. (2017), canopy openness is a prerequisite for effective terrain and stem modeling in photogrammetry, a condition met in our case but which may limit results in more closed-canopy settings." (p. 9)
